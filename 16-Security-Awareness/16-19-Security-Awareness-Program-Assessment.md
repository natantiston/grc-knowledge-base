## Introduction

A security awareness program should not be considered effective simply because employees complete their required training.

An organization may have:

* 100% training completion.
* A modern Learning Management System.
* Annual phishing simulations.
* Security awareness policies.
* Awareness campaigns.

Yet employees may still:

* Click phishing links.
* Share sensitive information.
* Ignore suspicious activity.
* Bypass security procedures.
* Fail to report incidents.

Therefore, organizations need a structured way to determine whether the **entire security awareness program is actually reducing human-related security risk**.

This is the purpose of an **Enterprise Security Awareness Program Review**.

> **An enterprise program review evaluates the governance, people, processes, technology, metrics, content, and outcomes of the security awareness program as a whole.**

---

# What Is an Enterprise Program Review?

An enterprise program review is a structured assessment of the organization's security awareness program across its entire scope.

It examines questions such as:

* Is the program properly governed?
* Are responsibilities clearly assigned?
* Are training requirements defined?
* Are employees receiving appropriate training?
* Is training role-based?
* Are third parties included?
* Are phishing simulations being conducted?
* Are results measured?
* Is behavior improving?
* Are management and executives receiving useful reports?
* Are regulatory requirements addressed?
* Is the program continuously improving?

The review looks beyond individual training courses.

---

# Why an Enterprise Review Is Necessary

Security awareness is a program, not a single activity.

Consider an organization where:

> 98% of employees completed annual security training.

That sounds positive.

But suppose:

* Phishing click rate remains 18%.
* Reporting rate is only 25%.
* Finance employees repeatedly fail BEC simulations.
* Contractors receive no awareness training.
* Executives are excluded from training.
* Security metrics are not reported to management.

The program may have high **completion**, but low **effectiveness**.

This demonstrates the difference between:

> **Training activity**

and

> **Security outcomes**.

---

# Program Review vs. Training Assessment

These are different.

### Training Assessment

Examines whether a particular course was effective.

For example:

> Did employees understand the phishing module?

### Program Review

Examines whether the entire awareness program is effective.

For example:

> Is the organization reducing human-related cybersecurity risk?

A program review is therefore broader and more strategic.

# Risk Alignment

The awareness program should be based on organizational risk.

For example:

If the organization identifies:

> **Business Email Compromise**

as a major risk, the awareness program should include:

* Executive impersonation training.
* Finance awareness.
* Payment verification.
* Phishing simulations.
* BEC scenarios.
* Reporting procedures.

The program should not simply deliver the same generic training to everyone.

# Enterprise Risk Assessment

The review should examine whether human-related risks are identified in the organization's risk-management process.

Examples include:

* Phishing susceptibility.
* Social engineering.
* Insider risk.
* Data handling errors.
* Weak authentication behavior.
* Unauthorized use of AI.
* Remote-working risks.
* Physical security behavior.

This creates a connection between cybersecurity awareness and enterprise risk management.

# New-Hire Training

The review should determine:

* Is security training part of onboarding?
* How quickly must it be completed?
* What happens if it is not completed?
* Is access affected?
* Are high-risk roles given additional training?

For example:

> New employees must complete security awareness training within 30 days.

A more mature approach may require critical security training **before access to sensitive systems is granted**.

* Completion rates.
* Training content.
* Relevance.
* Frequency.
* Assessment results.
* Overdue training.
* Exemptions.

However:

> **Annual training should not be the entire security awareness program.**

* Current threats.
* Organizational policies.
* Security technologies.

* Are scenarios realistic?

# Security Culture Review

Questions may include:

* Do employees report incidents?
* Are employees comfortable reporting mistakes?
* Do managers reinforce security?
* Do employees challenge suspicious requests?
* Is security included in business decisions?
* Is security treated as everyone's responsibility?

> "Do you understand how to protect sensitive information?"

> "Do you believe management takes cybersecurity seriously?"

Survey results can identify cultural and knowledge gaps.

# Program Maturity Review

The enterprise review should determine the program's maturity.

A simple model might be:

### Level 1 – Ad Hoc

Training is inconsistent and reactive.

### Level 2 – Developing

Basic annual training exists.

### Level 3 – Defined

A structured program with policies, roles, and metrics exists.

### Level 4 – Managed

The organization measures behavior and continuously improves the program.

### Level 5 – Optimized

Security awareness is integrated with human-risk management, enterprise risk, threat intelligence, and organizational culture.

# Evidence Triangulation

A mature assessment should compare multiple sources.

For example:

**Policy says:**

Employees must report phishing.

**Training says:**

Employees know how to report phishing.

**Simulation says:**

Only 30% report simulated phishing.

**Interviews say:**

Employees are unsure where to report.

This reveals a clear program weakness.

This approach is sometimes described as **triangulating evidence**.

1. **An enterprise program review evaluates the entire security awareness program, not just training completion.**
2. **The review should examine governance, people, processes, technology, metrics, and outcomes.**
3. **Training activity and security effectiveness are not the same thing.**
4. **The program should be aligned with organizational risks.**
5. **Roles and responsibilities should be clearly defined.**
6. **Policies should be current, approved, communicated, and accessible.**
7. **All relevant employee populations should be considered.**
8. **Third parties, contractors, executives, and high-risk roles should not automatically be excluded.**
9. **Role-based training should address different human risks.**
10. **Phishing simulations provide valuable behavioral evidence.**
11. **Security culture should be assessed alongside training activity.**
12. **Technology should support measurable program outcomes.**
13. **Metrics should include behavioral and outcome indicators, not only completion rates.**
14. **Executive reporting should communicate risk and trends rather than merely activity.**
15. **Compliance requirements should be incorporated into the program review.**
16. **Stakeholder and employee interviews can reveal weaknesses hidden by documentation.**
17. **Evidence should be triangulated across policies, training, simulations, metrics, and actual behavior.**

**Scope → Assess → Measure → Interview → Analyze → Identify Gaps → Assess Risk → Improve**

The most important principle is:

> **A mature security awareness program is not measured by how much training the organization delivers, but by how effectively it changes behavior and reduces human-related security risk.**

## Gap Analysis

A **gap analysis** is a structured comparison between an organization's **current security awareness program** and its **desired or required state**.

In a security awareness program assessment, gap analysis answers a fundamental question:

> **"What should our security awareness program be doing, and where are we falling short?"**

The objective is not simply to identify problems. A good gap analysis determines:

* What is missing.
* What is inadequate.
* Why the gap exists.
* What risk the gap creates.
* How the gap should be addressed.
* Who should own the remediation.
* When the improvement should be completed.

---

# Current State vs. Target State

The basic concept is straightforward:

**Current State**

↓

**Gap**

↓

**Target State**

For example:

### Current State

Employees receive annual security awareness training.

### Target State

Employees receive:

* Annual foundational training.
* Role-based training.
* Targeted phishing exercises.
* Incident-reporting guidance.
* Continuous security reinforcement.

### Gap

The organization lacks role-based and continuous awareness activities.

### Risk

Employees in high-risk roles may not recognize threats relevant to their responsibilities.

### Improvement

Develop a risk-based role-specific training program.

---

# Why Gap Analysis Matters

Without gap analysis, organizations may simply add more training.

For example:

> "Phishing click rates are high, so let's add another training course."

That may not solve the underlying problem.

The actual cause could be:

* Poor email filtering.
* Confusing reporting procedures.
* Weak MFA.
* Excessive workload.
* Unrealistic policies.
* Lack of management support.
* Poor training content.

A gap analysis helps identify the **root cause** rather than assuming every problem is a training problem.

---

# Gap Analysis in the Security Awareness Lifecycle

A mature program can follow:

**Assess Current State**

↓

**Define Target State**

↓

**Identify Gaps**

↓

**Assess Risk**

↓

**Prioritize**

↓

**Remediate**

↓

**Measure Improvement**

↓

**Reassess**

Gap analysis is therefore part of continuous improvement.

---

# Sources for the Target State

The target state should not be invented arbitrarily.

It can be based on:

* Organizational policies.
* Enterprise risk assessments.
* Security objectives.
* Regulatory requirements.
* Contractual requirements.
* ISO/IEC 27001.
* NIST frameworks.
* Industry practices.
* Internal standards.
* Audit requirements.
* Business objectives.

The target state should reflect what the organization actually needs.

---

# Compliance-Based Gap Analysis

An organization may compare its current program against applicable requirements.

For example:

| Requirement               | Current State   | Gap             |
| ------------------------- | --------------- | --------------- |
| Security awareness policy | Exists          | No gap          |
| Employee training         | Annual          | Partial         |
| Role-based training       | Limited         | Significant gap |
| Third-party awareness     | Inconsistent    | Significant gap |
| Training effectiveness    | Completion only | Significant gap |

This allows GRC teams to identify compliance-related weaknesses.

# Risk-Based Gap Analysis

Compliance alone is not sufficient.

Suppose an organization technically meets its annual awareness requirement.

However:

> 25% of Finance employees repeatedly fail BEC simulations.

There may be no obvious compliance violation, but there is a significant **human-risk gap**.

Risk-based analysis therefore asks:

> **"Where could weaknesses cause the greatest business impact?"**

# Gap Categories

Gaps can be classified to make remediation easier.

### Policy Gap

A required policy does not exist.

### Process Gap

A process exists but is incomplete or ineffective.

### People Gap

Employees lack required knowledge or skills.

### Technology Gap

Required technical capabilities are missing.

### Governance Gap

Ownership or accountability is unclear.

### Measurement Gap

The organization cannot demonstrate effectiveness.

### Compliance Gap

A regulatory or contractual requirement is not adequately addressed.

---

# Example: Reporting Gap

Consider the following situation.

### Current State

Employees are instructed to report phishing.

### Observation

Only 35% know how to report suspicious emails.

### Gap

Awareness of the reporting process is inadequate.

### Root Cause

The reporting process is poorly communicated and differs between business units.

### Risk

Potential incidents may not be reported quickly.

### Recommendation

Standardize the reporting mechanism and reinforce it through simulations and microlearning.

This is more useful than simply saying:

> "Employees need more training."

---

# Root Cause Analysis

A good gap analysis should investigate **why** the gap exists.

For example:

### Problem

Employees do not report suspicious emails.

Possible causes:

**Cause 1**

They don't know how.

**Cause 2**

The reporting button is difficult to find.

**Cause 3**

They believe reporting is only for serious incidents.

**Cause 4**

Employees fear being blamed.

**Cause 5**

The security team does not provide feedback.

Each cause requires a different solution.

---

# The Five Whys

The **Five Whys** technique can help identify root causes.

### Problem

Employees are not reporting phishing.

**Why?**

They do not know where to report it.

**Why?**

The reporting process is not clearly communicated.

**Why?**

Different business units use different procedures.

**Why?**

No global reporting standard exists.

**Why?**

Program governance was decentralized without a common control framework.

The original problem appeared to be a training issue.

The root cause is actually a **governance and process issue**.

---

# Gap Statement

A useful gap statement should be specific.

Weak:

> "Security awareness needs improvement."

Better:

> "Third-party contractors in three business units do not receive standardized security awareness training before receiving access to corporate systems."

This identifies:

* Who.
* What.
* Where.
* What is missing.

# Gap Severity

Organizations can classify gaps according to severity.

### Critical

Immediate action required due to significant risk.

### High

Significant weakness requiring prioritized remediation.

### Medium

Important weakness that should be addressed.

### Low

Minor improvement opportunity.

The exact classification methodology should be defined by the organization's risk-management framework.

---

# Risk-Based Prioritization

Not every gap should receive the same priority.

Consider:

> A minor content formatting issue

versus:

> Finance employees repeatedly approving simulated fraudulent payment requests.

The second clearly represents greater risk.

Prioritization should consider:

* Likelihood.
* Impact.
* Exposure.
* Threat activity.
* Regulatory consequences.
* Business criticality.
* Existing controls.

---

# Risk Scoring

A simple methodology might calculate:

**Risk Score = Likelihood × Impact**

For example:

| Gap                        | Likelihood | Impact | Score |
| -------------------------- | ---------: | -----: | ----: |
| Outdated awareness posters |          1 |      1 |     1 |
| Poor phishing reporting    |          3 |      4 |    12 |
| Finance BEC weakness       |          4 |      5 |    20 |
| Contractor awareness gap   |          4 |      4 |    16 |

The highest-risk gaps should generally receive priority.

Organizations may use more sophisticated risk models depending on their GRC framework.

---

# Control Gap vs. Awareness Gap

This distinction is important.

Suppose employees repeatedly click phishing emails.

It may appear to be an awareness problem.

But investigation might reveal:

* Email filtering is weak.
* MFA is not enabled.
* Browser protection is inadequate.
* Employees are overloaded.
* Reporting is difficult.

Therefore:

> **A human-risk problem does not necessarily have a training solution.**

The correct remediation may involve multiple controls.

---

# Example: Business Email Compromise

### Observation

Finance employees frequently fail BEC simulations.

### Possible gaps

**Awareness Gap**

Employees don't recognize BEC indicators.

**Process Gap**

Payment verification procedures are unclear.

**Technology Gap**

Email security does not detect impersonation effectively.

**Control Gap**

No independent verification is required for bank-account changes.

A mature gap analysis identifies all four.

For example:

↓

↓

**Gap**

↓

**Remediation**

Update training and conduct role-specific exercises.

This makes the analysis useful for GRC and audit activities.

# ISO/IEC 27001 Perspective

For an ISO/IEC 27001-based organization, the gap analysis can examine whether security awareness activities support the organization's ISMS.

The organization should consider:

* Competence.
* Awareness.
* Information security responsibilities.
* Relevant policies.
* Risk treatment.
* Control implementation.
* Evidence of effectiveness.

The important point is that awareness should be connected to the organization's broader information security management system rather than operating independently.

---

# NIST Perspective

The NIST Cybersecurity Framework can also provide a useful structure.

For example, the organization can consider human-related activities across:

* **Govern**
* **Identify**
* **Protect**
* **Detect**
* **Respond**
* **Recover**

Security awareness is particularly relevant to protection, but human behavior can influence every function.

For example:

**Protect**

Employees receive security training.

**Detect**

Employees report suspicious activity.

**Respond**

Employees follow incident-response instructions.

**Recover**

Employees follow recovery and continuity procedures.

---

# Regulatory Gap Analysis

The organization may also assess requirements related to:

* Privacy.
* Cybersecurity.
* Employee awareness.
* Incident reporting.
* Third-party risk.
* Data protection.

For multinational organizations, requirements may differ by jurisdiction.

The gap analysis should therefore identify:

* Which requirement applies.
* Which population is affected.
* Current compliance status.
* Evidence available.
* Remediation required.

---

# Third-Party Gap Analysis

Third parties should be evaluated separately where appropriate.

For example:

### Current State

Employees receive annual training.

### Target State

Employees and relevant contractors must complete security awareness training before accessing corporate systems.

### Gap

Contractors are not consistently included.

### Risk

External personnel may introduce human-related security risks.

### Remediation

Integrate third-party onboarding with security awareness requirements.

---

# Role-Based Gap Analysis

A program may appear effective at the enterprise level but still contain significant gaps in individual populations.

Example:

| Population        | Phishing Failure | Assessment |
| ----------------- | ---------------: | ---------- |
| General Employees |               5% | Acceptable |
| IT                |               3% | Low        |
| HR                |               7% | Moderate   |
| Finance           |              18% | High       |
| Executives        |              14% | High       |

The enterprise average may hide the Finance and Executive risks.

Therefore:

> **Always analyze high-risk populations separately.**

---

# Geographic Gap Analysis

Global organizations may discover regional differences.

For example:

* Europe has strong privacy awareness.
* Asia-Pacific has inconsistent third-party training.
* North America has stronger phishing reporting.
* Latin America has lower training completion.

The organization should determine whether differences are caused by:

* Local requirements.
* Language.
* Culture.
* Technology.
* Governance.
* Resource limitations.

---

# Content Gap Analysis

Training content should be compared against actual organizational risks.

For example:

### Existing Training

* Passwords.
* Phishing.
* Malware.
* Physical security.

### Emerging Risks

* Generative AI.
* Deepfakes.
* Business Email Compromise.
* Cloud collaboration.
* Data leakage through AI tools.

The gap analysis may determine that the awareness curriculum needs to evolve.

---

# Frequency Gap

Training frequency should also be evaluated.

For example:

### Current

Annual training only.

### Risk

Employees may forget key practices between annual sessions.

### Target

Annual foundational training plus:

* Quarterly microlearning.
* Targeted simulations.
* Incident-driven awareness.
* Security campaigns.

The target frequency should be based on risk rather than simply increasing training for its own sake.

---

# Measurement Gap

One of the most common weaknesses is poor measurement.

### Current

> 98% training completion.

### Missing

* Behavioral metrics.
* Phishing reporting.
* Repeat failure.
* Human-related incidents.
* Risk reduction.

The gap is therefore:

> **The organization measures participation but not effectiveness.**

The remediation should establish meaningful behavioral and outcome metrics.

---

# Data Quality Gaps

Metrics are only useful when the underlying data is reliable.

Potential problems include:

* Incomplete employee records.
* Duplicate accounts.
* Incorrect organizational mappings.
* Missing contractor data.
* Inconsistent reporting.
* Different definitions of "completion."

The assessment should therefore examine the quality of the data used to measure the program.

---

# Documentation Gaps

An organization may perform awareness activities but fail to document them adequately.

For example:

> Security training was conducted, but there are no attendance records.

This creates problems for:

* Audit.
* Compliance.
* Management reporting.
* Program evaluation.

Documentation is therefore an important part of the target state.

---

# Governance Gap

A common governance problem is:

> Everyone is responsible for security awareness, but nobody owns the program.

A target state might define:

* Program owner.
* Executive sponsor.
* Supporting functions.
* Business-unit responsibilities.
* Reporting structure.
* Decision authority.

Clear ownership improves accountability.

---

# Resource Gap

Sometimes the program has clear requirements but insufficient resources.

For example:

> The organization wants quarterly role-based training but has one awareness specialist supporting 50,000 employees.

The gap is not simply a process problem.

It may require:

* Additional staff.
* Technology.
* Outsourcing.
* Security champions.
* Budget.

---

# Technology Gap

A program may also have technology limitations.

For example:

### Current

Manual training assignment.

### Problem

Employees who change roles do not automatically receive appropriate training.

### Target

Automated role-based assignment through LMS and HR integration.

The gap is therefore a technology and process issue.

---

# Prioritization Matrix

A practical method is to categorize findings using:

**Impact**

and

**Effort**

|             | Low Effort         | High Effort        |
| ----------- | ------------------ | ------------------ |
| High Impact | Quick Wins         | Strategic Projects |
| Low Impact  | Minor Improvements | Defer              |

For example:

### High Impact / Low Effort

Add a phishing reporting button.

### High Impact / High Effort

Implement enterprise human-risk analytics.

### Low Impact / Low Effort

Update awareness posters.

### Low Impact / High Effort

Replace an entire LMS solely for minor reporting improvements.

This helps management allocate resources effectively.

---

# Gap Remediation Plan

Each significant gap should have:

* Gap ID.
* Description.
* Risk.
* Root cause.
* Recommendation.
* Owner.
* Priority.
* Target date.
* Status.
* Success criteria.

Example:

| Field            | Example                             |
| ---------------- | ----------------------------------- |
| Gap ID           | SA-007                              |
| Gap              | Finance lacks targeted BEC training |
| Risk             | High                                |
| Owner            | Security Awareness Manager          |
| Priority         | High                                |
| Target           | Q2                                  |
| Success Criteria | BEC reporting rate >80%             |

---

# Defining Success Criteria

A recommendation should be measurable.

Weak:

> "Improve phishing awareness."

Better:

> "Reduce repeat phishing simulation failures among Finance employees from 18% to below 8% within two quarters."

The second provides a measurable objective.

---

# Quick Wins

Some gaps can be addressed quickly.

Examples:

* Update outdated training content.
* Publish reporting instructions.
* Add phishing-reporting guidance.
* Update contact information.
* Add security awareness to onboarding.
* Create executive awareness material.

Quick wins can demonstrate progress while larger initiatives are being developed.

---

# Strategic Improvements

Other gaps require longer-term initiatives.

Examples:

* Implement Human Risk Management.
* Deploy enterprise awareness technology.
* Integrate LMS with HR.
* Establish security champion networks.
* Develop global role-based training.
* Integrate awareness metrics with enterprise GRC.

These should be included in the organization's strategic roadmap.

---

# Tracking Remediation

Gap analysis is not complete when the report is issued.

The organization should track:

**Finding**

↓

**Action**

↓

**Owner**

↓

**Due Date**

↓

**Implementation**

↓

**Validation**

↓

**Closure**

A finding should only be considered closed when the organization can demonstrate that the underlying gap has been adequately addressed.

---

# Validation

Suppose the organization identifies:

> Finance employees have a high BEC failure rate.

The organization introduces:

* BEC training.
* Payment verification procedures.
* Targeted simulations.

After six months:

> BEC failure rate decreases from 18% to 6%.

This provides evidence that the remediation was effective.

If the rate remains at 18%, the organization should reassess the underlying problem.

---

Threats change.

Technology changes.

Employees change.

Regulations change.

Business operations change.

Therefore, gap analysis should not happen only once.

A mature cycle is:

**Assess**

↓

**Identify Gaps**

↓

**Remediate**

↓

**Measure**

↓

**Reassess**

↓

**Identify New Gaps**

This creates continuous improvement.

# GRC Perspective

Gap analysis is particularly valuable for GRC professionals because it connects:

**Requirement**

↓

**Control**

↓

**Current State**

↓

**Gap**

↓

**Risk**

↓

**Treatment**

↓

**Evidence**

This is essentially the same logic used throughout governance, risk, and compliance programs.

For example:

> **Requirement:** Employees must understand security responsibilities.

↓

> **Control:** Security awareness training.

↓

> **Current State:** Annual training exists.

↓

> **Gap:** High-risk roles receive no specialized training.

↓

> **Risk:** Increased likelihood of successful social engineering.

↓

> **Treatment:** Implement role-based awareness.

↓

> **Evidence:** Training records and behavioral metrics.

This makes the security awareness program measurable and auditable.

---

# Key Takeaways

1. **Gap analysis compares the current security awareness program with the desired state.**
2. **The target state should be based on risk, business requirements, standards, and regulations.**
3. **A gap is not automatically a training problem.**
4. **Root-cause analysis helps determine why a gap exists.**
5. **Gaps can involve governance, people, processes, technology, measurement, or compliance.**
6. **Risk should drive remediation priorities.**
7. **High-risk populations should be analyzed separately.**
8. **Third parties should be included where they create organizational risk.**
9. **Behavioral data should be compared with training and policy requirements.**
10. **Completion rates alone cannot demonstrate program effectiveness.**
11. **Specific gap statements are more useful than generic observations.**
12. **Each significant gap should have an owner, priority, target date, and success criteria.**
13. **Quick wins can provide immediate improvements.**
14. **Strategic gaps may require technology, people, or governance changes.**
15. **Remediation should be validated after implementation.**
16. **Findings can be mapped to enterprise risks and security controls.**
17. **Gap analysis can support ISO/IEC 27001, NIST, regulatory, and audit activities.**
18. **Employee and stakeholder interviews can reveal gaps that documentation does not show.**
19. **Gap analysis should be repeated as risks, technology, and business requirements change.**
20. **The ultimate purpose is to turn identified weaknesses into measurable risk-reduction actions.**

The fundamental model is:

**Requirement → Current State → Gap → Root Cause → Risk → Remediation → Validation**

A strong GRC professional should therefore avoid simply saying:

> **"There is a security awareness gap."**

Instead, the assessment should be able to explain:

> **"What requirement are we trying to meet, what is happening today, why does the gap exist, what risk does it create, what should we change, who owns the change, and how will we prove that the change worked?"**

That is what turns a security awareness gap analysis into a meaningful GRC activity.

## Performance Assessment

A **security awareness performance assessment** evaluates how effectively the organization's awareness program is achieving its intended outcomes.

While a gap analysis identifies **what is missing or inadequate**, a performance assessment asks:

> **"How well is the security awareness program performing today?"**

This distinction is important.

An organization may have:

* A documented awareness policy.
* A formal training program.
* Phishing simulations.
* Security awareness campaigns.
* An LMS.
* Management reporting.

But these activities do not automatically demonstrate that the program is effective.

A performance assessment examines whether the program is producing measurable improvements in:

* Knowledge.
* Behavior.
* Security culture.
* Risk reduction.
* Incident prevention.
* Incident reporting.
* Compliance.

---

# Performance Assessment vs. Gap Analysis

These activities are closely related but different.

| Gap Analysis                      | Performance Assessment                     |
| --------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| Identifies deficiencies           | Measures effectiveness                     |
| Current vs. target state          | Actual performance over time               |
| Focuses on weaknesses             | Focuses on results                         |
| Identifies what needs improvement | Determines how well activities are working |
| Often point-in-time               | Often trend-based                          |

For example:

> **Gap:** Finance lacks role-based BEC training.

Performance assessment asks:

> **"After implementing BEC training, did Finance employees actually improve their behavior?"**

---

# Why Performance Assessment Matters

A security awareness program consumes:

* Employee time.
* Security team resources.
* Training budgets.
* Technology costs.
* Management attention.

Leadership therefore needs evidence that these resources are producing value.

A strong performance assessment can demonstrate:

> Training participation increased.

> Phishing reporting improved.

> Repeat failures decreased.

> Human-related incidents declined.

> High-risk groups improved.

This provides a stronger business case for continued investment.

---

# The Performance Measurement Chain

A useful model is:

**Activity**

↓

Training delivered

↓

**Participation**

↓

Employees complete training

↓

**Knowledge**

↓

Employees understand security requirements

↓

**Behavior**

↓

Employees apply security practices

↓

**Outcome**

↓

Human-related risk decreases

This distinction prevents organizations from confusing activity with effectiveness.

---

# Activity Metrics

Activity metrics measure what the program does.

Examples:

* Number of training courses delivered.
* Number of awareness campaigns.
* Number of phishing simulations.
* Number of employees enrolled.
* Number of workshops conducted.
* Number of security communications sent.

These are useful for program management.

However, they do not demonstrate risk reduction by themselves.

---

# Participation Metrics

Participation metrics measure whether employees engage with the program.

Examples:

* Training completion rate.
* Workshop attendance.
* Simulation participation.
* Assessment participation.
* Campaign engagement.

For example:

> 97% of employees completed annual training.

This is positive, but it does not tell us whether employees behave more securely.

---

# Knowledge Metrics

Knowledge metrics determine whether employees understand security concepts.

Examples:

* Assessment scores.
* Quiz results.
* Knowledge-test improvement.
* Scenario-based assessment results.
* Retention testing.

For example:

### Before Training

Average score: 64%

### After Training

Average score: 91%

This indicates knowledge improvement.

However:

> **Knowledge improvement does not necessarily equal behavioral improvement.**

---

# Behavioral Metrics

Behavioral metrics are generally more valuable for assessing security awareness effectiveness.

Examples include:

* Phishing click rate.
* Credential submission rate.
* Phishing reporting rate.
* Time to report.
* Repeat phishing failures.
* Policy violations.
* Data-handling incidents.

These metrics provide evidence of what employees actually do.

---

# Phishing Click Rate

One common metric is:

> **Phishing Click Rate**

It measures the percentage of users who interact with simulated phishing content.

For example:

**1,000 employees**

↓

**80 clicked**

↓

**Click rate = 8%**

A declining click rate over time may indicate improved awareness.

However, click rate should not be used alone.

---

# Phishing Reporting Rate

Reporting rate measures the percentage of employees who correctly report simulated phishing messages.

For example:

**1,000 employees receive simulation**

↓

**650 report it**

↓

**Reporting rate = 65%**

An organization may want to see reporting increase while clicking decreases.

---

# Repeat Failure Rate

Repeat failure is particularly important.

Suppose:

> 10% of employees fail a phishing simulation.

If the same employees repeatedly fail multiple simulations, they may represent a higher human-risk population.

The organization can therefore identify:

> **Repeat responders**

and provide targeted intervention.

---

# Time to Report

Another useful metric is:

> **How quickly do employees report suspicious activity?**

For example:

### January

Average reporting time: 4 hours.

### June

Average reporting time: 35 minutes.

This may indicate improved employee responsiveness.

Fast reporting can provide security teams with more time to contain threats.

---

# Reporting Quality

Simply reporting something is not always enough.

A mature program may also assess:

* Was the report actually suspicious?
* Was sufficient information provided?
* Was the report submitted through the correct channel?
* Did the employee follow the expected process?

This helps distinguish **meaningful security behavior** from simply increasing report volume.

---

# False Positive Reporting

Awareness programs should also consider false positives.

Suppose employees report:

> 1,000 legitimate emails as phishing.

This could create additional workload for the SOC.

Therefore, performance assessment should balance:

* Detection.
* Reporting.
* Accuracy.

The goal is:

> **Appropriate reporting, not maximum reporting at any cost.**

---

# Knowledge Retention

A post-training test may show:

> 95% knowledge score.

But six months later, employees may no longer remember the material.

Therefore, organizations can conduct periodic retention assessments.

For example:

**Immediately after training**

92%

↓

**Three months later**

83%

↓

**Six months later**

71%

This may indicate the need for reinforcement.

---

# Security Behavior Over Time

Performance should be analyzed as a trend.

For example:

| Quarter | Click Rate | Reporting Rate |
| ------- | ---------: | -------------: |
| Q1      |        14% |            42% |
| Q2      |        11% |            51% |
| Q3      |         8% |            62% |
| Q4      |         6% |            71% |

This provides stronger evidence than reporting a single quarterly result.

---

# Before-and-After Analysis

Organizations can compare performance before and after interventions.

Example:

### Before BEC Training

Finance phishing failure rate:

> 18%

### After BEC Training

Failure rate:

> 9%

### Later Simulation

Failure rate:

> 6%

This provides evidence that the intervention may have improved behavior.

However, other contributing factors should also be considered.

---

# Control Group Considerations

For more sophisticated assessments, an organization could compare groups.

For example:

### Group A

Receives targeted BEC training.

### Group B

Receives standard awareness training.

Both groups participate in a later simulation.

If Group A performs significantly better, the organization has stronger evidence that the targeted intervention was effective.

Organizations must ensure that such testing is ethical, fair, and consistent with internal policies.

---

# Measuring High-Risk Populations

Enterprise averages can hide important problems.

Consider:

| Group             | Failure Rate |
| ----------------- | -----------: |
| General Employees |           5% |
| IT                |           3% |
| HR                |           7% |
| Finance           |          17% |
| Executives        |          14% |

The enterprise average might look acceptable.

But Finance and Executives require additional attention.

Performance assessment should therefore include **role-based analysis**.

---

# Risk-Based Performance

Performance should be interpreted in the context of risk.

For example:

> A 5% phishing failure rate among general employees may be concerning.

But:

> A 5% failure rate among privileged administrators may represent greater risk.

Therefore:

> **The importance of a metric depends on the population, access level, threat exposure, and business impact.**

---

# Human Risk Scoring

Organizations may combine multiple indicators to identify higher-risk populations.

For example:

**Risk indicators**

* Repeated phishing failures.
* Failure to complete training.
* Policy violations.
* Suspicious behavior.
* High-risk privileges.

These can be combined into a human-risk profile.

However, organizations should use such systems carefully.

The purpose should be:

> **Risk reduction and targeted support**

rather than employee punishment.

---

# Ethical Considerations

Human-risk measurement can create privacy and employee-relations concerns.

Organizations should consider:

* Transparency.
* Data minimization.
* Purpose limitation.
* Access controls.
* Appropriate retention.
* Fairness.
* Avoiding unnecessary surveillance.
* Avoiding punitive use of awareness metrics.

Employees should understand how their data is being used where appropriate.

---

# Security Culture Metrics

Performance assessment can also examine culture.

Possible indicators include:

* Employee willingness to report incidents.
* Employee confidence in reporting.
* Management participation.
* Security champion activity.
* Employee survey results.
* Security-related suggestions.
* Participation in awareness events.

Culture is difficult to measure precisely, so multiple indicators should be considered.

---

# Employee Survey Example

An organization could ask employees to rate:

> "I know how to report a suspected phishing email."

> "I feel comfortable reporting a security mistake."

> "My manager takes cybersecurity seriously."

> "I understand my security responsibilities."

Responses can be tracked over time.

For example:

### Employee Confidence

2025: 64%

2026: 82%

This may indicate improved security culture.

---

# Incident Metrics

The strongest performance assessments connect awareness activities with real incidents.

Examples:

* Number of phishing-related incidents.
* Number of BEC incidents.
* Number of accidental data disclosures.
* Number of policy violations.
* Number of social-engineering incidents.
* Number of compromised accounts caused by phishing.

However, organizations should be careful when interpreting incident counts.

An increase in reported incidents does not necessarily mean security awareness is getting worse.

---

# Reporting Paradox

Suppose phishing reports increase from:

> 500 → 2,000

while phishing incidents increase from:

> 50 → 60.

At first glance, incidents increased.

But the organization may actually be improving because employees are detecting and reporting more suspicious activity.

Therefore:

> **More reporting can sometimes indicate better security awareness rather than worse security.**

Context matters.

---

# Leading and Lagging Indicators

Performance assessments should use both.

### Leading Indicators

These indicate future behavior.

Examples:

* Training completion.
* Knowledge scores.
* Simulation participation.
* Reporting behavior.
* Employee confidence.

### Lagging Indicators

These show outcomes.

Examples:

* Security incidents.
* Data exposure.
* BEC losses.
* Account compromises.
* Regulatory incidents.

A mature program uses both categories.

---

# KPI Examples

Useful security awareness KPIs include:

### Training Completion

> Percentage of required employees completing training.

### Phishing Failure Rate

> Percentage of employees interacting with simulated phishing.

### Reporting Rate

> Percentage of users correctly reporting simulations.

### Repeat Failure Rate

> Percentage of users repeatedly failing simulations.

### Time to Report

> Average time between receiving a suspicious message and reporting it.

### Knowledge Improvement

> Change in assessment scores after training.

---

# KRI Examples

Key Risk Indicators may include:

* Percentage of high-risk users repeatedly failing simulations.
* Number of BEC incidents.
* Number of social-engineering incidents.
* Number of privileged users exhibiting risky behavior.
* Number of business units without role-based training.
* Percentage of third parties without required awareness training.

KPIs tell you:

> **How the program is performing.**

KRIs tell you:

> **Where risk may be increasing.**

---

Executives generally need concise information.

A dashboard could show:

| Metric                          | Current | Target | Trend |
| ------------------------------- | ------: | -----: | ----- |
| Training completion             |     97% |   ≥95% | ↑     |
| Phishing failure                |      6% |    <5% | ↓     |
| Reporting rate                  |     71% |   >70% | ↑     |
| Repeat failures                 |      3% |    <3% | ↓     |
| BEC incidents                   |       2 |      0 | ↓     |
| High-risk third parties trained |     84% |   100% | ↑     |

The dashboard should focus on risk and trends.

---

# Measuring Return on Investment

Security awareness programs can also be evaluated from an investment perspective.

For example:

**Program Cost**

* Training platform.
* Personnel.
* Content.
* Simulations.
* Communications.

versus:

**Potential Risk Reduction**

* Fewer successful phishing attacks.
* Reduced fraud.
* Faster incident reporting.
* Reduced data exposure.

Calculating exact financial ROI can be difficult because prevented incidents are hypothetical.

Therefore, organizations should avoid claiming:

> "The training saved €5 million."

unless there is defensible evidence.

A more appropriate statement may be:

> "The program reduced phishing failure by 50% and increased reporting by 40%."

# Benchmarking Performance

Organizations can compare performance against:

* Previous quarters.
* Previous years.
* Internal targets.
* Business units.
* Risk groups.
* Industry benchmarks.

Internal trends are often more useful than generic external benchmarks because organizations have different:

* Risk profiles.
* Employee populations.
* Technologies.
* Business models.

---

# Avoiding Metric Manipulation

Metrics can create unintended behavior.

For example:

> "We need a phishing reporting rate above 90%."

Employees may start reporting almost every email.

The result looks good, but the metric becomes less meaningful.

Metrics should therefore be designed to encourage the **desired security behavior**, not simply maximize a number.

---

# Performance Assessment and Continuous Improvement

The results should feed directly into program improvement.

For example:

**Assessment**

↓

Finance has high BEC failure.

↓

**Analysis**

Employees understand phishing but not payment-verification procedures.

↓

**Intervention**

BEC workshop + process improvement.

↓

**Simulation**

Measure behavior.

↓

**Result**

Failure decreases.

↓

**Reassessment**

Determine whether improvement is sustained.

This is a continuous improvement cycle.

---

# Practical Enterprise Example

Consider an organization with 10,000 employees.

### Baseline

* Training completion: 92%.
* Phishing failure: 15%.
* Reporting rate: 35%.
* Average reporting time: 6 hours.
* Repeat failure: 12%.

The organization introduces:

* Targeted training.
* Monthly microlearning.
* Phishing simulations.
* Reporting-button improvements.
* Security champion engagement.

Six months later:

* Training completion: 98%.
* Phishing failure: 7%.
* Reporting rate: 68%.
* Average reporting time: 48 minutes.
* Repeat failure: 5%.

The performance assessment can demonstrate substantial improvement.

---

# But Is the Program Actually Reducing Risk?

The organization should then ask:

> Did real-world incidents also improve?

Suppose:

### Before

12 phishing-related account compromises.

### After

5 phishing-related account compromises.

This provides stronger evidence that awareness improvements may be contributing to risk reduction.

Still, other security controls may have changed during the same period, so causation should be interpreted carefully.

For GRC professionals, performance assessment provides evidence that controls are functioning.

The relationship can be represented as:

**Risk**

↓

↓

**Control**

↓

**Control Activity**

Training + simulations + campaigns

↓

**Performance**

Behavioral metrics

↓

**Assurance**

Assessment and testing

↓

**Risk Decision**

Accept / mitigate / transfer / avoid

This allows the awareness program to become part of the broader enterprise control environment.

---

# Key Takeaways

1. **Performance assessment determines how effectively the security awareness program is achieving its objectives.**
2. **It is different from gap analysis, which identifies deficiencies.**
3. **Training completion is an activity metric, not proof of effectiveness.**
4. **Knowledge should be measured separately from behavior.**
5. **Behavioral metrics provide stronger evidence of real-world effectiveness.**
6. **Phishing failure, reporting rate, repeat failure, and time to report are useful indicators.**
7. **High-risk roles should be analyzed separately from enterprise averages.**
8. **Both leading and lagging indicators should be used.**
9. **KPIs measure program performance, while KRIs highlight areas of risk.**
10. **Security culture can be assessed through surveys and behavioral indicators.**
11. **Increased reporting can sometimes indicate improved awareness rather than increased risk.**
12. **Metrics should encourage the right behavior rather than simply maximize numerical targets.**
13. **Human-risk measurement should respect privacy, fairness, and appropriate data-use principles.**
14. **Performance should be analyzed over time rather than as isolated numbers.**
15. **Before-and-after comparisons can demonstrate the effect of interventions.**
16. **Performance results should be connected to real security incidents where possible.**
17. **Executive dashboards should communicate trends, risk, and actionable findings.**
18. **Performance assessments can demonstrate the value of security awareness investments.**
19. **Assessment results should feed into continuous improvement.**
20. **The ultimate measure of success is whether the program contributes to reduced human-related security risk.**

The fundamental model is:

**Measure → Analyze → Interpret → Improve → Measure Again**

The most important principle is:

> **A security awareness program should not be judged by how much training it delivers, but by whether employees demonstrate safer behavior and whether that behavior contributes to measurable reduction in security risk.**

## Recommendations and Improvement Plan

The final stage of a **Security Awareness Program Assessment** is to transform the assessment findings into a practical **Recommendations and Improvement Plan**.

An assessment has limited value if it ends with a report that simply says:

* Training is incomplete.
* Phishing risk is high.
* Third-party awareness is inconsistent.
* Metrics need improvement.

The organization needs to know:

> **What should we do, who should do it, when should it happen, and how will we know that it worked?**

A recommendations and improvement plan provides that bridge between **assessment** and **action**.

---

# From Findings to Improvement

The overall process can be represented as:

**Assessment**

↓

**Findings**

↓

**Root Cause**

↓

**Risk**

↓

**Recommendation**

↓

**Action Plan**

↓

**Implementation**

↓

**Validation**

↓

**Continuous Improvement**

The objective is to ensure that every significant finding results in an appropriate response.

---

# What Is a Recommendation?

A recommendation is a proposed action to address an identified weakness or opportunity.

For example:

### Finding

Finance employees have a 17% phishing simulation failure rate.

### Recommendation

Implement targeted Business Email Compromise (BEC) awareness training and scenario-based simulations for Finance personnel.

The recommendation should address the underlying problem rather than simply repeating the finding.

---

# What Makes a Good Recommendation?

A good recommendation should be:

* Specific.
* Risk-based.
* Actionable.
* Realistic.
* Measurable.
* Assigned to an owner.
* Given a target date.
* Supported by evidence.

Weak:

> "Improve phishing awareness."

Strong:

> "Implement quarterly BEC-focused simulations for Finance and Accounts Payable personnel and reduce the repeat-failure rate from 17% to below 7% within two quarters."

The second recommendation provides a clear objective.

---

# Recommendation vs. Corrective Action

These terms are related but different.

### Recommendation

Describes what should be done.

> Implement role-based BEC training for Finance.

### Corrective Action

Describes the specific action being implemented.

> Develop a two-hour BEC module, assign it through the LMS, and conduct quarterly simulations.

A mature improvement plan converts recommendations into specific corrective actions.

---

# Prioritizing Recommendations

Organizations may identify dozens of findings.

They cannot necessarily address everything immediately.

Recommendations should therefore be prioritized according to factors such as:

* Risk severity.
* Business impact.
* Likelihood.
* Regulatory importance.
* Threat activity.
* Number of affected users.
* Criticality of affected systems.
* Implementation effort.
* Available resources.

---

# Risk-Based Priority

A simple priority model might be:

### Critical

Immediate remediation required.

### High

Remediation should be prioritized.

### Medium

Address within the planned improvement cycle.

### Low

Address when resources permit.

The organization should use its established enterprise risk methodology where one exists.

---

# Risk vs. Effort

Another useful approach is to compare:

**Risk Reduction**

against

**Implementation Effort**

|                     | Low Effort         | High Effort           |
| ------------------- | ------------------ | --------------------- |
| High Risk Reduction | Quick Wins         | Strategic Initiatives |
| Low Risk Reduction  | Minor Improvements | Lower Priority        |

For example:

### Quick Win

Standardize the phishing-reporting instructions.

### Strategic Initiative

Implement an enterprise Human Risk Management platform.

This helps leadership allocate resources efficiently.

Quick wins are improvements that can be implemented relatively quickly while providing meaningful value.

Examples include:

* Updating outdated training content.
* Adding phishing-reporting instructions.
* Improving training reminders.
* Updating security awareness posters.
* Adding security awareness to onboarding.
* Creating executive security briefings.
* Updating incident-reporting procedures.

Quick wins can build momentum for larger initiatives.

# Strategic Improvements

Some recommendations require substantial investment.

Examples include:

* Enterprise Human Risk Management.

* Security champion programs.
* AI-enabled awareness platforms.

* Enterprise behavioral analytics.

These initiatives may require:

* Architecture.

# Assigning Ownership

Every significant action should have an accountable owner.

Possible owners include:

* CISO.
* Security Awareness Manager.
* GRC.
* HR.
* IT.
* SOC.
* Privacy.

Avoid assigning:

> "Security Team"

For example:

> **Security Awareness Manager**

rather than:

> **Information Security**

# RACI Model

A **RACI** model can clarify responsibilities.

**R – Responsible**

Performs the work.

**A – Accountable**

Owns the outcome.

**C – Consulted**

Provides expertise.

**I – Informed**

Needs to know the status.

Example:

| Activity             | CISO | Awareness | HR | GRC | Business |
| -------------------- | ---- | --------- | -- | --- | -------- |
| Develop training     | A    | R         | C  | C   | C        |
| Assign training      | I    | R         | R  | I   | C        |
| Measure results      | A    | R         | C  | R   | I        |
| Report to executives | A    | R         | I  | C   | I        |

This prevents ambiguity.

# Setting Target Dates

Improvement plans should include realistic deadlines.

For example:

### 30 Days

* Update awareness policy.
* Standardize reporting instructions.
* Identify high-risk populations.

### 60 Days

* Develop targeted training.
* Establish new metrics.
* Integrate third-party requirements.

### 90 Days

* Launch targeted simulations.
* Begin executive reporting.
* Establish security champion program.

### 6–12 Months

* Implement advanced analytics.
* Measure long-term behavior change.
* Reassess program maturity.

---

# Defining Success Criteria

Every major recommendation should have measurable success criteria.

Weak:

> "Improve phishing awareness."

Better:

> "Reduce phishing simulation click rate among Finance employees from 17% to below 8%."

Another example:

### Finding

Third-party training completion is only 62%.

### Recommendation

Implement mandatory security awareness training for high-risk contractors.

### Success Criterion

> 95%+ of applicable third-party personnel complete training before system access.

This makes the improvement measurable.

---

# SMART Objectives

Improvement objectives can follow the **SMART** model:

### Specific

Clearly define what needs to change.

### Measurable

Define how success will be measured.

### Achievable

Ensure the target is realistic.

### Relevant

Connect the action to organizational risk.

### Time-Bound

Specify when the objective should be achieved.

Example:

> "Reduce repeat phishing failures among privileged users from 9% to below 3% within six months."

This is a much stronger improvement objective than:

> "Improve privileged-user awareness."

---

# Recommendations Should Address Root Causes

Suppose:

### Finding

Employees fail to report phishing.

A weak recommendation is:

> Conduct more phishing training.

But root-cause analysis reveals:

> Employees don't know where to report suspicious messages.

The better recommendation is:

> Standardize the reporting mechanism, integrate a reporting button into the email client, and reinforce the process through simulations.

The second recommendation addresses the actual cause.

---

# Avoiding "Training as the Solution"

This is one of the most important principles in security awareness.

Not every human-related problem requires more training.

For example:

### Problem

Employees approve fraudulent payment requests.

Possible solutions include:

* Training.
* Dual approval.
* Callback verification.
* Segregation of duties.
* MFA.
* Email security.
* Transaction monitoring.

The improvement plan should select the combination of controls that best reduces the risk.

# Status Categories

* **Open**
* **Planned**
* **In Progress**
* **Implemented**
* **Validated**
* **Closed**
* **Risk Accepted**

The distinction between **Implemented** and **Validated** is important.

---

# Implementation vs. Validation

Suppose an organization implements new phishing training.

The action is:

> **Implemented**

But the organization should still determine whether it worked.

After several months:

> Phishing failure rate decreased from 15% to 6%.

Now there is evidence of effectiveness.

Therefore:

> **Implemented ≠ Effective**

Validation is necessary.

---

# Closing a Finding

A finding should not be closed simply because someone completed an action.

Closure should require evidence.

For example:

### Finding

High phishing failure rate.

### Action

New training implemented.

### Evidence

* Training records.
* Simulation results.
* Behavioral metrics.

### Validation

Failure rate decreased from 18% to 7%.

### Status

**Closed / Validated**

This provides stronger assurance.

---

# Residual Risk

Even after remediation, some risk may remain.

For example:

### Initial Risk

High.

↓

### Remediation

Targeted training + technical controls.

↓

### Current Risk

Medium.

The organization may decide:

* Continue monitoring.
* Implement additional controls.
* Accept the remaining risk.

Security awareness improvement should therefore connect with enterprise risk management.

---

# Risk Acceptance

Sometimes a gap cannot be completely eliminated.

Management may decide to accept the remaining risk.

However, risk acceptance should be:

* Formal.
* Documented.
* Approved by the appropriate authority.
* Time-bound where appropriate.
* Periodically reviewed.

It should not simply be treated as:

> "We don't have enough resources."

# Practical Case Study

Consider a company with 15,000 employees.

The assessment identifies four major findings:

### Finding 1

Phishing failure rate is 13%.

### Finding 2

Third-party training completion is only 55%.

### Finding 3

Security awareness metrics focus primarily on completion.

### Finding 4

Generative AI security risks are not addressed.

| Finding       | Action                        | Priority | Success Measure                |
| ------------- | ----------------------------- | -------- | ------------------------------ |
| Phishing      | Targeted simulations          | High     | Failure <6%                    |

| Metrics       | Behavioral dashboard          | Medium   | KPIs implemented               |
| AI risk       | AI security awareness         | High     | 100% target population trained |

After implementation, the organization should reassess the results.

# Improvement Does Not Mean Perfection

A mature program does not aim for:

> **Zero human error.**

Humans will occasionally:

* Make mistakes.
* Click something malicious.
* Misunderstand a policy.
* Forget a procedure.
* Report something incorrectly.

The objective is to:

> **Reduce the probability and impact of human-related security incidents and improve the organization's ability to detect and respond to them.**

This is a much more realistic security objective.

# Lesson 16.9 – Measuring Security Awareness Effectiveness

> **Chapter:** 16 – Security Awareness
>
> **Topic:** Measuring Security Awareness Effectiveness
>
> **Difficulty:** Beginner
>
> **Estimated Reading Time:** 10–15 minutes

## Introduction

A security awareness program should not be judged only by how much training an organization delivers.

An organization may have:

* 100% training completion.
* Hundreds of awareness emails.
* Multiple security workshops.
* Regular phishing simulations.

Yet employees may still make security mistakes or fail to report suspicious activity.

This creates an important distinction:

> **Training activity does not automatically equal security effectiveness.**

Measuring security awareness effectiveness allows an organization to determine whether its awareness activities are actually improving employee knowledge, behavior, and security outcomes.

## Why Measure Security Awareness?

Measurement helps an organization answer important questions:

* Are employees completing required training?
* Do employees understand security requirements?
* Are employees changing their behavior?
* Are phishing reporting rates improving?
* Are security-related mistakes decreasing?
* Which departments require additional support?
* Are awareness investments reducing human-related risk?
* Is the program meeting its objectives?

Without measurement, security awareness can become a collection of activities without evidence that those activities are producing meaningful results.

## From Activity to Effectiveness

A mature awareness program should progress through several levels of measurement:

**Activity → Knowledge → Behavior → Risk Reduction**

For example:

**Activity**

Employees complete phishing awareness training.

↓

**Knowledge**

Employees demonstrate that they understand phishing indicators.

↓

**Behavior**

Employees correctly identify and report simulated phishing messages.

↓

**Risk Reduction**

Employees are less likely to interact with real phishing attacks.

Each level provides stronger evidence of effectiveness.

### Activity Metrics

Activity metrics measure what the organization did.

Examples include:

* Number of employees trained.
* Training completion rate.
* Number of awareness campaigns.
* Number of phishing simulations.
* Number of newsletters distributed.
* Number of workshops conducted.

These metrics are useful for program management but do not prove that security behavior improved.

### Knowledge Metrics

Knowledge metrics measure whether employees understood the training.

Examples include:

* Assessment scores.
* Quiz results.
* Knowledge-check performance.
* Training assessment pass rates.
* Survey results.

For example:

> 94% of employees achieved the required score on the phishing awareness assessment.

This provides more information than completion alone, but it still does not prove that employees will behave securely in real situations.

### Behavioral Metrics

Behavioral metrics measure what employees actually do.

Examples include:

* Phishing simulation click rate.
* Phishing reporting rate.
* Time to report suspicious messages.
* Secure data-handling behavior.
* Incident reporting behavior.
* Repeated security mistakes.

Behavioral metrics are generally more valuable for determining whether awareness is influencing day-to-day security practices.

### Risk Metrics

Risk metrics connect employee behavior to organizational risk.

Examples include:

* Reduction in human-related security incidents.
* Reduction in successful phishing attacks.
* Reduction in accidental data exposure.
* Reduction in policy violations.
* Reduction in security incidents caused by human error.

These metrics help management understand whether awareness activities are contributing to broader cybersecurity objectives.

## Awareness Metrics

An awareness metric is a measurement used to understand some aspect of the security awareness program.

Examples include:

| Metric                  | What It Measures       |
| ----------------------- | ---------------------- |
| Training completion     | Participation          |
| Assessment score        | Knowledge              |
| Phishing click rate     | Risky behavior         |
| Phishing reporting rate | Positive behavior      |
| Time to report          | Responsiveness         |
| Repeat failure rate     | Persistent weakness    |
| Security incident rate  | Potential risk outcome |

A mature program should use a combination of metrics rather than relying on one number.

## Completion Rate

Training completion is one of the most commonly reported awareness metrics.

It is calculated as:

**Completion Rate = Completed Training ÷ Assigned Training × 100**

For example:

> 950 employees complete mandatory training out of 1,000 assigned employees.

The completion rate is:

> **95%**

This can demonstrate that the organization is successfully delivering required training.

However, a 95% completion rate does not necessarily mean that employees are security-aware.

An employee could complete a course without understanding or applying the information.

## Assessment Scores

Assessment scores provide additional information.

For example:

> Average phishing awareness assessment score: 88%

This may indicate that employees understand the material presented in the course.

However, assessment results can also be misleading.

Employees may memorize answers without developing the ability to apply the concepts in realistic situations.

This is why knowledge measurements should be combined with behavioral measurements.

## Phishing Simulation Metrics

Phishing simulations provide useful behavioral data.

Common metrics include:

* Click rate.
* Credential submission rate.
* Attachment interaction rate.
* Reporting rate.
* Time to report.
* Repeat interaction rate.

For example:

**Click rate:** 10%

**Reporting rate:** 38%

**Credential submission:** 2%

These metrics provide a more realistic picture of employee behavior than training completion alone.

## Incident Reporting

Employee reporting behavior is an important security awareness indicator.

Suppose employees receive suspicious emails in the normal course of business.

The organization can measure:

* Number of reports.
* Percentage of employees reporting.
* Time required to report.
* Number of legitimate threats identified through employee reports.

An increase in reporting may indicate that employees are becoming more comfortable identifying and reporting suspicious activity.

However, organizations should also consider whether employees are submitting excessive false reports.

### False Positives

Not every reported message will be malicious.

Employees may report legitimate emails because they are uncertain.

This is not necessarily a negative outcome.

Early in a security awareness program, increased reporting may actually be desirable because employees are becoming more willing to ask for help.

The security team can then improve employee guidance on distinguishing suspicious activity from legitimate communication.

## Behavioral Change Measurement

Behavioral change is one of the most important but difficult aspects of awareness measurement.

The organization should ask:

> **Are employees doing something differently because of the awareness program?**

Examples include:

* More employees reporting phishing.
* Fewer employees clicking simulated phishing links.
* More employees using approved data-sharing methods.
* Fewer employees leaving sensitive documents unsecured.
* More employees using MFA correctly.
* Faster reporting of security incidents.

Behavioral change should ideally be measured over time rather than through a single event.

## Measuring Trends

A single measurement provides limited insight.

For example:

> Phishing click rate = 12%

Without historical information, it is difficult to determine whether this is good or bad.

Suppose previous results were:

| Campaign | Click Rate |
| -------- | ---------: |
| 1        |        25% |
| 2        |        19% |
| 3        |        15% |
| 4        |        12% |

The trend indicates improvement.

Similarly, if reporting rates increase:

| Campaign | Reporting Rate |
| -------- | -------------: |
| 1        |            14% |
| 2        |            22% |
| 3        |            31% |
| 4        |            39% |

the organization can see evidence of stronger reporting behavior.

## Baselines

Before setting improvement targets, organizations should establish a baseline.

A baseline represents the organization's starting point.

For example:

> Initial phishing click rate: 23%

The organization may then establish a target:

> Target click rate: below 12%

Future measurements can be compared against the baseline.

Baselines should be established for the most important awareness behaviors rather than every possible activity.

## Benchmarks and Targets

Organizations may establish internal targets for awareness metrics.

Examples:

* 95% mandatory training completion.
* 90% assessment pass rate.
* Increasing phishing reporting rate.
* Decreasing simulated phishing interaction rate.
* Reducing repeat failures.
* Improving incident reporting time.

Targets should be realistic and risk-based.

A target should not exist simply because another organization achieved a particular number.

### Why 100% Is Not Always the Goal

Organizations sometimes assume that every security metric should reach 100%.

For example:

> "Our target should be zero employees clicking phishing simulations."

This may not be realistic.

Real phishing attacks are continuously evolving, and some simulated scenarios may intentionally be sophisticated.

A more meaningful objective may be:

* Continuous improvement.
* Reduced high-risk behavior.
* Increased reporting.
* Faster detection.
* Better resilience among high-risk roles.

## Measuring Training Effectiveness

Training effectiveness can be evaluated by comparing employee performance before and after training.

For example:

**Before training**

Phishing click rate: 20%

**After training**

Phishing click rate: 13%

**Reporting rate**

Before: 17%

After: 35%

This provides stronger evidence that training may have influenced behavior.

However, the organization should avoid claiming that training alone caused the improvement without considering other factors.

## Correlation Between Awareness and Incidents

Organizations can also examine whether awareness improvements correspond with changes in actual security incidents.

For example:

> Employee phishing reports increased while successful phishing incidents decreased.

This may indicate that improved awareness is contributing to stronger organizational resilience.

However, cybersecurity incidents are influenced by many factors, including:

* Technical controls.
* Threat volume.
* Attack sophistication.
* Security monitoring.
* Identity controls.
* Email filtering.
* Incident response.

Therefore, awareness metrics should not be interpreted in isolation.

## Leading and Lagging Indicators

Security awareness programs can use both **leading** and **lagging indicators**.

### Leading Indicators

Leading indicators provide information about activities or behaviors that may influence future outcomes.

Examples include:

* Training completion.
* Assessment performance.
* Phishing reporting.
* Participation in awareness activities.
* Security policy acknowledgments.

### Lagging Indicators

Lagging indicators measure outcomes that have already occurred.

Examples include:

* Number of successful phishing incidents.
* Security incidents caused by human error.
* Data exposure incidents.
* Policy violations.

A mature program should use both.

For example:

**Leading indicator:**

Phishing reporting increased.

**Lagging indicator:**

Successful phishing incidents decreased.

Together, these provide a stronger picture of program effectiveness.

## Risk-Based Measurement

Not every awareness topic has the same importance.

Organizations should prioritize measurement according to risk.

For example, a financial institution may place greater emphasis on:

* Phishing.
* Business email compromise.
* Data protection.
* Social engineering.

A software company may place greater emphasis on:

* Secure development.
* Secrets management.
* Data protection.
* Supply chain security.

A healthcare organization may prioritize:

* Privacy.
* Patient information protection.
* Phishing.
* Unauthorized disclosure.

Metrics should therefore reflect the organization's actual threat and risk environment.

## GRC Perspective

From a GRC perspective, security awareness metrics provide evidence that security controls are being monitored and evaluated.

A useful relationship is:

**Requirement → Awareness Objective → Metric → Result → Risk Assessment → Improvement**

For example:

**Requirement:** Employees must receive security awareness training.

**Objective:** Ensure employees understand phishing risks.

**Metric:** Phishing reporting rate.

**Result:** Reporting increased from 20% to 37%.

**Risk Assessment:** Employee phishing resilience improved.

**Improvement:** Continue targeted training for high-risk groups.

This makes awareness measurement part of the broader governance and risk management process.

## Common Measurement Mistakes

Organizations should avoid several common mistakes.

### Measuring Only Completion

Completion demonstrates participation, not effectiveness.

### Using Too Many Metrics

A dashboard containing dozens of metrics can make it difficult to identify what actually matters.

### Ignoring Behavioral Data

Knowledge tests alone do not demonstrate secure behavior.

### Focusing Only on Negative Behavior

Reporting and other positive security behaviors should also be measured.

### Comparing Inconsistent Campaigns

Metrics can be misleading if the difficulty of simulations or employee populations changes significantly.

### Ignoring Context

A sudden increase in phishing clicks may have several possible explanations.

### Failing to Act

Measurement has little value if the organization does not use the results to improve the program.

## Building an Awareness Measurement Framework

A simple framework can be structured around four levels:

### Level 1 – Participation

**Did employees participate?**

Examples:

* Completion rate.
* Attendance.
* Course participation.

### Level 2 – Knowledge

**Did employees understand the material?**

Examples:

* Assessment scores.
* Knowledge checks.
* Quiz performance.

### Level 3 – Behavior

**Did employees change what they do?**

Examples:

* Phishing reporting.
* Reduced clicking.
* Faster incident reporting.

### Level 4 – Risk

**Did the organization's human-related risk decrease?**

Examples:

* Reduced successful phishing incidents.
* Reduced human-error incidents.
* Reduced data exposure.

This model helps organizations move beyond basic training statistics.

## Key Takeaways

Measuring security awareness effectiveness allows organizations to determine whether their awareness program is actually improving security outcomes.

The key principles are:

1. **Measure more than training completion.**
2. **Evaluate knowledge as well as participation.**
3. **Measure real or simulated security behavior.**
4. **Track trends over time.**
5. **Establish meaningful baselines and targets.**
6. **Use both leading and lagging indicators.**
7. **Prioritize metrics according to organizational risk.**
8. **Connect awareness metrics to broader security outcomes.**
9. **Avoid excessive or meaningless metrics.**
10. **Use measurement results to continuously improve the awareness program.**

The ultimate purpose of security awareness measurement is not to prove that employees completed training. It is to determine whether the organization is **building stronger security behaviors and reducing human-related cybersecurity risk**.

## KPIs and KRIs

Security awareness programs generate a large amount of information, but not every measurement is equally useful for decision-making. Organizations need a structured way to identify which measurements should be monitored, reported, and used to drive improvement.

Two important categories are **Key Performance Indicators (KPIs)** and **Key Risk Indicators (KRIs)**.

Although they are related, they answer different questions:

> **KPI:** How well is the security awareness program performing?

> **KRI:** What level of security risk is the organization facing?

Understanding the difference allows security awareness teams and GRC professionals to build more meaningful dashboards and management reports.

## What Is a KPI?

A **Key Performance Indicator (KPI)** is a measurable value used to determine whether a program, process, or activity is achieving its objectives.

For security awareness, KPIs generally focus on the **performance of the awareness program**.

Examples include:

* Security awareness training completion rate.
* Training assessment pass rate.
* Phishing simulation reporting rate.
* Phishing simulation click-rate reduction.
* Security awareness campaign participation.
* Average time to complete required training.
* Percentage of employees completing remedial training.

For example:

> **KPI:** 95% of employees complete mandatory security awareness training within the required period.

This tells management how effectively the organization is delivering the training program.

## What Is a KRI?

A **Key Risk Indicator (KRI)** is a measurement that provides information about the level or changing condition of a risk.

For security awareness, KRIs focus more directly on **human-related cybersecurity exposure**.

Examples include:

* High-risk phishing interaction rate.
* Credential submission rate during simulations.
* Number of repeat phishing failures.
* Security incidents caused by human error.
* Number of sensitive-data handling violations.
* Number of successful social engineering incidents.
* Percentage of high-risk employees demonstrating unsafe behavior.

For example:

> **KRI:** 14% of employees in a high-risk finance function interacted with a business-email-compromise simulation.

This provides information about potential human-related risk.

## KPI vs. KRI

The distinction can be illustrated as follows:

| Measurement                 | Category | What It Tells Us                                      |
| --------------------------- | -------- | ----------------------------------------------------- |
| Training completion         | KPI      | Is training being delivered?                          |
| Assessment score            | KPI      | Did employees understand the content?                 |
| Campaign participation      | KPI      | Are employees engaging?                               |
| Phishing reporting rate     | KPI      | Are employees performing the desired behavior?        |
| Repeat phishing failure     | KRI      | Where does persistent human risk exist?               |
| Credential submission       | KRI      | How significant is a particular risky behavior?       |
| Human-error incidents       | KRI      | Is human behavior contributing to security incidents? |
| Successful phishing attacks | KRI      | Is the organization experiencing actual impact?       |

Some metrics can function as either KPIs or KRIs depending on how the organization defines and uses them.

For example, phishing click rate could be treated as a performance metric for the awareness team or as an indicator of human-related risk.

## Why KPIs Matter

KPIs help security awareness teams understand whether their program is operating effectively.

For example, a security awareness manager may monitor:

* 97% training completion.
* 91% assessment pass rate.
* 38% phishing reporting rate.
* 15% reduction in phishing interactions.

These measurements can demonstrate that awareness activities are being delivered and that employee behavior may be improving.

KPIs are particularly useful for **program management and operational reporting**.

## Why KRIs Matter

KRIs are important because management ultimately needs to understand **risk**, not simply training activity.

Consider two organizations:

### Organization A

* 99% training completion.
* 95% assessment pass rate.
* 20% phishing simulation click rate.

### Organization B

* 94% training completion.
* 88% assessment pass rate.
* 7% phishing simulation click rate.

Organization A appears stronger based on training metrics.

However, Organization B may have lower phishing-related human risk.

This illustrates why KPIs alone are insufficient.

## Linking KPIs and KRIs

The strongest awareness programs connect performance indicators with risk indicators.

For example:

**KPI**

95% of employees complete phishing awareness training.

↓

**Behavioral Measurement**

Phishing reporting increases from 20% to 35%.

↓

**KRI**

High-risk phishing interactions decrease from 18% to 9%.

↓

**Outcome**

Reduced exposure to phishing-related human risk.

This creates a logical chain between the awareness program and organizational risk.

## SMART KPIs

Security awareness KPIs should ideally follow the **SMART** principle:

**Specific**

The objective should be clearly defined.

**Measurable**

The result should be quantifiable.

**Achievable**

The target should be realistic.

**Relevant**

The measurement should support security objectives.

**Time-bound**

The organization should establish a defined measurement period.

For example:

> "Increase the phishing reporting rate from 25% to 40% among employees within six months."

This is more useful than:

> "Improve phishing awareness."

## Example Security Awareness KPIs

An organization could establish KPIs such as:

### Training Completion KPI

> Achieve at least 95% completion of mandatory annual security awareness training within 30 days of assignment.

### Assessment KPI

> Achieve an average assessment score of at least 85%.

### Reporting KPI

> Increase the percentage of employees reporting simulated phishing messages by 15 percentage points over the next year.

### Remediation KPI

> Ensure 100% of employees requiring targeted remediation complete the assigned training within the defined timeframe.

These KPIs provide measurable program objectives.

## Example Security Awareness KRIs

KRIs should focus on areas where human behavior could create significant security exposure.

### Phishing Interaction KRI

> More than 10% of employees interact with high-risk simulated phishing messages.

This could trigger additional awareness activities.

### Credential Submission KRI

> More than 3% of employees attempt to submit credentials during simulations.

Because credential compromise can have significant consequences, this may be treated as a higher-priority risk indicator.

### Repeat Failure KRI

> Employees who repeatedly interact with phishing simulations exceed a defined threshold.

This may indicate persistent human-related risk.

### Human-Error Incident KRI

> Security incidents attributed to human error increase by more than 10% during a reporting period.

This could trigger further investigation and corrective action.

## Thresholds

KRIs become particularly useful when organizations establish thresholds.

For example:

| KRI                   | Green |  Amber |  Red |
| --------------------- | ----: | -----: | ---: |
| Phishing click rate   |   <8% |  8–12% | >12% |
| Credential submission |   <1% |   1–3% |  >3% |
| Reporting rate        |  >40% | 25–40% | <25% |
| Repeat failures       |   <5% |  5–10% | >10% |

The exact thresholds should be based on organizational risk and historical performance.

They should not be copied blindly from another organization.

### Green

Risk is within the organization's expected tolerance.

### Amber

Risk requires monitoring or corrective action.

### Red

Risk exceeds the organization's defined tolerance and requires management attention.

## Risk Appetite and Awareness Metrics

Security awareness KRIs can be connected to the organization's **risk appetite**.

For example, an organization may determine that repeated credential-related phishing failures among privileged users represent an unacceptable level of risk.

The KRI could therefore focus specifically on:

> **Percentage of privileged users interacting with credential-harvesting simulations.**

This is more meaningful than measuring the entire workforce equally.

## Role-Based KPIs and KRIs

Different roles may require different measurements.

### Executives

Potential KRI:

* Response to executive impersonation simulations.

### Finance

Potential KRI:

* Response to simulated payment fraud.

### IT Administrators

Potential KRI:

* Response to simulated privileged-access attacks.

### Developers

Potential KRI:

* Secure handling of simulated secrets or sensitive development information.

### General Employees

Potential KPI:

* Phishing reporting rate.

This supports a **risk-based and role-based awareness program**.

## Leading KRIs

Some KRIs provide early warning before an actual security incident occurs.

For example:

> Increasing phishing simulation failures among finance employees.

This may indicate elevated risk even if the organization has not yet experienced a successful business email compromise.

Leading KRIs allow organizations to take action before the risk results in an actual incident.

## Lagging KRIs

Other KRIs measure risk after it has already resulted in an event.

Examples include:

* Number of successful phishing incidents.
* Number of data exposure incidents caused by human error.
* Number of compromised accounts resulting from phishing.
* Number of security incidents caused by social engineering.

These indicators are important but provide less opportunity for prevention.

A mature program should use both leading and lagging indicators.

## KPI and KRI Relationships

Consider the following example:

**KPI**

98% of employees completed phishing awareness training.

**KPI**

Phishing reporting rate increased by 20%.

**KRI**

Phishing simulation credential submission remains at 5%.

The first two measurements suggest that the program is performing well.

However, the third measurement indicates that a significant risk remains.

Management should therefore not conclude that the phishing risk has been solved simply because training completion is high.

## Dashboard Design

Security awareness dashboards should provide management with a concise view of performance and risk.

A dashboard might contain:

### Program Performance

* Training completion.
* Assessment performance.
* Campaign participation.

### Behavioral Performance

* Phishing click rate.
* Reporting rate.
* Time to report.

### Risk

* High-risk user behavior.
* Repeat failures.
* Human-related incidents.

### Trends

* Current result.
* Previous result.
* Target.
* Direction of change.

For example:

| Indicator             | Current | Target | Status      |
| --------------------- | ------: | -----: | ----------- |
| Training completion   |     97% |   ≥95% | On Target   |
| Assessment score      |     91% |   ≥85% | On Target   |
| Phishing reporting    |     39% |   ≥40% | Near Target |
| Credential submission |    2.5% |    <1% | Attention   |
| Repeat failures       |      8% |    <5% | Attention   |

This gives management a much clearer picture than reporting training completion alone.

## Avoiding KPI Overload

Organizations sometimes create too many KPIs.

A dashboard containing 30 or 40 awareness metrics may make it difficult for management to understand what really matters.

A better approach is to identify a small number of **critical indicators**.

For example:

**Three program KPIs**

* Training completion.
* Assessment performance.
* Phishing reporting.

**Three major KRIs**

* Phishing interaction.
* Credential submission.
* Human-related security incidents.

Additional operational metrics can remain available to the security awareness team without appearing on the executive dashboard.

## GRC Perspective

KPIs and KRIs fit naturally into a GRC framework.

A typical relationship is:

**Requirement → Objective → KPI → KRI → Risk Assessment → Management Action**

For example:

**Requirement**

Employees must receive security awareness training.

**Objective**

Improve employee resilience against phishing.

**KPI**

95% training completion.

**KRI**

Phishing credential-submission rate.

**Risk Assessment**

Credential-related human risk remains above tolerance.

**Management Action**

Provide targeted training to high-risk groups and strengthen technical email controls.

This approach ensures that awareness measurement contributes to broader cybersecurity governance.

## Using KPIs and KRIs for Continual Improvement

Metrics should drive action.

For example:

> **KPI:** Training completion increased from 90% to 97%.

This indicates better program execution.

But:

> **KRI:** Phishing credential submission remains unchanged at 4%.

This indicates that the organization may need to investigate why improved training completion is not producing the expected behavioral improvement.

Possible actions could include:

* Revising training content.
* Introducing more realistic scenarios.
* Increasing microlearning.
* Improving phishing reporting.
* Providing targeted training.
* Strengthening technical controls.

This is how measurement becomes part of continual improvement.

## Common Mistakes

Organizations should avoid several common mistakes.

### Treating Every Metric as a KPI

Not every measurement deserves executive-level attention.

### Measuring Only Training Activity

Completion does not prove risk reduction.

### Ignoring KRIs

Management needs visibility into risk, not only program performance.

### Using Arbitrary Thresholds

Thresholds should reflect organizational risk and historical data.

### Using the Same Metrics for Everyone

Different roles may have different security risks.

### Focusing Only on Negative Behavior

Positive security behaviors such as reporting should also be measured.

### Failing to Take Action

A KRI that consistently exceeds its threshold should trigger investigation or corrective action.

## Key Takeaways

KPIs and KRIs provide complementary perspectives on security awareness.

The key principles are:

1. **KPIs measure program performance.**
2. **KRIs measure security risk.**
3. **Use both KPIs and KRIs together.**
4. **Connect awareness metrics to organizational objectives.**
5. **Use SMART targets where appropriate.**
6. **Establish risk-based thresholds for important KRIs.**
7. **Use leading and lagging indicators.**
8. **Customize measurements for high-risk roles.**
9. **Keep executive dashboards focused on meaningful indicators.**
10. **Use KPI and KRI results to drive corrective action and continual improvement.**

The ultimate objective is to move from:

> **"How much security awareness training did we deliver?"**

to:

> **"How effectively is our awareness program reducing human-related cybersecurity risk?"**

That shift—from measuring **activity** to measuring **performance and risk**—is an essential characteristic of a mature security awareness and GRC program.

## Behavioral Change Measurement

Security awareness is ultimately intended to influence how people behave when they encounter security-related situations. Employees may complete training and pass knowledge assessments, but the real question is whether they **apply what they learned in their day-to-day activities**.

Behavioral change measurement therefore focuses on observable actions rather than simply measuring training participation.

> **Knowledge tells us what employees know. Behavior tells us what employees actually do.**

### Why Behavioral Change Matters

Consider an employee who completes a phishing awareness course and scores 95% on the final assessment.

This demonstrates knowledge.

However, if the same employee later clicks on a realistic phishing email without reporting it, the organization has evidence that knowledge has not necessarily translated into secure behavior.

A mature awareness program therefore seeks to establish a relationship between:

**Training → Knowledge → Behavior → Risk Reduction**

The most important part is the transition from **knowledge to behavior**.

## What Is Behavioral Change?

Behavioral change occurs when employees consistently modify their actions in response to security requirements, awareness activities, or organizational expectations.

Examples include:

* Reporting suspicious emails.
* Verifying unusual requests.
* Using approved data-sharing methods.
* Protecting confidential information.
* Locking unattended workstations.
* Following secure authentication practices.
* Reporting security incidents promptly.
* Avoiding unauthorized software or services.

Behavioral change should ideally be **observable, measurable, and sustained over time**.

## Knowledge vs. Behavior

These two concepts should not be confused.

### Knowledge

An employee knows:

> "Phishing emails can contain suspicious links."

### Behavior

The employee receives a suspicious email and:

> Checks the sender, examines the link, avoids clicking, and reports the message.

The second example demonstrates the application of knowledge.

Security awareness programs should therefore include methods for measuring both.

## The Behavior Measurement Process

A practical approach is:

**1. Define the desired behavior**

What should employees do?

**2. Establish a baseline**

What are employees doing today?

**3. Measure the behavior**

Collect objective evidence.

**4. Provide reinforcement**

Training, reminders, coaching, or other interventions.

**5. Measure again**

Determine whether behavior changed.

**6. Evaluate sustainability**

Determine whether the improvement continues over time.

This creates a measurable behavioral improvement cycle.

## Define Desired Behaviors

Before measuring behavior, the organization needs to define what "good" behavior looks like.

For example:

**Phishing**

> Employees should report suspicious emails using the approved reporting mechanism.

**Physical security**

> Employees should challenge or report unauthorized individuals attempting to access restricted areas.

**Data protection**

> Employees should use approved corporate systems to store and share sensitive information.

**Incident response**

> Employees should report suspected security incidents promptly.

Without clearly defined desired behaviors, it becomes difficult to determine whether the awareness program is successful.

## Establish a Behavioral Baseline

Organizations should establish a baseline before attempting to measure improvement.

For example:

> Baseline phishing reporting rate: 18%

After training:

> Reporting rate: 31%

After additional reinforcement:

> Reporting rate: 42%

This provides evidence of a behavioral trend.

Baselines may be established using:

* Phishing simulations.
* Security incident records.
* Help-desk reports.
* Security reporting systems.
* Surveys.
* Audits.
* Compliance assessments.
* Observation or testing.

## Phishing as a Behavioral Measurement

Phishing simulations are one of the easiest ways to measure security behavior.

Organizations can observe whether employees:

* Open suspicious messages.
* Click links.
* Interact with attachments.
* Attempt to submit information.
* Report suspicious messages.
* Report the message quickly.

For example:

| Behavior              | Baseline | Current |
| --------------------- | -------: | ------: |
| Click rate            |      21% |     10% |
| Reporting rate        |      17% |     41% |
| Credential submission |       5% |      1% |

This provides stronger evidence of behavioral improvement than simply reporting that employees completed training.

## Measuring Security Reporting Behavior

Security reporting is particularly valuable because employees can become an additional detection layer.

Organizations can measure:

* Number of reports.
* Reporting rate.
* Time to report.
* Quality of reports.
* Percentage of legitimate threats identified.
* False-positive rate.

For example:

> Employees reported 1,200 suspicious messages during the quarter, including 85 confirmed malicious messages.

This provides evidence that employees are contributing to organizational threat detection.

## Measuring Verification Behavior

Employees should be trained to verify unusual or high-risk requests.

For example, an employee receives:

> "Please urgently transfer this payment to the new bank account."

The desired behavior is not simply to recognize that the email looks suspicious.

The employee should:

* Follow the approved verification process.
* Contact the requester using a trusted communication channel.
* Avoid relying solely on information contained in the suspicious message.
* Report the event if appropriate.

Organizations can measure this behavior through simulations and controlled exercises.

## Measuring Data Handling Behavior

Behavioral measurement should extend beyond phishing.

Organizations may evaluate whether employees:

* Store sensitive information in approved locations.
* Share information using approved platforms.
* Avoid sending sensitive information to unauthorized recipients.
* Properly classify information.
* Follow retention requirements.
* Secure physical documents.

For example, an organization could conduct an authorized data-handling exercise to determine whether employees correctly identify where confidential documents should be stored.

## Measuring Physical Security Behavior

Physical security awareness can also be measured.

Examples include observing whether employees:

* Follow clean-desk requirements.
* Prevent unauthorized access.
* Challenge suspicious individuals.
* Properly display identification badges.
* Secure sensitive documents.
* Dispose of confidential information correctly.

Organizations should ensure that such exercises are properly authorized and conducted in accordance with workplace policies.

## Measuring Incident Reporting Behavior

Another important behavioral indicator is how employees respond when something goes wrong.

For example:

> An employee accidentally clicks a suspicious link.

A mature security behavior is:

**Recognize → Stop → Report → Follow instructions**

An immature behavior may be:

**Recognize → Ignore → Hope nothing happens**

Awareness programs should encourage employees to report mistakes quickly.

This is important because early reporting can allow security teams to contain an incident before it becomes more serious.

## Measuring Time to Report

Speed can be as important as reporting itself.

For example:

**Employee A**

Suspicious email received → reported after 5 minutes.

**Employee B**

Suspicious email received → reported after 3 hours.

Both employees reported the message, but the first behavior provides security teams with a much greater opportunity to respond quickly.

Time-to-report can therefore be a useful behavioral metric.

## Measuring Repeat Behavior

One of the strongest indicators of behavioral change is whether risky behavior decreases over repeated measurements.

For example:

> Campaign 1: Employee interacts with simulation.

> Campaign 2: Employee reports simulation.

> Campaign 3: Employee reports simulation.

This provides stronger evidence of learning than simply passing a training assessment.

Organizations can therefore monitor:

* First-time failures.
* Repeat failures.
* Improvement after training.
* Persistent risky behavior.

## Measuring Behavior Across Time

Behavioral change should not be measured only immediately after training.

Employees may perform well shortly after completing a course but gradually forget the material.

For example:

**Immediately after training**

Phishing reporting: 45%

**Three months later**

Phishing reporting: 39%

**Six months later**

Phishing reporting: 27%

This may indicate that reinforcement is needed.

Organizations can use periodic simulations, microlearning, reminders, and campaigns to maintain awareness.

## Sustained Behavioral Change

A mature program should distinguish between:

**Temporary improvement**

and

**Sustained improvement**

For example:

> Training completed → behavior improves for one month → behavior returns to previous level.

This represents temporary improvement.

A stronger outcome would be:

> Training → improvement → continued improvement over multiple measurement periods.

Sustained behavior provides stronger evidence of program effectiveness.

## Behavioral Change and High-Risk Groups

Organizations should pay particular attention to roles with greater exposure to cybersecurity threats.

Examples include:

* Executives.
* Finance employees.
* Privileged IT administrators.
* Security personnel.
* Developers.
* HR personnel.
* Employees handling sensitive information.

For example, finance employees may be more exposed to payment fraud, while administrators may face credential theft and privileged-access attacks.

Behavioral measurements should therefore be risk-based.

## Positive Security Behaviors

Organizations should not measure only failures.

Positive behaviors should also be recognized.

Examples include:

* Reporting suspicious messages.
* Verifying unusual requests.
* Reporting lost devices.
* Following secure data-handling procedures.
* Asking security teams for clarification.
* Identifying suspicious physical activity.

A strong security culture encourages employees to actively participate in protecting the organization.

## Security Champions

Some organizations establish **security champion programs** where employees in business departments help promote secure behavior.

Behavioral measurements may include:

* Security champion participation.
* Awareness activity participation.
* Number of security issues identified.
* Department reporting activity.
* Completion of security initiatives.

Security champions can help bridge the gap between centralized security teams and business employees.

## Surveys and Self-Reported Behavior

Surveys can provide useful information about employee perceptions and self-reported behavior.

For example:

> "I know how to report a suspicious email."

Employees might respond on a scale from strongly disagree to strongly agree.

However, self-reported behavior has limitations.

An employee may believe they behave securely without actually doing so.

Therefore:

> **Self-reported behavior should ideally be combined with objective behavioral measurements.**

For example:

**Survey:** 90% say they know how to report phishing.

**Simulation:** Only 42% report simulated phishing.

The difference may indicate that employees understand the concept but do not consistently apply it.

## Behavioral Observation

In some environments, organizations may use authorized observation or controlled exercises to evaluate behavior.

Examples include:

* Clean-desk inspections.
* Physical security assessments.
* Controlled social engineering exercises.
* Data-handling reviews.
* Access-control assessments.

These activities should be governed by clear rules and conducted ethically.

The objective should be improvement rather than employee embarrassment.

## Measuring Behavior Through Real Incidents

Real security incidents can provide valuable behavioral information.

Organizations may analyze:

* How the incident started.
* Whether an employee recognized the problem.
* How quickly it was reported.
* Whether procedures were followed.
* Whether the employee received relevant training.
* Whether similar incidents occurred previously.

For example:

> An employee accidentally sends sensitive information to the wrong recipient.

The organization could determine:

* Why the mistake occurred.
* Whether the employee understood the data-handling policy.
* Whether the system provided adequate safeguards.
* Whether additional training is appropriate.

This helps distinguish between **individual behavior issues and systemic process weaknesses**.

## Do Not Blame Employees Automatically

A critical principle in behavioral analysis is that human error does not always mean employee negligence.

Suppose an employee repeatedly sends information to the wrong recipient.

Possible causes might include:

* Poorly designed software.
* Auto-complete behavior.
* Confusing procedures.
* Inadequate training.
* Excessive workload.
* Unclear data classification.
* Lack of technical controls.

Therefore, organizations should ask:

> **Why did the behavior occur?**

rather than simply:

> **Who made the mistake?**

This supports a stronger security culture.

## Human Factors

Behavior is influenced by the environment in which employees work.

Factors include:

* Workload.
* Time pressure.
* Usability.
* Organizational processes.
* Management expectations.
* Technology design.
* Training quality.
* Security culture.

For example, employees may bypass security procedures because the approved process is significantly more difficult than the insecure alternative.

In such cases, improving the process may be more effective than providing additional training.

## Behavioral Change and Security Culture

Behavioral measurement can provide evidence of whether a security culture is developing.

A strong culture may be reflected by employees who:

* Report suspicious activity voluntarily.
* Ask questions about security.
* Verify unusual requests.
* Take responsibility for protecting information.
* Help colleagues recognize threats.
* Treat security as part of their normal responsibilities.

This is more meaningful than simply completing annual training.

## GRC Perspective

From a GRC perspective, behavioral measurement helps determine whether security controls are operating effectively.

The relationship can be represented as:

**Requirement → Expected Behavior → Measurement → Control Effectiveness → Risk Decision**

For example:

**Requirement**

Employees must report suspected security incidents.

**Expected Behavior**

Employees report suspicious activity promptly.

**Measurement**

Average time to report is 12 minutes.

**Control Effectiveness**

Employee reporting behavior is improving.

**Risk Decision**

Continue current awareness controls while focusing additional training on high-risk groups.

This creates evidence that awareness activities are contributing to control effectiveness.

## Behavioral Change Maturity

Organizations can think about behavioral measurement as a progression.

### Level 1 – No Measurement

The organization provides training but does not measure behavior.

### Level 2 – Basic Measurement

The organization measures completion and assessment scores.

### Level 3 – Behavioral Measurement

The organization measures phishing, reporting, and other observable behaviors.

### Level 4 – Risk-Based Measurement

Behavioral metrics are connected to organizational risk.

### Level 5 – Continuous Human Risk Management

The organization continuously monitors relevant behaviors, adapts training, improves controls, and reassesses risk.

The objective of a mature program is to move beyond training completion toward measurable and sustained behavioral improvement.

## Example Behavioral Measurement Framework

An organization could establish the following framework:

| Desired Behavior         | Measurement              |           Target |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------ | ---------------: |
| Report phishing          | Reporting rate           |             >40% |
| Avoid suspicious links   | Simulation click rate    |             <10% |
| Report incidents quickly | Average reporting time   |          <30 min |
| Avoid repeat mistakes    | Repeat failure rate      |              <5% |
| Protect sensitive data   | Data-handling violations | Decreasing trend |

These measurements can then be reviewed periodically.

## Common Mistakes

Organizations should avoid:

### Measuring Knowledge Instead of Behavior

A high test score does not necessarily mean secure behavior.

### Measuring Only Failures

Positive security behaviors should also be measured.

### Ignoring Long-Term Trends

Short-term improvements may not represent sustained change.

### Blaming Employees

Human behavior should be analyzed within the broader organizational context.

### Ignoring Technical Controls

Some insecure behaviors are caused by poor systems or processes rather than lack of awareness.

### Measuring Everything

Focus on behaviors that are relevant to organizational risk.

### Failing to Act

Behavioral measurement should result in targeted improvements.

## Key Takeaways

Behavioral change measurement is essential because security awareness ultimately exists to influence what employees **actually do**.

The key principles are:

1. **Measure behavior, not just training completion.**
2. **Define the desired security behavior clearly.**
3. **Establish a baseline before measuring improvement.**
4. **Use simulations and real-world data where appropriate.**
5. **Measure both positive and negative behaviors.**
6. **Track repeat behavior and long-term trends.**
7. **Measure reporting and response speed.**
8. **Focus on high-risk roles and behaviors.**
9. **Consider human and organizational factors behind mistakes.**
10. **Do not automatically treat human error as employee negligence.**
11. **Connect behavioral measurements to cybersecurity risk.**
12. **Use the results to improve training, processes, and technical controls.**

The most mature security awareness programs do not simply ask:

> **"Did employees complete the training?"**

They ask:

> **"Are employees behaving more securely, and is that behavioral improvement reducing organizational risk?"**

That is the fundamental difference between measuring **security awareness activity** and measuring **security awareness effectiveness**.

## Executive Reporting and Dashboards

Security awareness programs generate many different types of information, but executives and senior management generally do not need to see every operational detail.

The purpose of executive reporting is to translate security awareness data into a **clear picture of performance, risk, trends, and required management action**.

A strong executive report should answer four fundamental questions:

> **Where are we now?**

> **Are we improving?**

> **What risks remain?**

> **What action is required?**

### Why Executive Reporting Matters

Security awareness is sometimes viewed as an employee training activity rather than a cybersecurity control.

Effective executive reporting helps demonstrate that security awareness contributes directly to organizational risk management.

For example, instead of reporting:

> "2,500 employees completed security awareness training."

a stronger executive message would be:

> "Training completion reached 97%, phishing reporting increased from 24% to 41%, and simulated credential submission decreased from 4.5% to 1.8%."

The second example provides management with information about **security behavior and risk**, not simply activity.

## From Operational Data to Executive Information

Security awareness teams may collect hundreds of data points.

Examples include:

* Training completion.
* Assessment scores.
* Phishing clicks.
* Reporting rates.
* Time to report.
* Repeat failures.
* Training remediation.
* Security incidents.
* Department performance.
* Campaign results.

Executives generally need these details summarized into a smaller number of meaningful indicators.

The process can be represented as:

**Raw Data → Metrics → KPIs/KRIs → Trends → Risk Interpretation → Executive Decision**

This is an important GRC capability.

## Executive Dashboard

A security awareness dashboard should provide a concise overview of the organization's current position.

A typical dashboard might include:

### Program Performance

* Training completion.
* Assessment performance.
* Awareness participation.

### Behavioral Performance

* Phishing click rate.
* Phishing reporting rate.
* Time to report.

### Human Risk

* Repeat failures.
* High-risk employee behavior.
* Human-related security incidents.

### Trends

* Current result.
* Previous result.
* Target.
* Direction of change.

### Actions

* Open remediation items.
* Responsible owners.
* Target completion dates.

## Example Executive Dashboard

| Indicator             | Current | Target | Previous | Status    |
| --------------------- | ------: | -----: | -------: | --------- |
| Training completion   |     97% |   ≥95% |      94% | Improving |
| Assessment score      |     91% |   ≥85% |      89% | Improving |
| Phishing reporting    |     41% |   ≥40% |      32% | On Target |
| Phishing click rate   |      9% |   <10% |      14% | Improving |
| Credential submission |    1.8% |    <1% |     3.5% | Attention |
| Repeat failures       |      6% |    <5% |       9% | Improving |

This gives management an immediate understanding of program performance.

## Use Trends Instead of Isolated Numbers

A single number often lacks context.

For example:

> Phishing click rate: 9%

Management may ask:

> "Is 9% good?"

The answer depends on historical performance and campaign difficulty.

If previous results were:

* 25%.
* 19%.
* 14%.
* 9%.

then the organization is clearly showing an improving trend.

Dashboards should therefore show:

**Current → Previous → Target → Trend**

rather than only the current value.

## Traffic-Light Reporting

A common executive reporting technique is to use status categories such as:

* **Green:** Within target or acceptable risk.
* **Amber:** Requires monitoring or corrective action.
* **Red:** Significant concern or risk exceeding tolerance.

For example:

| Metric                   | Result | Status |
| ------------------------ | -----: | ------ |
| Training completion      |    97% | Green  |
| Phishing reporting       |    41% | Green  |
| Credential submission    |   1.8% | Amber  |
| Repeat phishing failures |    11% | Red    |

The exact thresholds should be established according to organizational objectives and risk tolerance.

## Avoiding Misleading Dashboards

Dashboards can become misleading if metrics are presented without context.

For example:

> **98% training completion**

may appear excellent.

But if:

> **Credential submission during phishing simulations = 8%**

the organization may still have significant human-related risk.

Therefore, dashboards should present both **program performance and risk exposure**.

## KPI and KRI Dashboard Structure

A useful executive dashboard can separate:

### KPIs – Program Performance

* Training completion.
* Assessment performance.
* Campaign participation.
* Reporting rate.
* Remediation completion.

### KRIs – Human Risk

* Phishing interaction.
* Credential submission.
* Repeat failures.
* Human-error incidents.
* High-risk role exposure.

This makes it easier for management to distinguish:

> **"Is the program working?"**

from:

> **"What risk remains?"**

## Reporting to Different Audiences

Not everyone needs the same level of information.

### Board of Directors

The board generally needs:

* Major human-related risks.
* Risk trends.
* Significant incidents.
* Overall program maturity.
* Management actions.
* Resource or investment decisions.

### Executive Management

Executives may need:

* KPIs.
* KRIs.
* Department-level trends.
* Significant weaknesses.
* Corrective actions.
* Resource requirements.

### CISO

The CISO may require:

* Detailed behavioral trends.
* Risk analysis.
* Security awareness effectiveness.
* Incident correlations.
* High-risk groups.
* Control improvement recommendations.

### Security Awareness Team

The operational team may need:

* Individual campaign results.
* Employee-level training status.
* Simulation details.
* Scenario performance.
* Remediation tracking.
* Detailed analytics.

The principle is:

> **The higher the audience, the more the reporting should focus on risk, trends, decisions, and outcomes.**

## Board-Level Reporting

Board reporting should not become a training statistics presentation.

A board member is more likely to care about:

> "What human-related cybersecurity risk could materially affect the organization?"

rather than:

> "How many employees completed the awareness course?"

A board-level report might state:

> "Human-related phishing risk remains moderate. Employee reporting increased by 18 percentage points over the last six months, while simulated credential submission decreased by 55%. Finance and privileged IT roles remain the highest-risk groups and are subject to targeted awareness activities."

This provides strategic information without unnecessary operational detail.

## Reporting Risk Trends

Executives should be able to see whether risk is:

* Increasing.
* Decreasing.
* Stable.
* Uncertain.

For example:

**Phishing reporting**

↗ Increasing

**Credential submission**

↘ Decreasing

**Repeat failures**

↘ Decreasing

**Human-related incidents**

→ Stable

This makes the dashboard more useful for decision-making.

## Explaining Significant Changes

When a metric changes significantly, the report should explain why.

For example:

> **Phishing click rate increased from 8% to 13%.**

Possible explanation:

> "The latest campaign used a more sophisticated business-email-compromise scenario targeting finance personnel."

Without this context, management might incorrectly conclude that the awareness program has deteriorated.

A dashboard should therefore provide context for significant changes.

## Reporting Against Targets

Management should be able to compare actual performance against established objectives.

For example:

| Objective             | Target | Actual | Result       |
| --------------------- | -----: | -----: | ------------ |
| Training completion   |    95% |    97% | Achieved     |
| Phishing reporting    |    40% |    41% | Achieved     |
| Credential submission |    <1% |   1.8% | Not Achieved |
| Repeat failures       |    <5% |     6% | Near Target  |

This makes reporting objective and measurable.

## Reporting Exceptions

Executives generally do not need every normal operational result.

Instead, reports should highlight significant exceptions.

Examples include:

* A KRI exceeding its threshold.
* A significant increase in human-related incidents.
* Poor performance in a critical department.
* Repeated failures among privileged users.
* Significant training non-compliance.
* Failure to complete remediation actions.

This keeps executive reporting focused.

## Management Actions

A good dashboard should not simply show problems.

It should identify what is being done about them.

For example:

> **Issue:** High credential submission among finance employees.

> **Risk:** Increased exposure to business email compromise.

> **Action:** Targeted finance phishing training and additional simulations.

> **Owner:** Security Awareness Manager.

> **Due Date:** Q3.

This creates accountability.

## Action Tracking

Security awareness findings can be tracked using a corrective-action register.

| Finding                           | Risk   | Action                    | Owner              | Due Date | Status      |
| --------------------------------- | ------ | ------------------------- | ------------------ | -------- | ----------- |
| High finance phishing interaction | High   | Targeted training         | Security Awareness | Q3       | In Progress |
| Low reporting rate                | Medium | Improve reporting process | SOC                | Q3       | Completed   |
| Repeat failures                   | Medium | Additional simulations    | HR/Security        | Q4       | Planned     |

This allows management to monitor whether identified weaknesses are actually being addressed.

## Dashboard Data Quality

Executive reporting is only as good as the underlying data.

Organizations should ensure that:

* Metrics are consistently defined.
* Data sources are reliable.
* Calculations are accurate.
* Measurement periods are clear.
* Historical results are retained.
* Changes in methodology are documented.

For example, if the definition of "phishing report" changes between campaigns, trend comparisons may become unreliable.

## Metric Definitions

A mature awareness program should maintain a metric dictionary.

For example:

**Phishing Reporting Rate**

> Percentage of employees who report a simulated phishing message using the approved reporting mechanism.

**Phishing Click Rate**

> Percentage of recipients who interact with the simulated phishing link.

**Credential Submission Rate**

> Percentage of recipients who attempt to submit information on the simulated landing page.

Clear definitions prevent different teams from interpreting the same metric differently.

## Data Segmentation

Executive dashboards may provide aggregated results, while operational dashboards can provide more detailed segmentation.

Possible segmentation includes:

* Business unit.
* Department.
* Geographic region.
* Job role.
* Risk level.
* Employee population.

For example:

> Overall phishing reporting: 41%

But:

> Finance: 28%

> IT: 54%

> HR: 46%

This may identify a specific area requiring additional attention.

## Protecting Employee Privacy

Security awareness reporting can involve employee-related information.

Organizations should carefully consider:

* Data minimization.
* Access controls.
* Retention.
* Appropriate aggregation.
* Purpose limitation.
* Confidentiality.

Executive dashboards should generally focus on **aggregated trends and risk**, rather than exposing individual employee performance unnecessarily.

For example, it is usually more appropriate to report:

> "7% of employees in Finance repeatedly failed simulations."

than to provide a board-level list of individual employees.

Individual-level data may be appropriate for authorized operational remediation, subject to applicable policies and legal requirements.

## Comparing Departments Carefully

Department comparisons can be useful but can also be misleading.

For example:

> Finance click rate: 15%

> IT click rate: 5%

This does not automatically mean Finance has a poor security culture.

Finance may have received a significantly more difficult simulation because of its exposure to payment fraud.

The report should therefore provide context around:

* Scenario difficulty.
* Risk exposure.
* Employee population.
* Previous performance.
* Training received.

## Dashboard Automation

Organizations may automate dashboard generation using:

* Learning Management Systems.
* Phishing simulation platforms.
* GRC platforms.
* Security analytics platforms.
* Business intelligence tools.

Automation can reduce manual reporting effort and improve consistency.

However, automated dashboards should still be reviewed by security professionals.

Automation can display a metric accurately while still presenting the wrong interpretation.

## Integrating Awareness Data With GRC

Security awareness dashboards become particularly valuable when integrated into the broader GRC environment.

For example:

**Awareness Platform**

→ Phishing results

**GRC Platform**

→ Risk records

**Incident Management**

→ Security incidents

**Compliance**

→ Training requirements

**Executive Dashboard**

→ Consolidated risk view

This allows management to understand how employee behavior relates to broader cybersecurity risk.

## Executive Reporting Frequency

Reporting frequency should depend on the importance and volatility of the information.

Possible frequencies include:

**Monthly**

Operational awareness metrics.

**Quarterly**

Executive KPIs, KRIs, trends, and corrective actions.

**Annually**

Program effectiveness, maturity, strategic objectives, and budget.

**Immediately**

Significant risk changes or major human-related security incidents.

Not every metric needs to be reported at the same frequency.

Credential submission remains above the organization's target at 1.8%, with Finance and privileged IT users representing the highest-risk groups. * Trend.
* High-risk areas.
* Next actions.

## GRC Perspective

Executive reporting is an important part of the governance function.

From a GRC perspective, the reporting process should connect:

**Security Objectives**

↓

**KPIs and KRIs**

↓

**Risk Assessment**

↓

**Management Reporting**

↓

**Management Decisions**

↓

**Corrective Actions**

↓

**Continual Improvement**

This ensures that security awareness information reaches the people responsible for managing organizational risk.

## Common Reporting Mistakes

Organizations should avoid:

### Reporting Too Much Data

Executives may lose sight of the most important risks.

### Reporting Only Training Completion

Completion does not demonstrate behavioral improvement.

### Reporting Numbers Without Context

A metric needs a baseline, target, or trend.

### Ignoring Risk

Program performance should be connected to human-related risk.

### Showing Individual Employees Unnecessarily

Executive reporting should generally use appropriate aggregation.

### Not Explaining Significant Changes

A sudden change needs context.

### Failing to Track Actions

A dashboard should show what is being done about identified weaknesses.

### Using Unreliable Data

Poor data quality can result in poor management decisions.

## Key Takeaways

Executive reporting and dashboards transform security awareness data into information that management can use to make decisions.

The key principles are:

1. **Focus executive reporting on risk, trends, and outcomes.**
2. **Use both KPIs and KRIs.**
3. **Show current results against targets and previous periods.**
4. **Explain significant changes and anomalies.**
5. **Highlight high-risk groups and important exceptions.**
6. **Track corrective actions and accountability.**
7. **Use appropriate aggregation to protect employee privacy.**
8. **Maintain consistent metric definitions.**
9. **Tailor reporting to the audience.**
10. **Integrate awareness reporting with broader GRC and risk management.**
11. **Automate data collection where appropriate.**
12. **Use dashboards to support management decisions rather than simply display statistics.**

The ultimate purpose of executive reporting is to transform security awareness from an **employee training activity into a measurable cybersecurity risk-management capability**.

A mature dashboard should allow leadership to quickly understand:

> **How effective is our program?**

> **Where is human-related risk highest?**

> **Is the risk improving or deteriorating?**

> **What actions should management take?**

## Introduction

Successfully containing and recovering from a cybersecurity incident does not mark the end of the incident management lifecycle. Every incident, whether minor or major, presents an opportunity to strengthen an organization's cybersecurity capabilities. **Post-Incident Reviews (PIRs)** are structured evaluations conducted after an incident has been resolved to determine what happened, why it happened, how effectively the organization responded, and what improvements should be implemented to reduce the likelihood and impact of future incidents.

A Post-Incident Review focuses on learning rather than assigning blame. The objective is to identify strengths, weaknesses, process gaps, technical issues, communication challenges, and opportunities for improvement across people, processes, and technology. By analyzing the entire incident lifecycle—from detection and analysis through containment, eradication, recovery, and communication—organizations can improve incident response procedures, security controls, operational resilience, and governance practices.

Effective post-incident reviews involve multiple stakeholders, including the Security Operations Center (SOC), Computer Security Incident Response Team (CSIRT), IT Operations, Business Continuity, Disaster Recovery, Legal, Compliance, Risk Management, Human Resources, Communications, and business units affected by the incident. Cross-functional participation ensures that both technical and business perspectives are considered when developing corrective actions.

International standards and frameworks such as **ISO/IEC 27001**, **ISO/IEC 27035**, **NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF)**, **NIST SP 800-61 Revision 2**, **ISO 22301 Business Continuity Management Systems**, and the **Center for Internet Security (CIS) Controls** emphasize the importance of lessons learned and continual improvement following cybersecurity incidents. Within Governance, Risk, and Compliance (GRC), post-incident reviews provide valuable evidence for governance oversight, enterprise risk management, regulatory compliance, and continual improvement.

# What is a Post-Incident Review?

A **Post-Incident Review (PIR)** is a structured assessment performed after an incident has been resolved to evaluate the effectiveness of the organization's response and identify opportunities for improvement.

The primary objectives are to:

- Understand what happened.
- Identify the root cause.
- Evaluate response effectiveness.
- Improve security controls.
- Strengthen communication.
- Enhance operational resilience.
- Capture lessons learned.
- Prevent similar incidents.

The review should focus on continuous improvement rather than assigning blame.

---

# Objectives of a Post-Incident Review

An effective review seeks to answer several important questions.

These include:

- How was the incident detected?
- Was the response timely?
- Were escalation procedures effective?
- Were roles and responsibilities clear?
- Were communication processes effective?
- Which controls worked well?
- Which controls failed?
- What improvements are required?

Answering these questions provides valuable insight into organizational performance.

---

Participants may include:

- Legal and Compliance.
- Risk Management.
- Business unit representatives.

Cross-functional participation provides a comprehensive understanding of the incident.

# Activities Performed During the Review

Typical review activities include:

- Assessing communication effectiveness.

- Identifying process gaps.
- Defining corrective actions.
- Assigning improvement responsibilities.

Each activity contributes to strengthening future incident response.

# Outputs of a Post-Incident Review

Continual improvement depends on consistently applying lessons learned.

# Practical Example

A global healthcare organization successfully contains a phishing attack that resulted in the compromise of several employee accounts. After completing recovery activities, the organization conducts a Post-Incident Review involving representatives from the Security Operations Center (SOC), Incident Response Team, IT Operations, Human Resources, Legal, Compliance, and affected business units. The review identifies that although the technical response was timely, delays occurred because employees were uncertain about the reporting process. The review also reveals that multi-factor authentication had not yet been deployed to several legacy systems. Based on these findings, the organization updates its incident response procedures, accelerates the deployment of multi-factor authentication, enhances employee security awareness training, and revises its incident reporting process. Progress on these corrective actions is tracked through the organization's governance program and reviewed by executive leadership.

This example demonstrates how a structured Post-Incident Review transforms operational experience into measurable improvements that strengthen cybersecurity capabilities and organizational resilience.

# Root Cause Documentation

Identifying the root cause of a cybersecurity incident is only part of the learning process. To ensure that lessons learned lead to meaningful improvements, organizations must formally document the underlying causes of incidents, the contributing factors, the corrective actions taken, and the preventive measures required to reduce the likelihood of recurrence. **Root Cause Documentation** provides a structured record of these findings and serves as a critical input for governance, risk management, compliance activities, audits, and continual improvement initiatives.

Root Cause Documentation transforms technical investigations into organizational knowledge. It captures not only the technical vulnerabilities or attack methods that enabled an incident but also the procedural, operational, organizational, and human factors that contributed to the event. Effective documentation helps security teams recognize recurring patterns, evaluate the effectiveness of existing controls, justify security investments, improve incident response procedures, and strengthen organizational resilience.

The documentation process should be objective, evidence-based, and focused on improvement rather than assigning blame. A well-prepared root cause report enables technical teams, executive leadership, auditors, regulators, and business stakeholders to understand why an incident occurred and how similar incidents can be prevented in the future.

International standards and frameworks such as **ISO/IEC 27001**, **ISO/IEC 27035**, **NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF)**, **NIST SP 800-61 Revision 2**, **ISO 22301 Business Continuity Management Systems**, and the **Center for Internet Security (CIS) Controls** emphasize documenting incident investigations, corrective actions, and continual improvement. Within Governance, Risk, and Compliance (GRC), root cause documentation provides evidence of effective governance, enterprise risk management, and regulatory compliance.

# Practical Example

A multinational software company experiences a phishing attack that leads to unauthorized access to several employee accounts. During the investigation, analysts determine that the primary root cause was the absence of multi-factor authentication (MFA) on a legacy application, while contributing factors included outdated user awareness training and delayed patch management. The incident response team documents the incident timeline, technical evidence, affected systems, business impact, root cause analysis, and recommended corrective actions. The organization assigns responsibility for implementing MFA, updating security awareness training, improving vulnerability management procedures, and enhancing email security controls. Progress is monitored through the organization's governance process until all corrective actions are completed and verified.

This example demonstrates how comprehensive root cause documentation converts incident findings into actionable improvements that reduce future cyber risk and strengthen organizational resilience.

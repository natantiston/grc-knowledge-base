# 19.13 GRC Audit and Assurance Case Studies

## Part 1 – Planning a GRC Internal Audit

A GRC internal audit provides independent and systematic assurance that governance, risk-management, and control processes are:

* Properly designed
* Implemented
* Operating effectively
* Aligned with organizational objectives
* Compliant with applicable requirements
* Producing reliable evidence

The purpose of an internal GRC audit is not simply to find mistakes.

Its purpose is to answer:

> **"Can management reasonably rely on the organization's governance, risk, and control environment?"**

The audit lifecycle is:

**Audit Universe → Risk Assessment → Audit Planning → Scope → Objectives → Criteria → Resources → Audit Program → Evidence Plan → Fieldwork → Findings → Reporting → Follow-up**

---

# Case Study: GlobalConnect Telecom

GlobalConnect operates telecommunications, cloud, digital, and customer-facing services across several countries.

Its GRC environment includes:

* ISO 27001
* NIST CSF
* Enterprise Risk Management
* Third-Party Risk Management
* Privacy
* Business Continuity
* Regulatory compliance
* Cybersecurity governance
* Internal controls

The Internal Audit function has selected:

> **Cybersecurity Governance and Risk Management**

as a high-priority audit area for 2026.

You are the **GRC Audit Manager** responsible for planning the audit.

---

# 1. Why Audit Planning Matters

A poorly planned audit can result in:

* Excessive scope
* Insufficient evidence
* Inadequate resources
* Missed high-risk areas
* Weak findings
* Delayed reporting
* Management disagreement

A well-planned audit ensures that audit effort is concentrated where it creates the greatest assurance value.

---

# 2. Audit vs Assessment vs Compliance Review

These activities are related but different.

### Internal Audit

Provides independent assurance.

### Risk Assessment

Identifies and evaluates risks.

### Compliance Assessment

Determines whether requirements are being met.

### Control Assessment

Evaluates whether controls are designed and operating effectively.

### Consulting

Provides advice or recommendations.

The auditor must preserve sufficient independence when performing assurance work.

# 4. Step 2 – Perform Audit Risk Assessment

Internal Audit evaluates each area based on factors such as:

* Business impact
* Regulatory exposure
* Cybersecurity risk
* Financial impact
* Prior findings
* Control maturity
* Organizational change
* Management concerns
* Incident history
* Time since last audit

| Audit Area               | Risk      |
| ------------------------ | --------- |
| Cybersecurity Governance | Very High |
| Third-Party Risk         | Very High |
| Privacy                  | High      |
| Business Continuity      | High      |
| GRC Technology           | Medium    |

Cybersecurity Governance becomes a priority.

# 5. Step 3 – Determine Audit Priority

Suppose GlobalConnect recently:

* Implemented a new cybersecurity operating model.
* Migrated critical services to cloud.
* Expanded its third-party ecosystem.
* Received new regulatory requirements.
* Experienced several high-risk audit findings.

These changes increase audit priority.

Therefore:

> **Cybersecurity Governance and Risk Management**

is selected for the annual audit plan.

---

# 6. Step 4 – Define the Audit Objective

A good audit objective should be clear and testable.

Example:

> **To determine whether GlobalConnect has established and maintained an effective cybersecurity governance and risk-management framework that appropriately identifies, assesses, treats, monitors, and reports cybersecurity risk.**

This is better than:

> "Audit cybersecurity."

The objective defines what assurance the audit is expected to provide.

---

# 7. Step 5 – Define the Audit Scope

The scope determines what is included.

The audit will cover:

* Cybersecurity governance
* Cybersecurity risk management
* Risk appetite
* Risk register
* Security policies
* Security committees
* Risk reporting
* Control ownership
* Risk treatment
* Risk acceptance
* Cybersecurity metrics
* Management oversight

---

# 8. Scope Exclusions

The audit may exclude:

* Detailed penetration testing
* Source-code review
* Technical firewall testing
* Individual endpoint configuration
* Physical security

These may belong to separate audits.

Clearly defining exclusions prevents scope creep.

---

# 9. Scope Period

The audit covers:

**January 1 – December 31, 2026**

However, current conditions may also be considered if they materially affect the audit conclusion.

---

# 10. Organizational Scope

The audit includes:

* Corporate headquarters
* Regional business units
* Critical subsidiaries
* Central cybersecurity function
* GRC function
* Enterprise Risk Management

The auditor must determine whether all relevant entities are included.

---

# 11. Step 6 – Define Audit Criteria

Auditors need a basis for evaluation.

Potential criteria include:

### Internal

* Cybersecurity policies
* Risk-management policy
* Risk appetite
* Governance framework
* Committee charter
* Internal standards

### External

* ISO 27001
* NIST CSF
* COBIT
* Applicable laws and regulations

The criteria should be agreed before detailed testing begins.

---

# 12. Audit Criteria Example

The audit may test whether:

> Cybersecurity risks are identified and assessed according to the organization's approved risk-management methodology.

Evidence may include:

* Risk methodology
* Risk register
* Risk assessments
* Risk committee minutes
* Risk treatment plans

# 13. Step 7 – Develop the Audit Risk Model

The auditor identifies risks that could prevent the organization from achieving the audit objective.

Example:

### Risk 1

Cybersecurity risks are not identified.

### Risk 2

Risks are incorrectly assessed.

### Risk 3

Risk owners are not assigned.

### Risk 4

Risk treatment is incomplete.

### Risk 5

Risk acceptance exceeds authority.

### Risk 6

Management receives inaccurate risk reporting.

---

# 14. Control Objectives

Each risk should map to one or more control objectives.

Example:

**Risk:**

Cybersecurity risks are not properly identified.

**Control Objective:**

The organization maintains a structured process for identifying cybersecurity risks.

**Potential Control:**

Annual enterprise cybersecurity risk assessment.

# 16. Step 8 – Build the Audit Program

The audit program defines the tests auditors will perform.

Example:

| Audit Area      | Test                           |
| --------------- | ------------------------------ |
| Governance      | Review committee structure     |
| Risk Management | Test risk assessments          |
| Risk Register   | Sample cybersecurity risks     |
| Risk Treatment  | Review remediation plans       |
| Risk Acceptance | Test approval authority        |
| Reporting       | Validate management dashboards |
| Metrics         | Recalculate selected KRIs      |
| Policies        | Test policy review cycle       |

---

# 17. Step 9 – Determine Sampling

Internal Audit usually cannot test every transaction or control.

Sampling may be required.

Suppose GlobalConnect has:

**1,200 cybersecurity risks**

Internal Audit may select a representative sample.

For example:

**40 risks**

The exact sample size should be determined based on:

* Population
* Risk
* Control characteristics
* Audit methodology
* Desired assurance

---

# 18. Risk-Based Sampling

Not all samples should be random.

The auditor may deliberately include:

* Critical risks
* Recently created risks
* Overdue risks
* Accepted risks
* High residual-risk items
* Risks with previous findings

This provides greater risk coverage.

---

# 19. Step 10 – Identify Key Controls

The audit identifies controls such as:

### Governance

Cybersecurity Steering Committee.

### Risk

Quarterly cybersecurity risk assessment.

### Risk Acceptance

Formal approval workflow.

### Reporting

Monthly executive risk reporting.

### Monitoring

Key Risk Indicators.

### Treatment

Risk remediation tracking.

These become the audit's control population.

---

# 20. Step 11 – Evaluate Control Design

The first question is:

> **Is the control appropriately designed?**

Example:

Policy requires:

> "Cybersecurity risks must be reviewed quarterly."

But the process only reviews risks annually.

The control design may be inadequate.

---

# 21. Design Effectiveness

A control is design-effective when it is capable of addressing the relevant risk.

Example:

**Risk:**

Critical cybersecurity risks remain unidentified.

**Control:**

Quarterly enterprise risk assessment.

Potentially:

**Design Effective = Yes**

But this does not mean the control actually operates.

---

# 22. Step 12 – Test Operating Effectiveness

Now ask:

> **Did the control actually operate as designed?**

Suppose the policy requires:

**Quarterly risk review**

Audit examines four quarters.

Evidence:

* Q1: Completed
* Q2: Completed
* Q3: Not completed
* Q4: Completed

The control did not operate consistently.

---

# 23. Design vs Operating Effectiveness

This distinction is essential.

### Design Effectiveness

> Would the control work if properly performed?

### Operating Effectiveness

> Did the control actually operate as intended?

A control may be:

**Well designed but poorly operated.**

or:

**Poorly designed but consistently performed.**

Both situations matter.

---

# 24. Step 13 – Evidence Requirements

Auditors establish evidence requirements.

Examples:

* Approved policies
* Risk registers
* Risk assessments
* Committee minutes
* Meeting agendas
* Risk treatment plans
* Risk acceptance forms
* Dashboards
* GRC workflow records
* System reports
* Email approvals

Evidence should be:

**Sufficient**

**Reliable**

**Relevant**

**Traceable**

---

# 25. Evidence Quality

Weak evidence:

> "Management confirms that the review occurs."

Strong evidence:

> Approved quarterly risk review records showing date, participants, risks reviewed, decisions, and follow-up actions.

The second provides stronger audit assurance.

# 29. Step 17 – Test Risk Assessment

The methodology requires:

**Likelihood × Impact**

The sampled risk shows:

Likelihood:

**4**

Impact:

**5**

Expected score:

**20**

The GRC system shows:

**12**

This discrepancy requires investigation.

---

# 30. Audit Finding

The auditor determines:

> The risk score recorded in the GRC platform does not match the approved risk methodology.

Potential impact:

* Incorrect risk prioritization
* Incorrect management reporting
* Inappropriate treatment decisions

This may become an audit finding.

---

# 31. Step 18 – Test Risk Ownership

The methodology requires each material risk to have:

* Risk owner
* Control owner
* Treatment owner

Audit discovers:

**12 of 40 sampled risks**

have no formally assigned risk owner.

This represents a governance weakness.

---

# 32. Step 19 – Test Risk Acceptance

The policy states:

> High risks require executive approval.

Audit samples:

**15 accepted high-risk items**

and discovers:

**3**

were approved by managers without the required authority.

This is a potentially significant finding.

---

# 33. Step 20 – Test Risk Treatment

Audit reviews:

**30 high-risk items**

Results:

* 18 completed
* 7 in progress
* 3 overdue
* 2 without treatment plans

This indicates weaknesses in remediation governance.

---

# 34. Step 21 – Test Executive Reporting

The dashboard reports:

**12 critical risks**

Audit independently reconciles the GRC database and identifies:

**15 critical risks**

Difference:

**3 risks**

The auditor investigates whether:

* Dashboard filters are incorrect.
* Data integration failed.
* Risks were intentionally excluded.
* Reporting logic is inaccurate.

# 35. Data Integrity Is Also an Audit Concern

GRC reporting depends on data quality.

Auditors should consider:

* Completeness
* Accuracy
* Timeliness
* Consistency
* Traceability

A beautiful dashboard is not useful if its underlying data is unreliable.

# 36. Step 22 – Audit Workpapers

Auditors document:

* Objective
* Scope
* Criteria
* Risk
* Control
* Sample
* Evidence
* Test performed
* Results
* Exceptions

Workpapers should allow another qualified auditor to understand:

> **What was tested, how it was tested, what evidence was reviewed, and why the conclusion was reached.**

# 37. Step 23 – Preliminary Findings

Potential findings include:

### Finding 1

Risk ownership is incomplete.

### Finding 2

Risk scoring does not consistently follow methodology.

### Finding 3

Risk acceptance exceeds delegated authority.

### Finding 4

Risk treatment tracking contains overdue items.

### Finding 5

Executive reporting contains data inconsistencies.

These findings should be validated with management.

---

# 38. Step 24 – Validate Findings

Before issuing a finding, auditors discuss it with the control owner.

Questions include:

* Is the observation accurate?
* Is there additional evidence?
* Is there a legitimate exception?
* Is the control currently being redesigned?
* Is the issue already being remediated?

This prevents factual errors.

---

# 39. Step 25 – Root Cause Analysis

A strong audit finding identifies more than the symptom.

Example:

### Observation

Risk acceptance exceeded approval authority.

### Root Cause

The GRC workflow does not enforce approval thresholds.

### Impact

High risks may be accepted without appropriate executive oversight.

### Recommendation

Implement automated approval routing based on risk rating.

This is much stronger than:

> "Managers need more training."

---

# 40. Step 26 – Determine Finding Severity

A simple model may classify findings as:

### Critical

Could cause severe enterprise impact.

### High

Significant control weakness.

### Medium

Moderate weakness.

### Low

Limited impact.

Severity should consider:

* Impact
* Likelihood
* Regulatory implications
* Control failure
* Exposure
* Existing compensating controls

# 44. Auditor Independence

Internal Audit should maintain sufficient independence.

For example, if an auditor previously designed GlobalConnect's cybersecurity risk-management framework, assigning that same person to provide independent assurance over the framework may create a self-review concern.

The principle is:

> **You should not independently assure controls you personally designed or operated without appropriate safeguards.**

---

# 45. Three Lines Model

The audit also considers the organization's Three Lines structure.

### First Line

Business and operational control owners.

### Second Line

GRC, Risk, Compliance.

### Third Line

Internal Audit.

Internal Audit provides independent assurance over the first and second lines.

---

# 46. Audit Planning Mistakes

### Mistake 1

Starting fieldwork without a defined objective.

### Mistake 2

Making the scope too broad.

### Mistake 3

Testing controls without understanding risks.

### Mistake 4

Relying exclusively on interviews.

### Mistake 5

Ignoring evidence quality.

### Mistake 6

Testing every control equally.

### Mistake 7

Failing to define sampling.

### Mistake 8

Not considering previous findings.

### Mistake 9

Ignoring organizational changes.

### Mistake 10

Allowing management to determine the audit conclusion.

# 50. Key GRC Lesson

The central lesson is:

> **A strong internal audit begins before the auditor tests a single control.**

Good planning establishes:

* Why the audit matters
* What risks are being examined
* What controls should address those risks
* What evidence will demonstrate effectiveness
* What level of assurance management requires

The most important principle is:

> **Audit the organization's most important risks—not simply the easiest controls to test.**

A mature GRC auditor therefore starts with:

**Risk**

and works toward:

**Control → Evidence → Testing → Finding → Assurance.**

That is the foundation for an effective **GRC internal audit program**.

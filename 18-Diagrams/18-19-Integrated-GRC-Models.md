# 18.19 Integrated GRC Models

## Part 1 – Enterprise GRC Operating Model

An **Enterprise GRC Operating Model** provides a structured view of how **governance, risk management, and compliance** operate together across an organization.

Rather than treating GRC as a collection of independent activities, the operating model shows how:

* governance establishes direction;
* risk management identifies and manages uncertainty;
* compliance translates obligations into organizational requirements;
* controls reduce risk;
* assurance evaluates whether controls and processes are working;
* information and reporting support management decisions.

A simplified enterprise model is:

```text
                    BOARD / EXECUTIVE MANAGEMENT
                              │
                              ↓
                     GOVERNANCE & OVERSIGHT
                              │
             ┌────────────────┼────────────────┐
             ↓                ↓                ↓
        GOVERNANCE          RISK          COMPLIANCE
             │                │                │
             └────────────────┼────────────────┘
                              ↓
                         GRC OPERATING
                            MODEL
                              │
              ┌───────────────┼───────────────┐
              ↓               ↓               ↓
           CONTROLS        ASSURANCE        REPORTING
              │               │               │
              └───────────────┼───────────────┘
                              ↓
                     BUSINESS PERFORMANCE
                              │
                              ↓
                       MANAGEMENT DECISION
```

The purpose is to establish a common structure for how GRC activities are governed, executed, measured, and continuously improved.

---

# 1. What Is an Enterprise GRC Operating Model?

An enterprise GRC operating model defines **how GRC responsibilities, processes, information, technology, controls, and decision-making mechanisms work together**.

It answers questions such as:

* Who owns risk?
* Who establishes governance?
* Who manages compliance?
* Who operates controls?
* Who provides independent assurance?
* How are risks escalated?
* How is compliance monitored?
* How is GRC information reported?
* What technology supports GRC activities?
* How are decisions made?

A simplified representation is:

```text
GRC Strategy
     ↓
GRC Governance
     ↓
GRC Processes
     ↓
GRC Roles
     ↓
GRC Controls
     ↓
GRC Data
     ↓
GRC Technology
     ↓
GRC Reporting
     ↓
Management Decisions
```

---

# 2. Why an Enterprise GRC Operating Model Matters

Without an operating model, GRC activities can become fragmented.

For example:

```text
Risk Team       → Risk Assessment
Compliance Team → Compliance Assessment
Security Team   → Security Controls
Audit Team      → Audit Testing
Legal Team      → Regulatory Review
Business Units  → Operational Controls
```

If these activities operate independently, the organization may experience:

* duplicate assessments;
* inconsistent risk definitions;
* duplicated controls;
* conflicting requirements;
* fragmented evidence;
* inconsistent reporting;
* unclear accountability.

An integrated operating model attempts to connect these activities.

# 45. Common GRC Operating Model Problems

### Fragmented Ownership

Different departments may assume someone else owns the risk.

### Duplicate Assessments

The same business unit may be assessed repeatedly by different functions.

### Inconsistent Risk Definitions

Different teams may use different scoring models.

### Weak Data Integration

Risk, compliance, control, and audit information may exist in separate systems.

### Poor Accountability

Control owners and risk owners may not be clearly defined.

### Excessive Manual Processes

Large volumes of spreadsheets and email-based workflows can create inefficiency.

### Reporting Without Decision Support

Dashboards may contain large amounts of information but provide little actionable insight.

### GRC Detached from Business

GRC may become a compliance exercise instead of supporting business objectives.

The **Enterprise GRC Operating Model** provides the organizational architecture for integrating governance, risk, compliance, controls, assurance, information, and decision-making. For a GRC professional, the model is particularly important because it provides the **bridge between organizational strategy and day-to-day control execution**. # 18.19 Integrated GRC Models

## Part 2 – Integrated Risk, Compliance and Control Model

An **Integrated Risk, Compliance and Control Model** connects three fundamental GRC components:

* **Risk** — what could prevent the organization from achieving its objectives;
* **Compliance** — what the organization is required or expected to do;
* **Controls** — what the organization implements to manage risks and satisfy requirements.

The central concept is that these should not operate as separate management systems.

A simplified model is:

```text
                    BUSINESS OBJECTIVES
                           │
                           ↓
                 ┌───────────────────┐
                 │       RISK        │
                 │ What can go wrong?│
                 └─────────┬─────────┘
                           │
                           ↓
                 ┌───────────────────┐
                 │    COMPLIANCE     │
                 │ What must we do?  │
                 └─────────┬─────────┘
                           │
                           ↓
                 ┌───────────────────┐
                 │     CONTROLS      │
                 │ How do we manage? │
                 └─────────┬─────────┘
                           │
                           ↓
                    CONTROL EVIDENCE
                           │
                           ↓
                    ASSURANCE & TESTING
                           │
                           ↓
                    RESIDUAL RISK
                           │
                           ↓
                  MANAGEMENT DECISION
```

The purpose is to create a common GRC structure in which requirements, risks, controls, evidence, and assurance can be connected and traced.

# 50. Common Problems in Risk, Compliance and Control Integration

### Risk and Compliance Operate Separately

Compliance may focus on requirements while risk teams focus on organizational uncertainty without connecting the two.

### Too Many Controls

Organizations may create one control for every individual framework requirement.

### No Control Ownership

Controls may exist in the GRC platform without a clearly accountable owner.

### Evidence Is Not Connected

Evidence may be stored separately from the control and requirement it supports.

### Control Failures Do Not Update Risk

Audit findings may be closed without reassessing the associated risk.

### Duplicate Assessments

Different teams may repeatedly request similar information.

### Weak Traceability

Management cannot easily determine which controls address which risks and requirements.

# 52. Key Principles of the Integrated Model

An effective integrated model should follow several principles.

### One Risk Language

Use consistent definitions and scoring.

### One Control Framework

Where practical, establish common controls across multiple frameworks.

### Clear Ownership

Assign accountable risk and control owners.

### Traceability

Maintain relationships between requirements, risks, controls, evidence, and findings.

### Risk-Based Prioritization

Prioritize treatment based on organizational risk.

### Evidence Reuse

Use the same valid evidence across relevant assessments where appropriate.

### Continuous Monitoring

Update risk and control information as conditions change.

### Decision Orientation

GRC information should support management decisions.

The **Integrated Risk, Compliance and Control Model** establishes a common architecture through which organizational objectives, risks, requirements, controls, evidence, assurance, and management decisions can be connected.

Its fundamental relationship is:

```text
Business Objective
       ↓
Risk
       ↓
Requirement
       ↓
Control Objective
       ↓
Control
       ↓
Evidence
       ↓
Testing
       ↓
Assurance
       ↓
Residual Risk
       ↓
Management Decision
       ↓
Continuous Improvement
```

The most important principle is that **risk, compliance, and controls should not be managed as three independent disciplines**. They are different perspectives of the same organizational management system.

Risk explains **what could go wrong**.
Compliance explains **what the organization must satisfy**.
Controls explain **how the organization manages those risks and requirements**.
Evidence demonstrates **what has actually been implemented and operated**.
Assurance determines **whether the control environment can be relied upon**.
Residual risk provides management with the basis for **acceptance, treatment, escalation, or further investment**.

A mature GRC environment therefore creates a continuous relationship:

```text
Risk ↔ Requirements ↔ Controls ↔ Evidence ↔ Assurance ↔ Residual Risk
```

This integration enables organizations to reduce duplication, improve traceability, strengthen accountability, rationalize controls, improve audit readiness, and provide management with a more accurate view of the organization's overall risk and compliance posture.

A **GRC Framework Crosswalk Diagram** visually connects requirements from multiple governance, risk, compliance, cybersecurity, privacy, and resilience frameworks to a common set of organizational control objectives and controls.

Organizations rarely operate under a single framework. An enterprise may need to manage several frameworks and regulatory requirements simultaneously, such as:

* ISO/IEC 27001
* NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF)
* COBIT
* ISO 22301
* ISO 42001
* GDPR
* NIS2
* DORA
* Industry-specific requirements
* Internal corporate policies

Without a crosswalk, each framework may be managed separately, creating duplicate controls, assessments, evidence requests, and testing activities.

The fundamental concept is:

```text
Multiple Frameworks
        |
        v

        |
        v

        |
        v

        |
        v

        |
        v

        |
        v

        |
        v

```

The purpose of the crosswalk is not to suggest that different frameworks are identical. Instead, it identifies **relationships, overlaps, similarities, and unique requirements** so that the organization can manage them through an integrated GRC architecture.

# 1. What Is a GRC Framework Crosswalk?

A framework crosswalk is a structured mapping between two or more frameworks.

It identifies:

* equivalent requirements;
* related requirements;
* common control objectives;
* shared controls;
* unique requirements;
* control gaps;
* evidence requirements;
* assurance requirements.

```text
          v

          |
          v

          |
          v

          |
          v

```

The organizational control becomes the practical implementation point.

# 44. Common Crosswalk Mistakes

### Treating Frameworks as Identical

Two frameworks may address similar topics but have different objectives or scope.

### Mapping Only at the Domain Level

High-level mapping may not provide sufficient evidence for detailed compliance assessment.

### Ignoring Unique Requirements

Some framework-specific obligations cannot be satisfied through common controls.

### Creating Too Many Controls

A new framework does not automatically require a completely new control set.

### Failing to Maintain the Crosswalk

Frameworks and regulations change continuously.

### No Ownership

Each important mapping should have appropriate ownership and governance.

### Treating the Crosswalk as a One-Time Exercise

A crosswalk should be maintained as a living GRC artifact.

```text
                            +------------------+
                                               |
                                               v
                                        FRAMEWORK CHANGE
```

The **GRC Framework Crosswalk Diagram** is a fundamental mechanism for integrating multiple governance, risk, compliance, cybersecurity, privacy, and resilience frameworks into one coherent control environment.

Its primary purpose is to answer five questions:

1. **What frameworks and requirements apply?**
2. **Which requirements address similar objectives?**
3. **Which common controls address those requirements?**
4. **What evidence demonstrates that those controls operate?**
5. **What gaps and residual risks remain?**

The resulting relationship is:

```text
Framework
    ↓
Requirement
    ↓
Crosswalk
    ↓
Control Objective
    ↓
Common Control
    ↓
Evidence
    ↓
Testing
    ↓
Assurance
    ↓
Compliance Status
    ↓
Residual Risk
    ↓
Management Decision
```

The most important principle is that a framework crosswalk **does not eliminate the individual obligations of each framework**. Instead, it identifies where controls, evidence, testing, and assurance activities can be shared while preserving the specific requirements of each framework.

A mature crosswalk therefore enables:

* **control rationalization**;
* **evidence reuse**;
* **assessment efficiency**;
* **regulatory change management**;
* **framework gap analysis**;
* **audit readiness**;
* **consistent control ownership**;
* **better GRC reporting**; and
* **risk-informed management decisions**.

Ultimately, the crosswalk transforms a collection of separate frameworks into a coordinated GRC architecture:

```text
Multiple Frameworks
        ↓
Integrated Requirements
        ↓
Common Control Objectives
        ↓
Common Controls
        ↓
Shared Evidence
        ↓
Integrated Assurance
        ↓
Enterprise Risk View
        ↓
Management Decision
```

This makes the **GRC Framework Crosswalk** an essential building block of an integrated enterprise GRC operating model.

```text id="x8q4nt"

    ↓

    ↓
```

# 48. Common Traceability Problems

### Fragmented Data

GRC information exists in disconnected systems.

### Missing Relationships

Requirements are not linked to controls.

### Unowned Controls

Controls exist without accountable owners.

### Weak Evidence Links

Evidence cannot be traced back to a specific control.

### Findings Without Risk Context

Audit findings are not connected to business or cybersecurity risks.

### Remediation Without Validation

Actions are marked complete without confirming effectiveness.

### Manual Reporting

Management reports are manually assembled from disconnected data.

### Outdated Mappings

Framework changes are not reflected in the traceability model.

The **End-to-End GRC Traceability Model** provides the connective structure that allows an organization to follow a GRC issue from its original business objective or regulatory requirement through risk identification, control implementation, evidence collection, testing, audit findings, remediation, and residual-risk decision-making.

The key relationship is:

```text id="n9c5qx"
Business Objective
        ↓
Risk
        ↓
Requirement
        ↓
Control
        ↓
Evidence
        ↓
Testing
        ↓
Finding
        ↓
Remediation
        ↓
Residual Risk
        ↓

```

A mature GRC environment should support **bidirectional traceability**. Management should be able to start with a risk and identify the affected controls and requirements, while an auditor should be able to start with a requirement and trace it through controls, evidence, testing, findings, and remediation.

The model therefore connects the major components of enterprise GRC:

* **Governance** defines objectives and requirements.
* **Risk management** identifies and evaluates threats to those objectives.
* **Compliance** identifies applicable obligations.
* **Control management** defines and operates controls.
* **Evidence management** demonstrates control operation.
* **Audit and assurance** tests effectiveness.
* **Remediation management** addresses weaknesses.
* **Risk management** evaluates the remaining exposure.
* **Management** makes decisions based on residual risk.

```text id="q2m6wb"

    ↓
Residual Risk
    ↓
Decision
    ↓
Continuous Improvement
```

This makes end-to-end traceability one of the most important capabilities of a mature GRC operating model. It transforms disconnected compliance, risk, audit, and security activities into a **single traceable system of governance and assurance**.

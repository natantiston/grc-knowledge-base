# 19.3 Cybersecurity Governance Case Studies

## Part 1 – Establishing a Cybersecurity Governance Program

## 1. Case Study Overview

This case study demonstrates how a large organization establishes and matures a **cybersecurity governance program**.

The focus is not primarily on technical security implementation. Instead, it examines how an organization establishes:

* cybersecurity accountability;
* governance structures;
* policies;
* risk ownership;
* security decision-making;
* regulatory alignment;
* performance measurement;
* executive oversight.

The case illustrates an important GRC principle:

> **Cybersecurity governance establishes who makes security decisions, who is accountable for risk, how decisions are made, and how security performance is measured.**

---

**GlobalNet Telecom Group**

Telecommunications and Digital Services

### Employees

### Geographic Presence

# 3. Initial Situation

GlobalNet has invested heavily in cybersecurity technology.

The organization operates:

* Security Operations Center (SOC)
* SIEM
* Endpoint Detection and Response
* Firewalls
* Identity and Access Management
* Vulnerability Management
* Security Awareness
* Incident Response

However, the organization has a governance problem.

Security decisions are being made independently by different teams.

---

# 4. Governance Problems

The internal assessment identifies several weaknesses.

### Problem 1 – Unclear Accountability

Business executives are unsure who ultimately owns cybersecurity risk.

### Problem 2 – Fragmented Policies

Different departments maintain different security requirements.

### Problem 3 – Inconsistent Risk Decisions

Similar risks are treated differently across business units.

### Problem 4 – Weak Executive Reporting

The board receives technical security statistics but limited business risk information.

### Problem 5 – Compliance-Driven Security

Security activities are often initiated only when required by regulation or audit.

---

# 5. Governance Program Objective

The CEO establishes a cybersecurity governance transformation program.

The objectives are to:

1. establish clear accountability;
2. define cybersecurity governance structures;
3. establish security policies;
4. align cybersecurity with enterprise risk management;
5. integrate compliance requirements;
6. establish security performance metrics;
7. improve executive oversight;
8. establish consistent decision-making.

---

---

# 7. Board Responsibilities

The board is responsible for oversight rather than day-to-day cybersecurity operations.

The board should understand:

* major cyber risks;
* risk appetite;
* significant incidents;
* regulatory exposure;
* resilience;
* cybersecurity investment;
* management response.

The board should be able to answer:

> **"Is the organization managing cybersecurity risk at an acceptable level?"**

---

# 8. Executive Management Responsibilities

Executive management translates board expectations into business decisions.

Responsibilities include:

* approving cybersecurity strategy;
* allocating resources;
* accepting significant risks;
* approving major investments;
* resolving cross-business conflicts;
* ensuring regulatory compliance.

Cybersecurity becomes an enterprise responsibility rather than solely an IT responsibility.

---

# 9. CISO Responsibilities

The CISO leads the cybersecurity function.

Responsibilities include:

* cybersecurity strategy;
* security architecture;
* security operations;
* cyber risk management;
* incident response;
* security monitoring;
* security awareness;
* security performance;
* executive reporting.

The CISO should also provide independent challenge to business risk decisions where appropriate.

---

# 10. GRC Responsibilities

The cybersecurity GRC function provides governance and oversight.

Typical responsibilities include:

* policies;
* standards;
* risk assessments;
* compliance;
* control frameworks;
* audits;
* third-party risk;
* metrics;
* risk reporting;
* exception management.

GRC provides the bridge between cybersecurity operations and enterprise governance.

---

# 11. Business Owner Responsibilities

Business leaders remain responsible for the risks associated with their services.

For example:

The Digital Services Director owns risks associated with a customer application.

Cybersecurity may advise and monitor, but the business owner remains accountable for the business decision.

This prevents cybersecurity from becoming the sole owner of business risk.

---

# 12. Three Lines Alignment

The organization aligns cybersecurity governance with the Three Lines Model.

### First Line

Business and technology teams:

* own risks;
* operate controls;
* implement security requirements.

### Second Line

Cybersecurity GRC, risk, and compliance:

* establish frameworks;
* monitor;
* advise;
* challenge;
* report.

### Third Line

Internal Audit:

* independently evaluates governance;
* assesses risk management;
* evaluates controls.

---

# 13. Governance Committee

GlobalNet establishes a **Cybersecurity Governance Committee (CSGC)**.

Members include:

* CIO;
* CISO;
* Chief Risk Officer;
* Legal representative;
* Compliance Officer;
* Data Protection Officer;
* CTO;
* Business Unit representatives;
* Internal Audit observer where appropriate.

The committee meets monthly.

---

# 14. Committee Responsibilities

The committee reviews:

1. cybersecurity risk;
2. major security incidents;
3. regulatory developments;
4. policy exceptions;
5. control deficiencies;
6. security performance;
7. major projects;
8. third-party security;
9. security investment;
10. remediation progress.

# 19. Cybersecurity Policy

The top-level policy establishes mandatory organizational principles.

Examples:

* cybersecurity risk must be managed;
* critical information must be protected;
* access must be authorized;
* security incidents must be reported;
* regulatory obligations must be met;
* security controls must be monitored.

The policy should be approved by the appropriate governance authority.

---

# 20. Security Standards

Standards translate policy into measurable requirements.

Examples:

### Password Standard

Defines authentication requirements.

### Encryption Standard

Defines approved encryption requirements.

### Cloud Security Standard

Defines minimum cloud security controls.

### Logging Standard

Defines minimum logging requirements.

### Vulnerability Management Standard

Defines remediation expectations.

---

# 21. Governance Through Exceptions

Not every business system can immediately comply with every security requirement.

GlobalNet therefore establishes a formal security exception process.

Example:

A legacy telecommunications platform cannot support the current authentication standard.

Instead of ignoring the issue, the organization documents:

* requirement;
* reason for exception;
* affected system;
* risk;
* compensating controls;
* owner;
* expiration date;
* approval authority.

---

# 23. Cybersecurity Risk Appetite

The board establishes cybersecurity risk appetite.

Example:

GlobalNet has:

**Very low tolerance** for:

* prolonged critical service disruption;
* unauthorized disclosure of sensitive customer information;
* compromise of critical infrastructure;
* regulatory violations.

It may have greater tolerance for:

* minor security weaknesses;
* low-impact operational vulnerabilities;
* temporary deviations with approved compensating controls.

---

# 24. Risk-Based Governance

The governance process prioritizes decisions according to risk.

For example:

### Risk A

Low-impact internal system vulnerability.

### Risk B

Potential compromise of a national telecommunications platform.

Even if both systems have vulnerabilities, Risk B receives substantially greater governance attention.

---

# 25. Cybersecurity Governance Metrics

The organization establishes executive-level metrics.

Examples:

* critical vulnerabilities overdue;
* critical systems covered by EDR;
* privileged accounts reviewed;
* security incidents;
* mean time to detect;
* mean time to respond;
* unresolved high-risk findings;
* security exceptions;
* third-party security exposure.

Metrics are selected according to decision value rather than technical availability.

---

# 26. KPI and KRI Distinction

### KPI

Measures performance.

Example:

> Percentage of critical vulnerabilities remediated within SLA.

### KRI

Measures risk exposure.

Example:

> Number of critical vulnerabilities exceeding the remediation threshold.

The two metrics should not be confused.

# 28. Regulatory Alignment

The cybersecurity governance program therefore considers:

# 31. Security Investment Governance

The organization receives three major investment proposals.

### Proposal A

Upgrade SOC platform.

Cost:

€1.2 million.

### Proposal B

Replace legacy authentication platform.

Cost:

€3.5 million.

### Proposal C

Implement additional security awareness technology.

Cost:

€400,000.

The governance committee evaluates each proposal according to:

* risk reduction;
* regulatory requirement;
* business impact;
* threat exposure;
* implementation complexity;
* cost;
* strategic alignment.

---

# 32. Risk-Based Investment Decision

The legacy authentication platform is associated with a high risk affecting critical systems.

The committee therefore prioritizes Proposal B.

The decision is based on **risk reduction and business impact**, rather than simply choosing the least expensive project.

# 37. Incident Lessons Learned

After the incident, the organization asks:

* Was the risk previously identified?
* Were controls effective?
* Were warnings ignored?
* Was the incident escalation timely?
* Was management prepared?
* Were regulatory obligations fulfilled?
* What governance changes are required?

The answers feed back into the cybersecurity governance program.

# 38. Cybersecurity Governance Maturity

GlobalNet assesses maturity using five levels.

### Level 1 – Ad Hoc

Security decisions are reactive.

### Level 2 – Developing

Basic policies and responsibilities exist.

### Level 3 – Defined

Governance structures and processes are documented.

### Level 4 – Managed

Risk, performance, compliance, and governance are integrated.

### Level 5 – Optimized

Cybersecurity governance is continuously improved using data, automation, threat intelligence, and business metrics.

# 43. Lessons Learned

## Lesson 1 – Governance Is Not the Same as Operations

The SOC operates security controls.

Governance determines whether the organization is managing security risk appropriately.

## Lesson 2 – Accountability Must Be Explicit

Security responsibilities should be documented and assigned.

## Lesson 3 – Business Ownership Matters

Cybersecurity should advise and challenge, but business owners remain accountable for business risk.

## Lesson 4 – Metrics Must Support Decisions

A dashboard containing hundreds of technical statistics does not necessarily provide effective governance.

## Lesson 5 – Exceptions Are Risk Decisions

A security exception should never become an undocumented permanent workaround.

## Lesson 6 – Governance Must Connect to Enterprise Risk

Cybersecurity risk should be visible within the organization's overall risk management framework.

# 46. Case Study Conclusion

The GlobalNet case demonstrates that effective cybersecurity governance is fundamentally about **accountability, decision-making, oversight, and risk management**.

A mature cybersecurity governance program connects:

**Business Strategy → Cybersecurity Strategy → Risk → Controls → Compliance → Assurance → Metrics → Executive Decisions**

The key principle is:

> **Cybersecurity governance ensures that security decisions are aligned with business objectives, risk appetite, regulatory obligations, and organizational accountability.**

For a GRC professional, the ability to design and operate this governance model is particularly important because it connects technical cybersecurity activities with **enterprise risk management and executive decision-making**.

## Introduction

Cybersecurity incidents can disrupt critical business operations, interrupt essential services, compromise sensitive information, and impact customers, employees, and business partners. While incident management focuses on detecting, responding to, containing, eradicating, and recovering from cybersecurity incidents, **Business Continuity Management (BCM)** ensures that the organization can continue delivering critical products and services during and after disruptive events. These two disciplines are closely related and must operate together to minimize operational disruption and support organizational resilience.

Incident management and business continuity have different primary objectives but share a common goal of protecting the organization. Incident management addresses the technical aspects of identifying and responding to cyber threats, while business continuity focuses on maintaining or restoring critical business functions within acceptable timeframes. Effective coordination between these disciplines ensures that technical recovery activities align with business priorities and that essential services remain available even when cybersecurity incidents occur.

Organizations should integrate incident response procedures with Business Continuity Plans (BCPs), Disaster Recovery Plans (DRPs), crisis management processes, and operational resilience strategies. This integration enables technical teams, business units, executive leadership, and external stakeholders to coordinate activities, allocate resources effectively, and make informed decisions during major incidents.

International standards and frameworks such as **ISO/IEC 27001**, **ISO/IEC 27035**, **ISO 22301 Business Continuity Management Systems**, **NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF)**, **NIST SP 800-61 Revision 2**, and the **Center for Internet Security (CIS) Controls** recognize the importance of aligning incident response with business continuity. Within Governance, Risk, and Compliance (GRC), this relationship strengthens governance, supports enterprise risk management, and enhances organizational resilience.

# Understanding Business Continuity

**Business Continuity Management (BCM)** is the capability of an organization to continue delivering products and services at acceptable predefined levels following a disruptive incident.

The primary objectives of BCM are to:

- Maintain critical business operations.
- Protect employees and customers.
- Reduce operational disruption.
- Minimize financial losses.

- Strengthen organizational resilience.

Business continuity focuses on sustaining business operations despite disruption.

# Practical Example

A multinational healthcare provider experiences a ransomware attack that encrypts several clinical information systems. The Security Operations Center (SOC) immediately initiates incident response procedures to contain the attack, isolate affected servers, and begin forensic analysis. At the same time, the organization's Business Continuity Plan (BCP) is activated. Hospital staff switch to manual patient registration procedures, emergency departments continue operating using predefined contingency processes, and backup communication channels are used to coordinate clinical activities. While technical teams restore systems from secure backups, business continuity teams ensure that essential healthcare services remain available to patients. Once systems are validated and safely restored, normal operations resume, and the organization conducts a lessons learned review to improve both incident response and business continuity plans.

This example demonstrates how incident management and business continuity work together to protect both technical systems and essential business operations during a significant cybersecurity incident.

# Disaster Recovery Integration

Disaster Recovery (DR) is a critical component of Incident Recovery and Business Continuity. While incident response focuses on identifying, containing, and eradicating cybersecurity threats, **Disaster Recovery (DR)** focuses on restoring affected information systems, infrastructure, applications, and data to normal operation after a disruptive event. Integrating disaster recovery with incident management ensures that organizations can recover efficiently from cyber incidents while minimizing downtime, protecting critical business services, and maintaining operational resilience.

Modern cyberattacks such as ransomware, destructive malware, insider threats, supply chain attacks, and cloud service disruptions can render systems unavailable or corrupt critical business data. In these situations, restoring systems is often as important as removing the threat itself. Disaster Recovery provides structured processes for recovering IT services using backups, redundant infrastructure, cloud recovery solutions, alternate processing sites, and predefined recovery procedures.

Effective Disaster Recovery cannot operate independently from incident management. Recovery activities should only begin after the threat has been sufficiently contained and eradicated; otherwise, restored systems may become compromised again. Security teams, IT operations, business continuity teams, and executive leadership must coordinate closely to ensure that recovery activities are secure, prioritized, and aligned with business objectives.

International standards and frameworks such as **ISO/IEC 27001**, **ISO/IEC 27035**, **ISO 22301 Business Continuity Management Systems**, **NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF)**, **NIST SP 800-61 Revision 2**, **NIST SP 800-34 Contingency Planning Guide for Federal Information Systems**, and the **Center for Internet Security (CIS) Controls** recognize disaster recovery as an essential component of organizational resilience. Within Governance, Risk, and Compliance (GRC), disaster recovery integration strengthens governance, supports enterprise risk management, and demonstrates preparedness for major operational disruptions.

# Recovery Objectives

Disaster Recovery planning is guided by two important recovery objectives.

### Recovery Time Objective (RTO)

The maximum acceptable amount of time that a business process or IT service can remain unavailable following a disruption.

### Recovery Point Objective (RPO)

The maximum acceptable amount of data loss measured in time.

For example:

- RTO = 4 hours
- RPO = 30 minutes

Organizations define RTOs and RPOs based on business requirements identified during the Business Impact Analysis (BIA).

# Practical Example

A multinational retail company experiences a ransomware attack that encrypts several virtual servers supporting its online sales platform. The Security Operations Center (SOC) isolates the affected systems while the Computer Security Incident Response Team (CSIRT) investigates the attack and confirms that the malware has been eradicated. Once containment is verified, the Disaster Recovery team activates the organization's recovery plan. Clean virtual machine images are deployed, application servers are restored from verified backups, databases are recovered to meet the defined Recovery Point Objective (RPO), and business applications are validated before reconnecting them to the production network. Throughout the recovery process, Business Continuity teams coordinate with business units to prioritize critical services and minimize customer disruption. After successful validation, normal operations resume, and the organization updates its disaster recovery procedures based on lessons learned from the incident.

This example illustrates how integrating disaster recovery with incident management enables organizations to restore technology securely, meet business recovery objectives, and strengthen overall operational resilience.

# Crisis Management

Not every cybersecurity incident develops into a business crisis. However, major incidents such as ransomware attacks, large-scale data breaches, prolonged service outages, supply chain compromises, or attacks against critical infrastructure can rapidly escalate beyond technical issues and threaten the organization's operations, reputation, finances, regulatory compliance, and stakeholder confidence. When this occurs, **Crisis Management** provides the strategic leadership, coordination, and decision-making required to guide the organization through the event while minimizing business impact.

Crisis Management operates at the executive level and focuses on protecting the organization as a whole rather than resolving technical security issues. While the Security Operations Center (SOC) and Computer Security Incident Response Team (CSIRT) investigate and contain the cyberattack, the Crisis Management Team (CMT) coordinates executive decisions, allocates resources, manages communications, oversees business continuity, engages regulators, and protects the organization's reputation. Effective crisis management ensures that technical response activities remain aligned with business priorities throughout the incident lifecycle.

A mature Crisis Management capability integrates closely with Incident Management, Business Continuity Management (BCM), Disaster Recovery (DR), Legal, Compliance, Human Resources, Communications, Risk Management, and executive leadership. Clearly defined governance structures, escalation procedures, decision-making authority, and communication plans enable organizations to respond confidently during high-pressure situations.

International standards and frameworks such as **ISO/IEC 27001**, **ISO/IEC 27035**, **ISO 22301 Business Continuity Management Systems**, **NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF)**, **NIST SP 800-61 Revision 2**, and the **Center for Internet Security (CIS) Controls** emphasize executive coordination and organizational resilience during significant cybersecurity incidents. Within Governance, Risk, and Compliance (GRC), crisis management strengthens governance, supports enterprise risk management, and enhances organizational resilience.

# Operational Resilience

Modern organizations operate in an increasingly complex digital environment where cyberattacks, technology failures, natural disasters, third-party disruptions, and human error can significantly affect business operations. While incident response, disaster recovery, and business continuity help organizations recover from disruptive events, **Operational Resilience** focuses on ensuring that critical business services can continue to operate before, during, and after disruptions. Rather than concentrating solely on recovering systems, operational resilience emphasizes the organization's ability to anticipate, withstand, adapt to, respond to, and recover from adverse events while maintaining essential business functions.

Operational resilience extends beyond traditional cybersecurity by integrating people, processes, technology, facilities, third-party services, governance, and risk management into a unified resilience strategy. It recognizes that disruptions cannot always be prevented, but their impact can be minimized through effective planning, preparedness, and continuous improvement. A resilient organization not only recovers from incidents but also learns from them and becomes stronger over time.

Cybersecurity incident management plays a vital role in operational resilience by detecting threats early, coordinating response activities, supporting business continuity, enabling disaster recovery, and protecting critical business services. Organizations that integrate these disciplines are better positioned to reduce downtime, protect customers, maintain regulatory compliance, and preserve stakeholder confidence during major disruptions.

International standards and frameworks such as **ISO/IEC 27001**, **ISO/IEC 27035**, **ISO 22301 Business Continuity Management Systems**, **NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF)**, **NIST SP 800-61 Revision 2**, **NIST SP 800-34 Contingency Planning Guide**, and the **Center for Internet Security (CIS) Controls** support the development of resilient cybersecurity and business operations. Within Governance, Risk, and Compliance (GRC), operational resilience strengthens governance, enhances enterprise risk management, and supports long-term organizational sustainability.

# Common Challenges

Organizations commonly encounter challenges such as:

- Complex technology environments.
- Third-party dependencies.
- Resource constraints.
- Legacy infrastructure.
- Rapidly evolving cyber threats.
- Inconsistent governance.
- Limited resilience testing.
- Organizational silos.

Addressing these challenges requires enterprise-wide collaboration.

---

# Best Practices

Organizations should:

- Identify and prioritize critical business services.
- Integrate incident management with business continuity and disaster recovery.
- Conduct regular resilience exercises.
- Review resilience metrics.
- Strengthen third-party risk management.
- Improve executive governance.
- Update resilience plans regularly.
- Apply lessons learned from incidents and exercises.

Operational resilience should evolve continuously as the organization's business and technology landscape changes.

---

# GRC Perspective

Operational resilience strengthens Governance, Risk, and Compliance by ensuring that critical business services remain available during disruptions while supporting effective governance, enterprise risk management, and regulatory compliance.

### Governance

Governance responsibilities include:

- Establishing resilience policies.
- Defining resilience objectives.
- Monitoring organizational performance.
- Supporting executive oversight.
- Aligning resilience with business strategy.
- Driving continual improvement.

### Risk Management

Risk management activities include:

- Identifying operational risks.
- Protecting critical business services.
- Managing technology dependencies.
- Supporting enterprise resilience.
- Prioritizing resilience investments.
- Reducing business disruption.

### Compliance

Operational resilience supports compliance with:

- ISO/IEC 27001 Information Security Management System (ISMS)
- ISO/IEC 27035 Information Security Incident Management
- ISO 22301 Business Continuity Management Systems
- NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF)
- NIST SP 800-34 Contingency Planning Guide for Federal Information Systems
- NIST SP 800-61 Computer Security Incident Handling Guide
- Center for Internet Security (CIS) Controls
- NIS2 Directive
- General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR)
- Industry-specific cybersecurity and resilience regulations

Documented resilience strategies, recovery testing, incident metrics, and continual improvement activities demonstrate operational readiness and support regulatory and audit requirements.

# Practical Example

A multinational energy company operates critical infrastructure that supports electricity distribution across several regions. To strengthen operational resilience, the organization integrates its Security Operations Center (SOC), Incident Response Team, Business Continuity Management (BCM), Disaster Recovery (DR), Crisis Management Team, and enterprise Risk Management functions. During a sophisticated cyberattack targeting operational technology (OT) systems, security teams rapidly detect and contain the attack while business continuity procedures maintain essential services using redundant infrastructure. Disaster recovery teams restore affected systems from secure backups, executive leadership coordinates communications with regulators and customers, and risk managers continuously assess business impact throughout the incident. Following recovery, the organization conducts a comprehensive lessons learned review and updates its resilience strategy, response plans, and recovery procedures to improve preparedness for future disruptions.

This example demonstrates how operational resilience enables organizations to coordinate technical, operational, and strategic capabilities to maintain critical services, recover efficiently, and continually strengthen their ability to withstand future cyber incidents.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Operational resilience is the ability of an organization to anticipate, withstand, respond to, recover from, and adapt to disruptive events while maintaining critical business services.
- Incident management, business continuity, disaster recovery, crisis management, governance, and enterprise risk management work together to build organizational resilience.
- Recovery objectives, resilience metrics, regular testing, and continual improvement are essential for maintaining operational readiness.
- Organizations should integrate resilience planning across people, processes, technology, facilities, and third-party services to reduce operational disruption.
- From a Governance, Risk, and Compliance (GRC) perspective, operational resilience strengthens governance through executive oversight, supports enterprise risk management by protecting critical business services, and demonstrates compliance with international standards and regulatory expectations while improving long-term organizational sustainability.

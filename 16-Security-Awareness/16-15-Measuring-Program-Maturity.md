## Security Awareness Maturity Models

A security awareness program can exist at very different levels of maturity.

Some organizations have only basic mandatory training:

> "Complete the annual cybersecurity course."

Others operate sophisticated programs that use:

* Risk-based training.
* Phishing simulations.
* Behavioral analytics.
* Role-based awareness.
* Metrics and KPIs.
* Continuous improvement.
* Human risk management.
* Executive reporting.

A **security awareness maturity model** provides a structured way to determine where an organization currently stands and what it should do to improve.

The fundamental concept is:

> **Maturity measures how effectively and consistently an organization manages security awareness—not simply how much training it delivers.**

---

# What Is a Maturity Model?

A maturity model is a structured framework used to evaluate how developed a capability is.

For security awareness, it can assess areas such as:

* Governance.
* Training.
* Communication.
* Phishing simulations.
* Measurement.
* Behavioral change.
* Technology.
* Role-based awareness.
* Third-party awareness.
* Continuous improvement.

A maturity model normally defines several levels.

For example:

**Level 1 → Initial**

↓

**Level 2 → Developing**

↓

**Level 3 → Defined**

↓

**Level 4 → Managed**

↓

**Level 5 → Optimized**

The exact terminology can vary between organizations and frameworks.

---

# Why Measure Maturity?

Without maturity assessment, an organization may know:

> "We conduct security awareness training."

But it may not know:

* Whether the program is effective.
* Whether employees are changing behavior.
* Whether high-risk groups receive appropriate training.
* Whether management supports the program.
* Whether metrics are meaningful.
* Whether the program is sustainable.
* Whether the organization is improving.

Maturity assessment provides a broader perspective.

---

# Training Completion Is Not Maturity

Consider two organizations.

### Organization A

* 100% training completion.
* No phishing simulations.
* No behavioral metrics.
* No role-based training.
* No executive reporting.

### Organization B

* 95% training completion.
* Risk-based simulations.
* Behavioral analytics.
* Role-based awareness.
* Executive dashboards.
* Continuous improvement.

Organization A has higher completion but may have a less mature program.

This demonstrates:

> **Completion is a metric. Maturity is a capability.**

---

# Typical Security Awareness Maturity Levels

A five-level model can be used as a practical example.

## Level 1 – Initial

Security awareness is mostly reactive and informal.

Characteristics may include:

* Limited formal training.
* No dedicated ownership.
* Minimal metrics.
* Training occurs mainly after incidents.
* Policies exist but are not reinforced consistently.

Example:

> Employees receive occasional security emails after a security incident.

The program depends heavily on individual effort.

---

# Level 2 – Developing

The organization begins establishing a formal program.

Characteristics may include:

* Annual mandatory training.
* Basic awareness policies.
* Defined training requirements.
* Basic tracking.
* Some phishing simulations.
* Initial management involvement.

The organization has moved beyond an informal approach.

However, the program may still be largely compliance-driven.

---

# Level 3 – Defined

The organization has a structured and documented awareness program.

Characteristics may include:

* Formal security awareness strategy.
* Defined roles and responsibilities.
* Regular training.
* Phishing simulations.
* Role-based training.
* Defined metrics.
* Documented governance.
* Third-party awareness requirements.

At this stage, security awareness becomes an established organizational capability.

---

# Level 4 – Managed

The organization actively measures and manages awareness effectiveness.

Characteristics may include:

* Behavioral analytics.
* Human-risk indicators.
* Risk-based training.
* Executive dashboards.
* Advanced phishing simulations.
* Targeted remediation.
* Continuous measurement.
* Integration with GRC and security operations.

The organization can answer:

> **"Is our awareness program actually reducing human-related risk?"**

---

# Level 5 – Optimized

The program continuously evolves based on data, threat intelligence, and organizational risk.

Characteristics may include:

* AI-assisted personalization.
* Predictive human-risk analytics.
* Continuous learning.
* Advanced behavioral analysis.
* Threat-informed awareness.
* Automated recommendations.
* Mature security culture.
* Continuous improvement.
* Strong integration with enterprise risk management.

At this stage:

> **Security awareness becomes an integral component of enterprise cyber-risk management.**

| Capability          | Level 1  | Level 2    | Level 3    | Level 4    | Level 5     |
| ------------------- | -------- | ---------- | ---------- | ---------- | ----------- |
| Governance          | Informal | Basic      | Defined    | Managed    | Optimized   |
| Training            | Ad hoc   | Annual     | Structured | Risk-based | Adaptive    |
| Phishing            | None     | Occasional | Regular    | Targeted   | Adaptive    |
| Metrics             | Minimal  | Completion | KPIs       | Behavioral | Predictive  |
| Role-Based Training | None     | Limited    | Defined    | Risk-based | Dynamic     |
| Reporting           | Basic    | Compliance | Management | Executive  | Strategic   |
| Improvement         | Reactive | Periodic   | Planned    | Continuous | Data-driven |

This provides a useful way to visualize maturity.

---

# Maturity Is Multi-Dimensional

An organization does not necessarily have one maturity level across everything.

For example:

| Capability            | Maturity |
| --------------------- | -------: |
| Governance            |  Level 4 |
| Training              |  Level 3 |
| Phishing Simulation   |  Level 4 |
| Behavioral Analytics  |  Level 2 |
| Third-Party Awareness |  Level 2 |
| Executive Reporting   |  Level 3 |
| AI Enablement         |  Level 1 |

The organization might therefore have an overall maturity of approximately Level 3, while some capabilities are more advanced and others require improvement.

This is much more useful than simply assigning one overall number.

# Maturity Domains

A security awareness maturity assessment can examine several domains.

## 1. Governance

Questions include:

* Is there a formal awareness policy?
* Is ownership defined?
* Is there executive sponsorship?
* Are responsibilities documented?

---

## 2. Strategy

Questions include:

* Is there an awareness strategy?
* Is it aligned with business risks?
* Are objectives defined?
* Is there a roadmap?

---

## 3. Training

Questions include:

* Is training mandatory?
* Is it role-based?
* Is it updated regularly?
* Is completion monitored?

---

## 4. Phishing Simulation

Questions include:

* Are simulations conducted?
* Are they risk-based?
* Is behavior measured?
* Is remediation provided?

---

## 5. Measurement

Questions include:

* Are KPIs defined?
* Are KRIs monitored?
* Is behavioral change measured?
* Is trend analysis performed?

---

## 6. Technology

Questions include:

* Is an LMS used?
* Is a phishing platform used?
* Is behavioral analytics available?
* Are systems integrated?

---

## 7. Culture

Questions include:

* Do employees report suspicious activity?
* Does leadership reinforce security?
* Are employees encouraged to speak up?
* Is security part of everyday work?

---

## 8. Continuous Improvement

Questions include:

* Are program results reviewed?
* Are weaknesses addressed?
* Is training updated?
* Are lessons from incidents incorporated?

# Maturity Assessment Criteria

Each maturity level should have clearly defined characteristics.

For example:

### Phishing Simulation

**Level 1**

No simulations.

**Level 2**

Occasional simulations.

**Level 3**

Regular organization-wide campaigns.

**Level 4**

Risk-based and role-based simulations.

**Level 5**

Adaptive simulations based on threat intelligence and behavioral analytics.

This allows assessors to determine the organization's current position objectively.

---

# Evidence-Based Assessment

Maturity should be based on evidence rather than assumptions.

For example, an organization might claim:

> "We have a mature awareness program."

The assessor should request evidence such as:

* Awareness strategy.
* Training records.
* Phishing campaign results.
* Metrics.
* Management reports.
* Risk assessments.
* Awareness policies.
* Training materials.
* Improvement plans.

This is particularly important from a GRC perspective.

---

# Maturity Assessment Evidence

Examples include:

### Governance Evidence

* Approved awareness policy.
* Program charter.
* RACI matrix.

### Training Evidence

* Training schedule.
* Completion reports.
* Training content.

### Simulation Evidence

* Campaign reports.
* Click rates.
* Reporting rates.

### Measurement Evidence

* KPI dashboards.
* KRI reports.
* Trend analysis.

* Corrective action plans.

* Program reviews.

Evidence demonstrates that the capability actually exists.

# Current State vs. Target State

A maturity model should not simply ask:

> "What level are we?"

It should also ask:

> **"What level should we be?"**

A small organization may not need a Level 5 AI-enabled awareness program.

For example:

**Current state:** Level 2

**Target state:** Level 3

That may be appropriate.

Another organization handling highly sensitive information may need:

**Current state:** Level 3

**Target state:** Level 4 or 5

The target should be determined by **risk and business requirements**, not technology trends.

---

# Maturity and Risk

Maturity should be connected to organizational risk.

Consider two companies.

### Company A

Small organization with limited sensitive data.

Target maturity:

**Level 3**

### Company B

Large financial institution with highly sensitive customer data.

Target maturity:

**Level 4–5**

It would not make sense to apply exactly the same maturity expectations to both.

---

# Risk-Based Maturity

A useful approach is:

**Business Risk**

*

**Threat Landscape**

*

**Regulatory Requirements**

*

**Organizational Complexity**

↓

**Required Awareness Maturity**

This prevents organizations from pursuing maturity for its own sake.

---

# Maturity and Compliance

Compliance requirements can influence maturity.

For example, organizations may need to demonstrate:

* Security awareness.
* Employee training.
* Role-specific competence.
* Security responsibilities.
* Evidence of ongoing improvement.

However:

> **Compliance should be a baseline, not the ultimate objective.**

An organization can be technically compliant while still having poor security behavior.

---

# Maturity and ISO/IEC 27001

ISO/IEC 27001 provides requirements for establishing, implementing, maintaining, and continually improving an information security management system.

Security awareness and competence are important components of an ISMS.

A maturity assessment can therefore help an organization determine how effectively its awareness capability supports the ISMS.

For example:

**Requirement**

↓

Security awareness and competence

↓

**Current capability**

Level 2

↓

**Gap**

Limited measurement

↓

**Improvement**

Introduce behavioral metrics and targeted training

↓

**Target**

Level 3 or 4

This demonstrates a structured improvement approach.

---

# Maturity and NIST

NIST-based programs can also use maturity-style thinking to evaluate how well awareness capabilities are integrated into cybersecurity risk management.

For example:

**Identify**

Human-related risks.

**Protect**

Provide awareness and training.

**Detect**

Measure suspicious behavior and reporting.

**Respond**

Train employees on incident reporting.

**Recover**

Use lessons learned to improve awareness.

The awareness program therefore supports the broader cybersecurity lifecycle.

---

# Maturity and Human Risk Management

Modern security programs increasingly view employees as part of the organization's risk landscape.

This shifts the focus from:

> **Security Awareness Training**

to:

> **Human Risk Management**

The maturity model should therefore consider whether the organization can:

* Identify human risks.
* Measure human behavior.
* Prioritize high-risk populations.
* Apply interventions.
* Measure improvement.

---

# Maturity Assessment Process

A practical assessment can follow these steps.

### Step 1 – Define the Scope

Determine what is being assessed.

For example:

> Enterprise security awareness program.

### Step 2 – Define the Maturity Model

Establish the maturity levels and criteria.

### Step 3 – Gather Evidence

Review:

* Policies.
* Procedures.
* Training.
* Metrics.
* Reports.
* Interviews.

### Step 4 – Score Each Domain

Assign maturity levels.

### Step 5 – Validate the Results

Discuss findings with stakeholders.

### Step 6 – Identify Gaps

Compare current and target states.

### Step 7 – Prioritize Improvements

Focus on the highest-risk gaps.

### Step 8 – Create a Roadmap

Define actions, owners, and timelines.

### Step 9 – Implement

Execute improvement initiatives.

### Step 10 – Reassess

Measure maturity again.

# Interviews and Workshops

Document review alone may not reveal the true maturity level.

Interviews can involve:

* CISO.
* Security awareness manager.
* HR.
* IT.
* SOC.
* Privacy.
* Legal.
* Business managers.

Questions may include:

> Who owns security awareness?

> How are high-risk employees identified?

> How do you measure behavioral change?

> What happens after a phishing simulation failure?

> How are lessons from incidents incorporated into training?

These questions reveal whether processes actually operate in practice.

---

# Maturity Assessment Scoring

An organization can use a simple 1–5 scoring approach.

### 1 – Initial

Ad hoc.

### 2 – Developing

Partially established.

### 3 – Defined

Documented and standardized.

### 4 – Managed

Measured and actively managed.

### 5 – Optimized

Continuously improved and adaptive.

The score itself is less important than the evidence and improvement actions behind it.

---

# Avoiding False Precision

A common mistake is treating:

> **3.72**

as though it represents an exact measurement.

Maturity models involve judgment.

A score of 3.7 does not necessarily mean an organization is objectively 23% more mature than an organization scoring 3.0.

Therefore:

> **Use maturity scores as decision-support tools, not mathematical truths.**

# Prioritizing Maturity Gaps

Not every gap needs immediate remediation.

A GRC professional can prioritize based on:

**Risk Impact**

*

**Likelihood**

*

**Regulatory Importance**

*

**Effort**

For example:

### Gap A

No executive awareness training.

High risk.

→ High priority.

### Gap B

Awareness dashboard lacks advanced visualization.

Low risk.

→ Lower priority.

This ensures maturity improvement remains risk-driven.

---

# Maturity Roadmap

Suppose an organization is currently Level 2 and wants to reach Level 4.

A roadmap might look like:

### Year 1

* Establish governance.
* Formalize training.
* Introduce phishing simulations.
* Define KPIs.

### Year 2

* Implement behavioral analytics.
* Introduce role-based training.
* Develop executive reporting.
* Integrate awareness with GRC.

### Year 3

* Implement human-risk management.
* Introduce advanced analytics.
* Develop predictive capabilities.
* Establish continuous improvement.

The roadmap converts the maturity assessment into actionable improvement.

---

# Common Mistakes

Organizations should avoid:

### Pursuing Maturity Without Risk Context

Higher maturity is not automatically better.

### Focusing Only on Training

Awareness includes culture, behavior, measurement, and governance.

### Scoring Without Evidence

A maturity score should be supported by objective evidence.

### Treating Compliance as Maturity

Compliance represents requirements; maturity measures capability.

### Ignoring Human Behavior

Completion does not equal secure behavior.

### Trying to Reach Level 5 Immediately

Organizations should improve progressively.

### Ignoring Sustainability

A mature program must continue operating even when personnel change.

### Failing to Reassess

Maturity should be measured periodically.

# Practical Example

Consider a multinational company with 15,000 employees.

The organization performs a maturity assessment.

### Findings

**Governance:** Level 3

A formal program exists.

**Training:** Level 3

Annual training is established.

**Phishing:** Level 4

Regular risk-based simulations are conducted.

**Metrics:** Level 2

The organization mainly measures completion.

**Culture:** Level 3

Employees generally report suspicious activity.

**Third Party:** Level 1

Contractors receive limited awareness requirements.

The assessment identifies:

> **Third-party awareness as the largest maturity gap.**

The organization therefore prioritizes:

1. Vendor awareness requirements.
2. Contractor training.
3. Third-party security clauses.
4. Third-party completion tracking.
5. Third-party phishing awareness.

This is a much more effective approach than simply increasing employee training frequency.

**Current State**

↓

**Risk**

↓

**Control Capability**

↓

**Gap**

↓

**Target State**

↓

**Improvement Roadmap**

For example:

↓

> Risk: Employees have limited ability to recognize targeted phishing.

> Gap: No behavioral measurement.

> Improvement: Implement phishing simulations and behavioral analytics.

# Key Takeaways

Security awareness maturity models help organizations determine how effectively their awareness capability is governed, implemented, measured, and improved.

The key principles are:

1. **Maturity is broader than training completion.**
2. **A maturity model provides a structured way to evaluate program capability.**
3. **Organizations can use multiple maturity levels, such as Initial through Optimized.**
4. **Different security awareness domains can have different maturity levels.**
5. **Maturity assessments should be evidence-based.**
6. **The target maturity level should be determined by organizational risk and business requirements.**
7. **Compliance requirements can influence maturity but should not be the sole objective.**
8. **Behavioral measurement becomes increasingly important at higher maturity levels.**
9. **Maturity gaps should be prioritized based on risk rather than convenience.**
10. **A maturity assessment should result in an actionable improvement roadmap.**
11. **Maturity scores should not create false precision.**
12. **Security awareness maturity should be reassessed periodically.**
13. **A mature program integrates governance, training, technology, metrics, culture, and continuous improvement.**
14. **Third-party and human-risk considerations should be included in enterprise maturity assessments.**
15. **The ultimate goal is not achieving the highest maturity level—it is achieving the level necessary to manage the organization's human-related security risks effectively.**

The strongest maturity programs evolve from:

> **Compliance-driven training**

to:

> **Structured awareness**

to:

> **Measured behavioral change**

to:

> **Risk-based human security management**

Ultimately, a maturity model gives the GRC professional a structured answer to an important question:

> **"How capable are we today, where do we need to be, and what must we do to close the gap?"**

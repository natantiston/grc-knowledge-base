# 19.6 ISO 27001 Audit and Continual Improvement Case Studies

## Part 1 – Preparing for an ISO 27001 Internal Audit

## 1. Case Study Overview

**IberiaCloud Digital Services** has operated its ISO/IEC 27001 Information Security Management System (ISMS) for approximately one year.

Management now requires an internal audit before the organization's next certification surveillance audit.

The objective is not simply to determine whether documents exist.

The internal audit must determine whether the ISMS:

* conforms to applicable ISO 27001 requirements;
* conforms to the organization's own policies and procedures;
* is effectively implemented;
* operates as intended;
* produces sufficient evidence;
* identifies opportunities for improvement.

The GRC team therefore develops a structured internal-audit program.

---

# 2. Organization Profile

### Organization

**IberiaCloud Digital Services**

### Industry

Cloud and telecommunications services

### Employees

Approximately 1,800

### ISMS Scope

Cloud and managed digital services supporting operations in Spain and Portugal.

### Certification

ISO 27001 certified approximately one year earlier.

### Current Environment

Since certification, the organization has:

* migrated several workloads to Azure;
* introduced an AI-enabled customer-service platform;
* changed two critical suppliers;
* expanded remote working;
* implemented a new IAM platform;
* experienced an increase in phishing attempts.

These changes make the internal audit particularly important.

---

# 3. Why the Internal Audit Matters

Management initially asks:

> "Can we pass the surveillance audit?"

The GRC Manager reframes the objective:

> **"The internal audit should determine whether the ISMS is actually effective before the certification body evaluates it."**

This changes the audit from a compliance exercise into an assurance mechanism.

---

# 4. Internal Audit Objectives

The audit objectives are to determine whether:

1. the ISMS conforms to ISO 27001 requirements;
2. the ISMS conforms to internal policies;
3. risks are appropriately managed;
4. controls are implemented;
5. controls operate effectively;
6. evidence is reliable;
7. previous findings have been addressed;
8. management requirements are being met;
9. changes have been incorporated into the ISMS;
10. opportunities for improvement exist.

---

# 5. Internal Audit Scope

The audit covers:

### Governance

* ISMS governance;
* leadership;
* responsibilities;
* policies.

### Risk

* risk methodology;
* risk register;
* treatment;
* risk acceptance.

### Controls

* access management;
* supplier security;
* incident management;
* vulnerability management;
* business continuity.

### Assurance

* monitoring;
* internal audit;
* management review;
* corrective actions.

---

# 6. Audit Criteria

The audit team establishes formal criteria.

These include:

* ISO/IEC 27001 requirements;
* organizational security policies;
* documented procedures;
* risk-management methodology;
* Statement of Applicability;
* contractual requirements;
* applicable regulatory requirements.

The auditor will compare **actual practice against defined criteria**.

---

# 7. Audit Independence

The GRC Manager wants the IAM Manager to audit privileged-access management because the IAM Manager knows the process best.

Internal Audit rejects the proposal.

The reason is independence.

The IAM Manager owns the process and therefore should not audit their own work.

Instead, an independent auditor is assigned.

---

# 8. Audit Team

The audit team consists of:

| Role               | Responsibility                    |
| ------------------ | --------------------------------- |
| Lead Auditor       | Overall audit coordination        |
| GRC Auditor        | ISMS and risk management          |
| Technical Auditor  | Security controls                 |
| Privacy Specialist | Data protection controls          |
| Business Auditor   | Business continuity               |
| Process Owners     | Provide evidence and explanations |

The audit team has sufficient knowledge without auditing its own activities.

---

# 9. Audit Program

The organization establishes a risk-based annual audit program.

High-risk areas receive more attention.

Example:

| Area                | Risk   | Audit Frequency |
| ------------------- | ------ | --------------- |
| Privileged Access   | High   | Annual          |
| Supplier Security   | High   | Annual          |
| Incident Management | High   | Annual          |
| Business Continuity | High   | Annual          |
| Security Awareness  | Medium | Annual          |
| Document Management | Low    | Periodic        |

The program is adjusted when significant changes occur.

---

# 10. Risk-Based Audit Planning

The audit team does not allocate equal time to every control.

For example:

A low-risk document-control process may require:

**2 hours**

while privileged-access management may require:

**2 days**

because the potential business impact is substantially higher.

This is a fundamental GRC principle:

> **Audit effort should reflect risk.**

---

# 11. Step 1 – Review Previous Audit Results

The first activity is reviewing previous findings.

Last year's audit identified:

* supplier reassessment delays;
* incomplete access-review evidence;
* outdated incident procedures.

The team checks whether corrective actions were:

* implemented;
* documented;
* effective.

---

# 12. Step 2 – Review Organizational Changes

The audit team reviews major changes since the previous audit.

Changes include:

* new cloud architecture;
* AI platform;
* IAM migration;
* new suppliers;
* organizational restructuring.

The team asks:

> **Were these changes incorporated into the ISMS?**

---

# 13. Change-to-Risk Traceability

For the AI platform, the auditor expects to see:

---

# 14. Step 3 – Review ISMS Documentation

The auditor reviews:

* ISMS scope;
* policies;
* procedures;
* risk methodology;
* risk register;
* risk treatment plan;
* Statement of Applicability;
* objectives;
* management review records.

The purpose is not simply to check whether documents exist.

The auditor evaluates whether the documentation reflects **actual operations**.

---

# 15. Example Documentation Gap

The access-management procedure states:

> "Privileged access shall be reviewed quarterly."

The auditor asks:

> "Show me the most recent quarterly review."

The process owner provides evidence.

However, the review was actually performed after five months.

The auditor investigates the discrepancy between:

**Defined Process**

and

**Actual Process**.

---

# 16. Step 4 – Conduct Interviews

Interviews are an important audit technique.

Employees are asked questions such as:

* What are your responsibilities?
* How do you report an incident?
* How is privileged access approved?
* What happens when an employee leaves?
* How are security risks identified?
* Where do you find the relevant procedure?

The auditor compares employee responses with documented processes.

---

# 17. Interview Example

The procedure states:

> Employees must report security incidents through the security portal.

The employee explains:

> "We normally send an email to our manager."

The auditor identifies a possible process-awareness gap.

The issue is investigated rather than immediately classified as a finding.

---

# 18. Step 5 – Sampling

The audit team cannot test every transaction.

Instead, it uses sampling.

For example:

### Access Management

Sample:

**30 privileged accounts**

### Supplier Security

Sample:

**15 critical suppliers**

### Incident Management

Sample:

**20 incidents**

### Training

Sample:

**50 employees**

Sampling should be risk-based and sufficiently representative.

---

# 19. Step 6 – Evidence Testing

The auditor evaluates evidence using several dimensions:

### Authenticity

Is the evidence genuine?

### Completeness

Does it contain enough information?

### Relevance

Does it actually demonstrate the control?

### Timeliness

Is it from the appropriate period?

### Traceability

Can it be connected to the requirement and control?

---

# 20. Weak vs Strong Evidence

### Weak Evidence

A spreadsheet stating:

> "Quarterly review completed."

### Strong Evidence

A system-generated record showing:

* account population;
* review date;
* reviewer;
* decisions;
* exceptions;
* remediation;
* approval.

The second provides substantially stronger assurance.

---

# 21. Step 7 – Control Testing

The auditor selects a control:

**Privileged Access Review**

The defined control requires:

1. quarterly review;
2. review by account owner;
3. removal of unnecessary access;
4. documented approval.

The auditor tests whether each requirement was satisfied.

---

# 22. Control Testing Example

Sample:

**30 privileged accounts**

Results:

* 30 identified;
* 28 reviewed on time;
* 2 reviewed late;
* 3 unnecessary permissions identified;
* all 3 subsequently removed.

The control is operating, but there is a timeliness weakness.

The auditor investigates the significance.

---

# 23. Step 8 – Walkthrough Testing

The auditor performs a walkthrough.

Example:

```text id="wlk6m1"
Access Request
      |
      v
Manager Approval
      |
      v
Security Approval
      |
      v
IAM Provisioning
      |
      v
Access Granted
      |
      v
Periodic Review
      |
      v
Access Removal
```

The auditor follows an actual access request through the entire process.

This exposes weaknesses that document review alone may not identify.

---

# 24. Step 9 – Control Design vs Operating Effectiveness

The audit distinguishes two questions.

### Design

> Is the control appropriately designed?

### Operation

> Does the control actually operate as designed?

A control can therefore be:

### Well Designed + Effective

Ideal.

### Well Designed + Ineffective

Implementation problem.

### Poorly Designed + Consistently Performed

Still problematic.

### Poorly Designed + Ineffective

High concern.

---

# 25. Example

The organization has a quarterly access-review control.

The process is clearly defined.

However, reviewers receive no automated reminders.

The control is:

**Adequately designed but operationally inefficient.**

The auditor may recommend automation.

---

# 26. Step 10 – Risk Register Testing

The auditor selects five high risks from the risk register.

For each risk, the auditor verifies:

* risk owner;
* assessment;
* treatment;
* controls;
* residual risk;
* review date.

One risk has not been reviewed for 14 months.

The auditor investigates why.

---

# 27. Risk-Control Traceability

The auditor expects:

```text id="rct4m8"
Risk
 |
 v
Risk Treatment
 |
 v
Control
 |
 v
Control Owner
 |
 v
Evidence
 |
 v

```

A risk register that has no connection to operational controls provides limited assurance.

---

# 28. Step 11 – Statement of Applicability Review

The auditor selects several controls from the SoA.

For each selected control, the auditor verifies:

* applicability;
* justification;
* implementation status;
* supporting evidence;
* relationship to risk treatment.

One control is marked:

**Implemented**

but the supporting evidence is incomplete.

The auditor raises an observation for further investigation.

---

# 29. Step 12 – Supplier Security Testing

The audit team selects 15 critical suppliers.

The auditor checks:

* due diligence;
* security requirements;
* contractual clauses;
* assessments;
* remediation;
* monitoring.

Results:

* 13 fully compliant;
* 1 reassessment overdue;
* 1 supplier undergoing remediation.

The auditor assesses whether the organization is appropriately managing the associated risks.

---

# 30. Step 13 – Incident Management Testing

The auditor selects 20 incidents.

The review checks:

* classification;
* response;
* escalation;
* investigation;
* evidence;
* closure;
* lessons learned.

One incident lacks a documented lessons-learned record.

The auditor evaluates whether this represents:

* an isolated documentation issue;
* a process weakness;
* or a systemic failure.

---

# 31. Step 14 – Business Continuity Testing

The auditor reviews recovery testing.

The organization claims:

> "Critical systems are tested annually."

Evidence shows that 8 of 10 critical systems participated in the most recent exercise.

Two systems were excluded.

The auditor asks:

> "Why?"

The organization provides documented justification and a scheduled test.

This may be acceptable if risk is appropriately managed.

---

# 32. Step 15 – Security Awareness Testing

The auditor samples employee training records.

The organization reports:

**99% completion**

However, interviews reveal several employees cannot explain the incident-reporting procedure.

The auditor therefore distinguishes:

**Training completion**

from:

**Actual awareness effectiveness.**

This is an important GRC lesson.

---

# 33. Step 16 – Audit Findings Classification

The audit team classifies observations.

### Conformity

Requirement is satisfied.

### Observation

Potential improvement opportunity.

### Minor Nonconformity

A limited failure to meet a requirement.

### Major Nonconformity

A significant failure affecting the ability of the ISMS to achieve intended outcomes.

Organizations may use different internal classification terminology, but the underlying principle remains:

**Severity should reflect risk and the nature of the conformity gap.**

---

After the audit:

| Result                    | Number |
| ------------------------- | -----: |
| Conforming areas          |     31 |
| Observations              |      5 |
| Minor findings            |      3 |
| Major findings            |      0 |

---

# 35. Step 17 – Audit Closing Meeting

The lead auditor presents:

* audit objective;
* scope;
* methodology;
* positive observations;
* findings;
* risk implications;
* required corrective actions.

The auditor avoids presenting unverified speculation.

All findings are supported by evidence.

---

# 36. Example Finding

### Finding

Privileged-access reviews were not consistently completed within the defined quarterly timeframe.

### Evidence

2 of 30 sampled accounts had reviews completed late.

### Requirement

Organizational access-review procedure.

### Risk

Delayed identification of unnecessary privileged access.

### Classification

Minor nonconformity.

---

# 37. Management Response

Management accepts the finding.

The IAM Manager proposes:

> "We will remind reviewers more frequently."

The auditor asks whether this addresses the root cause.

The GRC Manager proposes a stronger solution:

* automated reminders;
* escalation;
* dashboard monitoring;
* clear ownership;
* monthly management reporting.

This is more sustainable.

---

The finding enters the corrective-action process.

# 39. Step 19 – Management Review Integration

Audit results are presented during management review.

Management considers:

* findings;
* risk trends;
* control performance;
* incidents;
* security objectives;
* resource requirements.

Management approves:

* additional IAM automation;
* supplier monitoring improvements;
* security-awareness enhancements.

---

# 40. Step 20 – Certification Readiness

The internal audit provides management with an independent assessment before surveillance.

The CISO concludes:

### Overall ISMS Status

**Generally effective**

### Critical Risk

None identified.

### Priority Improvements

* privileged-access review automation;
* supplier monitoring;
* awareness effectiveness;
* evidence consistency.

The organization enters the surveillance audit with a significantly stronger understanding of its actual ISMS condition.

---

# 41. Internal Audit vs Certification Audit

The GRC team documents the distinction.

| Internal Audit                    | Certification Audit                     |
| --------------------------------- | --------------------------------------- |
| Conducted by/for the organization | Conducted by certification body         |
| Improvement-oriented              | Independent certification assurance     |
| Can be more exploratory           | Formal external assessment              |
| Identifies weaknesses early       | Determines conformity for certification |
| Supports management               | Provides certification assurance        |

The internal audit should not attempt to impersonate the certification body.

Its purpose is to provide **independent internal assurance**.

---

# 42. Lessons Learned

## Lesson 1 – Audit the Process, Not Just Documents

Documentation may say one thing while operations do another.

## Lesson 2 – Follow the Evidence

Auditors should trace claims back to objective evidence.

## Lesson 3 – Interview People

Employee understanding can reveal weaknesses invisible in documentation.

## Lesson 4 – Test Samples

Sampling provides practical assurance without testing every transaction.

---

# 43. Additional Lessons

## Lesson 5 – Test Effectiveness

A control existing on paper does not prove that it works.

## Lesson 6 – Follow Changes

New technology, suppliers and business processes can create new risks.

## Lesson 7 – Preserve Independence

People should not audit their own work wherever independence would be compromised.

## Lesson 8 – Use Findings for Improvement

The purpose of internal audit is not simply to produce a list of problems.

# 46. Case Study Conclusion

The IberiaCloud case demonstrates that an effective ISO 27001 internal audit is much more than a documentation checklist.

A mature internal audit evaluates the complete chain:

**Requirement → Process → Risk → Control → Operation → Evidence → Effectiveness → Finding → Corrective Action → Improvement**

The strongest internal audits do not ask only:

> **"Do you have the required document?"**

They ask:

> **"Show me how this process operates, show me the evidence, and demonstrate how you know the control is effective."**

For a GRC professional, this distinction is fundamental.

Internal audit provides the organization with an opportunity to discover weaknesses **before an external auditor, regulator, customer, or security incident exposes them**.

The ultimate purpose is therefore not simply to prepare for certification surveillance.

It is to provide management with **credible, risk-based assurance that the ISMS continues to operate effectively and support the organization's security objectives.**

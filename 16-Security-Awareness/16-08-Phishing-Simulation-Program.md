# Lesson 16.8 – Phishing Simulation Program

> **Chapter:** 16 – Security Awareness
>
> **Topic:** Phishing Simulation Program
>
> **Difficulty:** Beginner
>
> **Estimated Reading Time:** 10–15 minutes

## Introduction

Phishing is one of the most common ways attackers attempt to manipulate employees into revealing information, clicking malicious links, opening harmful attachments, transferring money, or providing access to systems.

Security awareness training can teach employees how to recognize phishing, but knowledge alone does not always translate into behavior. **Phishing simulations** provide organizations with a controlled way to evaluate how employees respond to realistic phishing scenarios and identify areas where additional awareness is needed.

A phishing simulation program should therefore be viewed as a **learning and risk-reduction activity**, not simply as a test designed to catch employees making mistakes.

## What Is a Phishing Simulation?

A phishing simulation is an authorized exercise in which an organization sends simulated phishing messages to employees without exposing them to an actual malicious payload.

The simulated message may resemble a real phishing attempt, such as:

* A password-reset notification.
* A delivery notification.
* An invoice request.
* A Microsoft 365 or other cloud-service alert.
* An HR communication.
* A request from a manager.
* A security notification.
* An unexpected document-sharing invitation.

The objective is to observe whether employees:

* Recognize suspicious indicators.
* Click simulated links.
* Open simulated attachments.
* Submit information to a simulated page.
* Report the message.
* Ignore the message.
* Follow the organization's security procedures.

The simulation should be carefully designed so that employees can learn from the experience without creating unnecessary operational or psychological harm.

## Purpose of Phishing Simulations

The primary purpose of phishing simulations is to **measure and improve employee resilience against phishing attacks**.

A mature program can help an organization:

1. Establish a baseline of phishing awareness.
2. Identify common employee vulnerabilities.
3. Measure improvement over time.
4. Reinforce security training.
5. Evaluate reporting behavior.
6. Identify departments or roles requiring additional support.
7. Improve security awareness content.
8. Provide management with meaningful human-risk metrics.

The goal should not be to produce a report stating how many employees "failed."

Instead, the organization should ask:

> **What can we learn from employee behavior, and how can we reduce the risk of real phishing attacks?**

## Why Phishing Simulations Are Important

Traditional security awareness training might explain:

> "Do not click suspicious links."

An employee may understand the statement but still click a convincing message when under pressure.

A simulation provides an opportunity to observe behavior in a controlled environment.

For example:

**Training:** Employee learns how to identify suspicious links.

**Simulation:** Employee receives a realistic simulated phishing email.

**Behavior:** Employee clicks the simulated link.

**Feedback:** Employee immediately receives an explanation of the warning signs.

**Reinforcement:** Employee completes a short learning activity.

**Future measurement:** The employee encounters another simulation later.

This creates a continuous learning cycle.

## Simulated Phishing vs. Real Phishing

A phishing simulation should be clearly controlled by the organization or its authorized security awareness provider.

A real phishing attack may attempt to:

* Steal credentials.
* Install malware.
* Capture financial information.
* Compromise accounts.
* Establish unauthorized access.
* Steal sensitive data.

A properly designed simulation should not expose employees or the organization to these real consequences.

Instead, the exercise should reproduce selected characteristics of phishing while keeping the environment controlled.

## Common Types of Simulated Phishing

Organizations can simulate different phishing scenarios.

### Credential Harvesting

A simulated message may direct an employee to a fake login page.

The simulation can determine whether the employee attempts to enter credentials.

The system should be designed so that real passwords are not collected or retained.

### Malicious Attachment Simulation

A simulated email may contain an attachment that appears suspicious or unexpected.

The exercise can determine whether employees attempt to open it.

The simulation should never deliver actual malware.

### Business Email Compromise

A simulated message may appear to come from:

* A senior executive.
* A manager.
* A finance employee.
* A trusted supplier.

The purpose is to test whether employees verify unusual requests before acting.

### Urgent Requests

Attackers often create a sense of urgency.

A simulation might involve:

> "Your account will be disabled today unless you verify your information."

This tests whether employees respond to pressure without first verifying the message.

### Document-Sharing Notifications

A simulated message may claim that someone has shared an important document.

The employee must determine whether the notification is legitimate before interacting with it.

## Realistic but Responsible Simulations

An effective phishing simulation should be realistic enough to test behavior but carefully controlled.

There is an important difference between:

**Realistic**

and

**Needlessly harmful**

For example, an organization might simulate a suspicious password-reset request.

However, using highly sensitive personal events, family emergencies, layoffs, health situations, or other emotionally distressing scenarios may be inappropriate.

The objective is to measure security behavior, not to manipulate employees through unnecessary emotional distress.

## Employee Trust and Transparency

Phishing simulations require careful consideration of employee trust.

If employees believe that the security team is deliberately trying to embarrass or punish them, they may become resistant to future security activities.

The program should therefore communicate that:

* Simulations are authorized.
* Their purpose is education and risk reduction.
* Employees are not being personally targeted for punishment.
* Mistakes are opportunities to learn.
* Reporting suspicious messages is encouraged.
* Results will be handled appropriately.

The exact level of advance notification depends on the organization's simulation strategy. Some organizations may provide general awareness that simulations occur without revealing when they will happen.

## Learning From a Simulation

The most valuable part of a phishing simulation may occur **after the employee interacts with the simulated message**.

For example, if an employee clicks a simulated phishing link, the organization may immediately display an educational page explaining:

* That the message was a simulation.
* Which indicators were suspicious.
* What the employee should have checked.
* How to report similar messages.
* Where to obtain additional training.

This turns the event into a learning opportunity.

### Example

A simulated email states:

> "Your corporate password expires today. Click here immediately to prevent account suspension."

The employee clicks the link.

The simulation could explain:

**Warning signs:**

* Urgent language.
* Threat of account suspension.
* Unexpected request.
* Suspicious link.
* Pressure to act immediately.

**Recommended behavior:**

* Do not click the link.
* Access the service through the normal approved portal.
* Report the message using the organization's phishing-reporting mechanism.

This provides immediate reinforcement.

## Reporting Behavior

Click rate is only one measure of phishing resilience.

A mature simulation program should also evaluate whether employees **report suspicious messages**.

For example:

| Behavior                        | Security Significance             |
| ------------------------------- | --------------------------------- |
| Clicked simulated link          | Potential vulnerability           |
| Submitted simulated information | Higher-risk behavior              |
| Opened simulated attachment     | Potential vulnerability           |
| Ignored message                 | Neutral/limited evidence          |
| Deleted message                 | Appropriate in some circumstances |
| Reported message                | Positive security behavior        |

Reporting behavior is particularly important because employees can become an additional detection mechanism for the organization's security team.

An employee who recognizes and reports a real phishing attempt may help prevent other employees from becoming victims.

## Psychological Safety

Security awareness programs should avoid creating a culture where employees are afraid to report mistakes.

Consider an employee who accidentally clicks a suspicious link.

If the employee expects punishment, they may avoid reporting the incident.

That can make the situation worse.

A stronger security culture encourages the employee to:

**Stop → Report → Seek Help → Learn**

This allows the security team to investigate potential exposure and provide support.

## Frequency of Simulations

There is no universal frequency that is appropriate for every organization.

The frequency should consider:

* Organizational risk.
* Industry.
* Workforce size.
* Threat environment.
* Previous simulation results.
* Employee roles.
* Regulatory requirements.
* Security awareness maturity.

Running simulations too rarely may provide insufficient information.

Running them excessively may cause employees to become frustrated or treat every email as suspicious.

The goal should be **meaningful measurement and reinforcement**, not simply maximizing the number of campaigns.

## GRC Perspective

From a GRC perspective, phishing simulations can provide valuable evidence about human-related security risk.

A typical process may look like:

**Risk Identification → Simulation → Measurement → Training → Reassessment → Improvement**

The organization identifies phishing as a relevant risk, conducts controlled simulations, measures employee behavior, provides additional training, and then reassesses performance.

Potential evidence includes:

* Phishing simulation policy.
* Program objectives.
* Campaign plans.
* Employee participation records.
* Simulation results.
* Reporting metrics.
* Training assignments.
* Corrective actions.
* Management reports.
* Trend analysis.

This information can contribute to broader human-risk and security awareness reporting.

## What Phishing Simulations Should Not Become

A phishing simulation program should not become a competition to see how many employees can be caught.

It should not be primarily used to:

* Shame employees.
* Publicly identify individuals who clicked.
* Punish mistakes.
* Create fear.
* Demonstrate that the security team is "smarter" than employees.

Instead, the program should help the organization answer:

> **Where are our human-related weaknesses, and what can we do to improve them?**

## Key Takeaways

Phishing simulations provide a controlled method for measuring and improving employee resilience against phishing attacks.

The key principles are:

1. **Use simulations to measure and improve behavior.**
2. **Keep simulations authorized and controlled.**
3. **Use realistic scenarios without unnecessary psychological manipulation.**
4. **Measure reporting as well as clicking behavior.**
5. **Provide immediate educational feedback.**
6. **Use results to identify training and risk areas.**
7. **Protect employee trust and psychological safety.**
8. **Avoid using simulations primarily for punishment or embarrassment.**
9. **Repeat simulations appropriately to measure improvement over time.**
10. **Connect simulation results to the broader security awareness and GRC program.**

A mature phishing simulation program treats every simulated interaction as an opportunity to learn. The ultimate objective is not to achieve a low simulation click rate for its own sake, but to develop employees who can **recognize, resist, and report real phishing attacks**.

## Planning and Executing Campaigns

A successful phishing simulation program requires more than sending a simulated phishing email to employees. Each campaign should be **planned, authorized, controlled, measured, and followed by appropriate learning activities**.

Poorly planned simulations can create confusion, damage employee trust, disrupt business operations, or produce misleading results. A structured campaign helps ensure that the exercise provides useful information about the organization's human-related security risk.

### Establish the Campaign Objective

Before creating a simulation, the organization should define what it wants to learn or improve.

Possible objectives include:

* Establishing a baseline phishing susceptibility rate.
* Measuring whether employees can identify suspicious emails.
* Increasing phishing reporting rates.
* Testing specific employee groups.
* Evaluating the effectiveness of recent training.
* Measuring improvement from previous campaigns.
* Testing awareness of a particular phishing technique.

For example:

> **Objective:** Determine whether employees are more likely to report simulated phishing emails after completing the new phishing awareness training.

A clearly defined objective makes it easier to select the appropriate scenario and metrics.

### Define the Scope

The organization should determine who will participate in the campaign.

Possible scopes include:

* Entire workforce.
* Selected departments.
* New employees.
* High-risk departments.
* Executives.
* Remote workers.
* Specific geographic locations.
* Employees with previous phishing-related weaknesses.

A campaign may begin with a limited group before expanding across the organization.

Scope should also define what systems, applications, domains, and communication channels may be used.

### Obtain Authorization

Phishing simulations should always be properly authorized.

This is particularly important because simulated emails may resemble real attacks and could potentially trigger security systems or cause employees to report the simulation as a genuine incident.

Authorization should establish:

* Who approved the campaign.
* Who is responsible for execution.
* Which employees are included.
* What types of simulations are permitted.
* What information may be collected.
* How results will be handled.
* What systems may be used.
* What actions are prohibited.

The organization should also ensure that the exercise does not conflict with legal, privacy, employment, or contractual requirements.

### Establish Rules of Engagement

A formal **Rules of Engagement (RoE)** document can define how the campaign will operate.

It may specify:

* Campaign dates.
* Target population.
* Approved scenarios.
* Approved sending infrastructure.
* Simulation domains.
* Allowed landing pages.
* Data that may be collected.
* Data that must never be collected.
* Escalation procedures.
* Emergency stop procedures.
* Responsible personnel.

For example, a simulation may record whether an employee clicked a link but must not collect the employee's actual password.

Clear rules reduce the possibility of the simulation accidentally becoming a real security incident.

### Select the Phishing Scenario

The scenario should reflect realistic threats faced by the organization.

Possible scenarios include:

**Credential phishing**

A simulated request to verify an account.

**Business email compromise**

A simulated request from a manager or executive.

**Invoice fraud**

A simulated request involving an invoice or payment.

**Cloud-service notification**

A simulated notification about a shared document or account.

**Delivery notification**

A simulated package or delivery message.

**HR-related phishing**

A simulated employee-related communication.

The scenario should be selected based on the organization's threat environment and risk assessment.

### Use Risk-Based Scenarios

Organizations should avoid selecting scenarios simply because they are interesting.

Instead, campaign scenarios should reflect relevant risks.

For example, if an organization has experienced business email compromise attempts targeting finance employees, a simulation involving a payment-related request may provide more useful information than a generic password-reset scenario.

This creates a direct relationship between:

**Threat Intelligence → Risk Assessment → Simulation Scenario → Measurement**

### Establish a Baseline

Before evaluating improvement, organizations need to understand their current performance.

A baseline campaign can measure indicators such as:

* Click rate.
* Attachment interaction rate.
* Credential submission rate.
* Reporting rate.
* Time to report.
* Department-level performance.

The baseline provides a point of comparison for future campaigns.

For example:

> Initial reporting rate: 18%

After several awareness activities:

> Reporting rate: 34%

This provides evidence that employee reporting behavior may be improving.

### Campaign Design

The simulation should be designed to resemble a realistic phishing attempt without becoming unnecessarily deceptive or harmful.

Design considerations include:

* Sender identity.
* Subject line.
* Message content.
* Call to action.
* Link destination.
* Timing.
* Branding.
* Scenario relevance.
* Landing-page behavior.

The more realistic the simulation, the more useful the behavioral measurement may be. However, realism should remain within the organization's approved rules and ethical boundaries.

### Avoid Excessive Personalization

Modern phishing attacks can use publicly available information to create highly convincing messages.

Simulation programs may therefore consider personalization, but organizations should establish appropriate boundaries.

For example, using an employee's job role may be reasonable:

> "Finance team: Please review the attached invoice."

Using sensitive personal information purely to increase the likelihood that an employee will click may be inappropriate.

The objective is to test security behavior, not to exploit personal vulnerabilities.

### Technical Preparation

Before launching a campaign, the technical team should verify that the simulation infrastructure is functioning correctly.

Preparation may include:

* Configuring approved sending domains.
* Configuring email authentication.
* Testing links.
* Configuring landing pages.
* Testing tracking mechanisms.
* Confirming that security tools will not unnecessarily block the campaign.
* Establishing reporting mechanisms.
* Testing data collection.

The simulation should be tested internally before being sent to the broader workforce.

### Coordination With Security Teams

Phishing simulations can interact with existing security controls.

For example, an organization's email security platform may identify the simulation as suspicious and automatically quarantine it.

This is not necessarily a problem, but the campaign team needs to understand how the security controls will behave.

Relevant teams may include:

* Security operations.
* Email security administrators.
* IT operations.
* Security awareness.
* GRC.
* Human resources.
* Legal or privacy teams.
* Communications.

Appropriate coordination helps prevent unnecessary operational disruption.

### Avoiding Accidental Incidents

A poorly designed simulation can accidentally resemble a genuine malicious campaign too closely.

For example, a simulation could trigger:

* Incident response procedures.
* Account lockouts.
* Security alerts.
* Help-desk overload.
* Employee panic.
* External security notifications.

Testing should therefore be performed before launch.

The campaign should also have an emergency mechanism that allows authorized personnel to stop it if unexpected problems occur.

### Campaign Timing

Timing can significantly influence simulation results.

Factors to consider include:

* Business hours.
* Employee schedules.
* Holidays.
* Major organizational events.
* System maintenance.
* Financial closing periods.
* Major operational activities.

For example, launching a complex phishing simulation during a critical business operation may create unnecessary disruption.

At the same time, organizations should avoid always sending simulations at predictable times.

Real attackers do not necessarily operate according to an organization's training calendar.

### Staggered Campaigns

Large organizations may use staggered campaigns rather than sending the simulation to everyone simultaneously.

For example:

**Week 1:** Finance

**Week 2:** Operations

**Week 3:** HR

**Week 4:** General workforce

This can make campaign management easier and allow the security team to analyze results between groups.

However, segmentation should be designed carefully so that employees do not simply learn to expect simulations in a predictable pattern.

### Landing Pages and Immediate Feedback

If an employee interacts with a simulated phishing message, the simulation may redirect them to an educational landing page.

The page can explain:

* That the message was simulated.
* What warning signs were present.
* What the employee should have checked.
* How to report suspicious emails.
* Where to obtain additional training.

For example:

> **This was a security awareness simulation.**

> The message contained several warning signs, including an urgent request and a suspicious link. In a real attack, interacting with the message could have exposed your account.

Immediate feedback creates a learning opportunity while the experience is still fresh.

### Reporting Mechanism

Employees should have a simple way to report suspicious emails.

This might be:

* A "Report Phishing" button.
* A dedicated email address.
* A security reporting portal.
* A collaboration-platform reporting function.
* A help-desk process.

The reporting process should be easier than forwarding the suspicious email manually to multiple people.

The organization should also make clear what happens after a report is submitted.

### Measuring Time to Report

The time between receiving a phishing message and reporting it can provide useful information.

For example:

> Employee receives simulation at 10:00.

> Employee reports it at 10:04.

This may demonstrate rapid recognition and reporting.

Time-to-report can become particularly valuable when the organization is trying to determine how quickly employees can help security teams identify potential phishing campaigns.

### Post-Campaign Communication

After the campaign, employees should receive appropriate communication.

The communication may explain:

* That a simulation occurred.
* What the organization learned.
* Which warning signs employees should recognize.
* How many employees reported the message.
* What improvements are expected.

Organizations should be cautious about publishing individual employee results.

The focus should remain on **collective learning and improvement**.

### Remediation and Follow-Up Training

Employees who demonstrate difficulty recognizing simulated phishing should receive additional support.

This could include:

* Microlearning.
* Targeted eLearning.
* Additional simulations.
* Short coaching sessions.
* Security awareness reminders.

The response should be proportionate to the risk and organizational policy.

For example:

**Simulation interaction → Educational feedback → Additional training → Future reassessment**

This is more useful than simply recording that the employee "failed."

### Repeat Testing

A single simulation provides limited information.

A stronger program conducts campaigns over time and compares results.

For example:

| Campaign   | Click Rate | Reporting Rate |
| ---------- | ---------: | -------------: |
| Baseline   |        22% |            15% |
| Campaign 2 |        17% |            23% |
| Campaign 3 |        12% |            31% |
| Campaign 4 |         9% |            39% |

The trend can provide evidence of improving resilience.

However, organizations should also examine whether the simulations themselves differ in difficulty. A lower click rate does not necessarily mean employees improved if the later simulation was significantly easier.

### Avoiding Predictable Campaigns

If employees learn that simulations always occur at the same time or use the same types of messages, they may adapt to the testing process rather than becoming better at recognizing real phishing.

For example, if employees know that every simulation contains a fake password-reset message, they may become good at identifying that specific scenario without developing broader phishing awareness.

Campaigns should therefore vary appropriately across:

* Scenarios.
* Sender types.
* Message themes.
* Urgency levels.
* Attack techniques.
* Business contexts.

### GRC Perspective

From a GRC perspective, campaign planning should demonstrate that phishing simulations are governed activities rather than ad hoc exercises.

A mature process may follow:

**Risk Assessment → Objective → Scope → Authorization → Rules of Engagement → Campaign → Measurement → Training → Reassessment**

Potential evidence includes:

* Approved campaign plan.
* Rules of engagement.
* Risk assessment.
* Target population.
* Scenario documentation.
* Technical test results.
* Campaign results.
* Training assignments.
* Corrective actions.
* Management reporting.

This documentation can demonstrate that the organization has a structured approach to managing human-related phishing risk.

### Key Takeaways

Effective phishing simulation campaigns require careful planning and controlled execution.

The key principles are:

1. **Define a clear campaign objective.**
2. **Use risk-based scenarios.**
3. **Obtain appropriate authorization.**
4. **Establish clear rules of engagement.**
5. **Protect employee information and avoid collecting real credentials.**
6. **Test the simulation before deployment.**
7. **Coordinate with security and operational teams.**
8. **Provide immediate educational feedback.**
9. **Provide additional training where necessary.**
10. **Compare results across multiple campaigns.**
11. **Vary scenarios appropriately to avoid predictable testing.**
12. **Document the campaign for governance and audit purposes.**

A well-designed phishing simulation is not simply an email campaign. It is a **controlled security exercise that connects risk identification, employee behavior, awareness training, measurement, and continuous improvement**.

**Simulation interaction → Educational feedback → Additional training → Future reassessment**

This is more useful than simply recording that the employee "failed."

### Repeat Testing

A single simulation provides limited information.

A stronger program conducts campaigns over time and compares results.

For example:

| Campaign   | Click Rate | Reporting Rate |
| ---------- | ---------: | -------------: |
| Baseline   |        22% |            15% |
| Campaign 2 |        17% |            23% |
| Campaign 3 |        12% |            31% |
| Campaign 4 |         9% |            39% |

The trend can provide evidence of improving resilience.

However, organizations should also examine whether the simulations themselves differ in difficulty. A lower click rate does not necessarily mean employees improved if the later simulation was significantly easier.

### Avoiding Predictable Campaigns

If employees learn that simulations always occur at the same time or use the same types of messages, they may adapt to the testing process rather than becoming better at recognizing real phishing.

For example, if employees know that every simulation contains a fake password-reset message, they may become good at identifying that specific scenario without developing broader phishing awareness.

Campaigns should therefore vary appropriately across:

* Scenarios.
* Sender types.
* Message themes.
* Urgency levels.
* Attack techniques.
* Business contexts.

### GRC Perspective

From a GRC perspective, campaign planning should demonstrate that phishing simulations are governed activities rather than ad hoc exercises.

A mature process may follow:

**Risk Assessment → Objective → Scope → Authorization → Rules of Engagement → Campaign → Measurement → Training → Reassessment**

Potential evidence includes:

* Approved campaign plan.
* Rules of engagement.
* Risk assessment.
* Target population.
* Scenario documentation.
* Technical test results.
* Campaign results.
* Training assignments.
* Corrective actions.
* Management reporting.

This documentation can demonstrate that the organization has a structured approach to managing human-related phishing risk.

### Key Takeaways

Effective phishing simulation campaigns require careful planning and controlled execution.

The key principles are:

1. **Define a clear campaign objective.**
2. **Use risk-based scenarios.**
3. **Obtain appropriate authorization.**
4. **Establish clear rules of engagement.**
5. **Protect employee information and avoid collecting real credentials.**
6. **Test the simulation before deployment.**
7. **Coordinate with security and operational teams.**
8. **Provide immediate educational feedback.**
9. **Provide additional training where necessary.**
10. **Compare results across multiple campaigns.**
11. **Vary scenarios appropriately to avoid predictable testing.**
12. **Document the campaign for governance and audit purposes.**

A well-designed phishing simulation is not simply an email campaign. It is a **controlled security exercise that connects risk identification, employee behavior, awareness training, measurement, and continuous improvement**.

## Measuring User Performance

Measuring user performance is an important part of a phishing simulation program because organizations need to determine whether employees are becoming more capable of recognizing, resisting, and reporting phishing attempts.

Simply conducting simulations is not enough. The organization needs meaningful metrics that show **what employees did, how behavior is changing, and where additional security awareness efforts are needed**.

The objective should be to measure security behavior without turning the program into a system for embarrassing or punishing employees.

### What Should Be Measured?

A phishing simulation can generate several types of performance information.

Common measures include:

* Email open rate.
* Link click rate.
* Attachment interaction rate.
* Simulated credential submission rate.
* Reporting rate.
* Time to report.
* Repeat failure rate.
* Training completion after a simulation.
* Improvement across campaigns.

Each metric provides a different perspective on employee behavior.

### Click Rate

The **click rate** measures the percentage of employees who interact with a simulated phishing link.

For example:

> 1,000 employees receive a simulation.
> 120 employees click the simulated link.
> Click rate = 12%.

A decreasing click rate over multiple campaigns may indicate improved phishing awareness.

However, click rate should not be treated as the only measure of performance.

An employee may click a link but immediately recognize the mistake and report it. Another employee may not click the link but also fail to report the suspicious message.

These behaviors have different security implications.

### Credential Submission Rate

Some simulations use controlled landing pages to determine whether employees attempt to submit information.

For example, an employee may be presented with a simulated login page.

The simulation can measure whether the employee attempts to enter information without collecting real passwords.

This metric can be more significant than simple link clicking because entering information into a phishing page demonstrates a potentially higher-risk behavior.

However, organizations should be extremely careful about what information is collected.

**Real passwords, authentication codes, or sensitive personal information should not be collected as part of a standard awareness simulation.**

### Reporting Rate

The **reporting rate** measures how many employees report the simulated phishing message.

For example:

> 1,000 employees receive a simulation.
> 350 employees report it.
> Reporting rate = 35%.

A higher reporting rate generally indicates stronger employee participation in the organization's phishing detection process.

Reporting is particularly valuable because employees can act as an additional detection layer.

Instead of security teams identifying every phishing campaign independently, employees can help identify suspicious messages and alert the organization.

### Time to Report

Organizations may also measure how quickly employees report simulated phishing messages.

For example:

> Simulation delivered: 09:00
> First report: 09:03
> Majority of reports received: 09:20

Faster reporting can help the security team investigate and respond to real phishing campaigns more quickly.

Time-to-report can therefore provide insight into both employee behavior and the effectiveness of the organization's reporting process.

### Repeat Interaction

One useful measure is whether employees repeatedly interact with simulated phishing messages.

For example:

**Campaign 1:** Employee clicks.

**Campaign 2:** Employee clicks again.

**Campaign 3:** Employee reports the message.

This information can help identify whether additional awareness activities are producing improvement.

However, repeated interactions should be interpreted carefully. A different scenario may be more difficult than the previous one, so raw comparisons do not always tell the full story.

### First-Time vs. Repeat Participants

Organizations may distinguish between:

* Employees participating for the first time.
* Employees who have participated in previous campaigns.
* Employees who previously interacted with simulations.
* Employees who previously reported simulations.

This allows the organization to determine whether awareness is improving among employees who have already received training.

### Performance by Department

Organizations may analyze results by organizational group.

For example:

| Department | Click Rate | Reporting Rate |
| ---------- | ---------: | -------------: |
| Finance    |        14% |            32% |
| HR         |        10% |            38% |
| Operations |        18% |            24% |
| IT         |         6% |            51% |

These results can help identify areas requiring additional awareness support.

However, department-level comparisons should be interpreted carefully.

A department receiving a more difficult simulation may naturally have a higher click rate.

### Performance by Role

Different roles may face different phishing risks.

For example:

**Finance**

May be heavily targeted by payment fraud and business email compromise.

**Executives**

May be targeted by highly personalized attacks.

**HR**

May receive malicious resumes, employee-related requests, and personal information attacks.

**IT**

May receive technical support impersonation or credential-related attacks.

Role-based analysis can therefore provide more useful information than simply calculating one organization-wide number.

### Global and Regional Analysis

Multinational organizations may also analyze performance across geographic regions.

For example:

* Europe.
* North America.
* Middle East.
* Asia-Pacific.

However, organizations should consider differences in:

* Language.
* Culture.
* Training content.
* Local business practices.
* Working environments.
* Phishing scenario relevance.

A difference in performance does not automatically indicate that one workforce is less security-aware.

### Risk-Based Interpretation

Metrics should be interpreted in the context of organizational risk.

For example:

> A 10% click rate may appear low.

But if those clicks primarily involve employees with privileged administrative access, the risk could still be significant.

Conversely:

> A 15% click rate among employees with limited system access may have a different risk impact.

This is why security awareness metrics should eventually connect employee behavior to **business and cybersecurity risk**.

### Measuring Improvement

The most useful analysis often involves trends over time.

For example:

| Campaign   | Click Rate | Reporting Rate |
| ---------- | ---------: | -------------: |
| Baseline   |        24% |            16% |
| Campaign 2 |        19% |            23% |
| Campaign 3 |        14% |            30% |
| Campaign 4 |        10% |            37% |

The trend suggests that employees may be becoming more resilient.

However, the organization should also consider:

* Whether the scenarios had comparable difficulty.
* Whether training was delivered between campaigns.
* Whether employee populations changed.
* Whether the campaign timing affected behavior.
* Whether reporting mechanisms changed.

### Training Effectiveness

Simulation results can be used to evaluate whether awareness training is working.

For example:

**Before training**

Click rate: 21%

**After training**

Click rate: 13%

This may suggest improvement.

But a stronger analysis would examine several indicators:

* Click rate decreased.
* Reporting rate increased.
* Time to report decreased.
* Repeat failures decreased.

Multiple indicators provide stronger evidence of behavioral improvement.

### Measuring the Reporting Culture

An organization should encourage employees to report suspicious messages even if they are uncertain.

For example, an employee may think:

> "I'm not sure whether this email is malicious, but something does not look right."

The desired behavior is to report it rather than ignore it.

Security teams can then investigate the message.

This creates a security culture in which employees contribute to threat detection.

### Avoiding Vanity Metrics

A **vanity metric** is a number that looks impressive but does not provide much information about security effectiveness.

For example:

> "98% of employees completed phishing training."

This demonstrates participation but does not demonstrate that employees are better at recognizing phishing.

Similarly:

> "10,000 employees received our awareness email."

This demonstrates reach but not necessarily behavioral improvement.

More meaningful measurements may include:

* Reduction in phishing interaction.
* Increase in reporting.
* Faster reporting.
* Reduced repeat failures.
* Improved performance among high-risk roles.

### Measuring Human Risk

Phishing performance can contribute to broader **human risk measurement**.

For example:

**Training data**

→ Did the employee receive the required training?

**Simulation data**

→ How did the employee respond?

**Reporting data**

→ Did the employee identify and report suspicious activity?

**Incident data**

→ Was the employee involved in a real security event?

**Risk analysis**

→ What level of human-related risk remains?

This provides a more complete view of organizational exposure.

### Individual vs. Organizational Measurement

Organizations should carefully distinguish between individual performance and program-level measurement.

Individual results may be useful for:

* Targeted training.
* Coaching.
* Risk analysis.
* Identifying repeated behavior patterns.

However, publishing individual employee results can damage trust and discourage reporting.

Program-level reporting is often more appropriate for executive audiences.

For example:

> "The organization achieved a 42% phishing reporting rate, representing a 15 percentage-point improvement from the previous campaign."

This provides useful information without publicly identifying employees.

### Privacy Considerations

Phishing simulation results may be associated with individual employees and can therefore raise privacy and employee-monitoring considerations.

Organizations should establish clear requirements for:

* What information is collected.
* Why it is collected.
* Who can access it.
* How long it is retained.
* How it is reported.
* Whether individual results are shared with managers.
* How results are protected.

The principle of **data minimization** should be applied.

The organization should collect information necessary to achieve the legitimate security objective rather than collecting unnecessary personal information.

### GRC Perspective

From a GRC perspective, phishing performance measurement supports the organization's ability to demonstrate that awareness controls are being evaluated.

A useful measurement process is:

**Simulation → Data Collection → Analysis → Risk Interpretation → Training → Reassessment**

Potential evidence includes:

* Campaign metrics.
* Reporting statistics.
* Trend analysis.
* Department-level results.
* Training records.
* Corrective actions.
* Risk assessments.
* Management dashboards.
* Program improvement records.

GRC professionals should ensure that metrics are connected to defined objectives and risk indicators.

### Example of a Performance Dashboard

A security awareness dashboard might show:

| Metric                 | Current | Previous | Trend     |
| ---------------------- | ------: | -------: | --------- |
| Click rate             |      9% |      14% | Improving |
| Reporting rate         |     41% |      30% | Improving |
| Credential submission  |      2% |       5% | Improving |
| Average time to report |  18 min |   27 min | Improving |
| Repeat interaction     |      7% |      11% | Improving |

This provides management with a clearer picture of program performance than simply reporting training completion.

### Common Measurement Mistakes

Organizations should avoid several common mistakes.

**Using click rate as the only KPI**

Clicking is important, but reporting behavior is also critical.

**Comparing campaigns with different difficulty levels**

A harder simulation may naturally produce different results.

**Ignoring reporting behavior**

Employees who report phishing can become an important defensive layer.

**Publicly shaming individuals**

This can damage trust and discourage reporting.

**Collecting unnecessary personal information**

Simulation data should be limited to legitimate security objectives.

**Focusing only on short-term results**

A single successful campaign does not prove long-term behavioral change.

**Failing to act on the results**

Metrics have little value if the organization does not use them to improve training and controls.

### Key Takeaways

Measuring user performance allows organizations to determine whether phishing simulations are producing meaningful improvements.

The key principles are:

1. **Measure multiple behaviors, not just clicks.**
2. **Track reporting rates and time to report.**
3. **Monitor performance trends over time.**
4. **Analyze high-risk departments and roles where appropriate.**
5. **Interpret metrics in the context of business risk.**
6. **Distinguish training participation from behavioral effectiveness.**
7. **Protect employee privacy and avoid unnecessary data collection.**
8. **Use results to provide targeted education and reinforcement.**
9. **Report meaningful trends to management rather than vanity metrics.**
10. **Use measurement results to continuously improve the phishing simulation program.**

The ultimate measure of success is not simply whether employees can pass a simulated phishing test. It is whether they increasingly **recognize suspicious activity, avoid risky actions, report potential attacks quickly, and contribute to the organization's overall ability to detect and respond to phishing threats**.

## Continuous Improvement Through Simulations

A phishing simulation program should not be treated as a series of isolated tests. Its greatest value comes from using the results of each campaign to **improve employee behavior, awareness content, security controls, and the overall human-risk management program**.

The objective is to create a continuous improvement cycle:

**Simulate → Measure → Analyze → Educate → Improve → Simulate Again**

Each campaign should provide information that helps the organization make the next campaign and the broader security awareness program more effective.

### Why Continuous Improvement Matters

A single phishing simulation provides only a snapshot of employee behavior.

For example, an organization may discover that:

> 18% of employees clicked the simulated phishing link.

This tells the organization something about current behavior, but it does not explain:

* Why employees clicked.
* Which phishing techniques were most effective.
* Which roles were most vulnerable.
* Whether training was effective.
* Whether reporting mechanisms are working.
* Whether employees improve over time.

Continuous improvement turns simulation results into actionable security improvements.

### The Continuous Improvement Cycle

A mature program can use the following cycle:

**1. Simulate**

Conduct an authorized phishing campaign.

**2. Measure**

Collect relevant performance metrics.

**3. Analyze**

Identify patterns, weaknesses, and trends.

**4. Educate**

Provide targeted training and reinforcement.

**5. Improve**

Modify awareness content, processes, and controls.

**6. Reassess**

Conduct future simulations to determine whether performance improved.

This creates a feedback loop between employee behavior and security awareness activities.

### Analyze the Results

After each campaign, the security awareness team should analyze the results.

Questions may include:

* Which scenarios generated the highest interaction rate?
* Which departments performed poorly?
* Which roles demonstrated strong reporting behavior?
* How quickly did employees report the simulation?
* Did employees who received additional training improve?
* Were there repeated patterns of failure?
* Did the campaign reveal weaknesses in existing processes?

The analysis should go beyond simply reporting the overall click rate.

### Identify Root Causes

A high phishing interaction rate does not automatically mean that employees lack security awareness.

There may be other contributing factors.

For example:

**Problem:** Employees frequently click simulated password-reset links.

Possible causes could include:

* Employees are accustomed to receiving legitimate password notifications.
* The organization's password-reset process is confusing.
* The simulated message closely resembles legitimate company communications.
* Employees have not been trained on the correct password-reset process.
* Employees are under time pressure.
* The reporting mechanism is difficult to use.

Understanding the root cause allows the organization to select a more effective corrective action.

### Adjust Training Content

Simulation results can reveal areas where existing training is not sufficiently effective.

For example, if employees repeatedly fail to identify:

* Suspicious URLs.
* Impersonation attempts.
* Urgent requests.
* Fake document-sharing notifications.

the organization can introduce targeted microlearning covering those specific behaviors.

This creates a direct relationship between:

**Simulation Results → Training Content**

rather than continuing to deliver generic annual training.

### Targeted Remediation

Employees or groups demonstrating higher-risk behavior may receive additional training.

Possible remediation activities include:

* Microlearning.
* Short eLearning modules.
* Scenario-based exercises.
* Additional phishing simulations.
* Coaching.
* Security awareness workshops.

The objective should be to **improve behavior**, not simply punish the employee.

For example:

> Employee interacts with simulation → Immediate feedback → Targeted learning → Future reassessment.

This provides a measurable improvement cycle.

### Adaptive Simulations

Future phishing simulations can be adjusted based on previous results.

For example, suppose an organization discovers that employees are highly resistant to generic phishing but struggle with executive impersonation.

The next campaign could focus on:

**Executive Impersonation → Verification Behavior → Reporting**

If employees subsequently improve, the organization can move to another relevant scenario.

This allows the simulation program to evolve according to actual organizational weaknesses.

### Increasing Difficulty Carefully

A mature program may gradually increase the sophistication of simulations.

For example:

**Level 1:** Obvious phishing indicators.

**Level 2:** Moderate phishing indicators.

**Level 3:** More realistic business communication.

**Level 4:** Sophisticated impersonation or social engineering scenarios.

The purpose is not to make every simulation increasingly difficult simply to increase failure rates.

Difficulty should be aligned with the organization's threat environment and learning objectives.

### Avoiding the "Gotcha" Mentality

One of the biggest risks in phishing simulations is creating a **"gotcha" culture**.

Employees may begin to feel that the security team is trying to trick them.

This can result in:

* Reduced trust.
* Lower participation.
* Fear of reporting mistakes.
* Employees avoiding communication with security teams.
* Negative perceptions of security awareness.

A better approach is to position simulations as:

> **Practice for real-world attacks.**

Employees should understand that the organization is giving them an opportunity to develop skills before they encounter a real attack.

### Learning From Successful Employees

Organizations should not focus only on failures.

Employees who correctly identify and report simulations demonstrate desirable security behavior.

The organization can analyze:

* What characteristics they recognized.
* How quickly they reported the message.
* Which training they previously completed.
* Whether certain departments demonstrate stronger practices.

This can help identify effective awareness approaches.

### Positive Reinforcement

Positive reinforcement can encourage desirable behavior.

For example, organizations may recognize:

* Security champions.
* Teams with strong reporting rates.
* Departments showing significant improvement.
* Employees who consistently report suspicious activity.

Recognition should be designed carefully so that it encourages participation without turning security into an unhealthy competition.

### Improving the Reporting Process

Simulation results may reveal that employees recognize phishing but do not know how to report it.

For example:

**Click rate:** 8%

**Reporting rate:** 12%

This may indicate that some employees are identifying suspicious messages but the reporting process is not widely used.

The organization could improve:

* The visibility of the reporting button.
* Employee instructions.
* Help-desk procedures.
* Security-team workflows.
* Automated reporting mechanisms.

This demonstrates an important principle:

> **Security awareness is not only about changing people; it is also about improving the environment in which people make security decisions.**

### Improving Security Controls

Simulation results can also identify opportunities to strengthen technical controls.

For example, if employees repeatedly receive messages that impersonate a particular cloud service, the organization might evaluate:

* Email security controls.
* Domain protection.
* Anti-phishing policies.
* URL filtering.
* Authentication controls.
* DMARC configuration.
* Security monitoring.

This means phishing simulation results can contribute to both **human controls and technical controls**.

### Connecting Simulations With Incident Response

Phishing simulations can also evaluate whether employees know what to do after interacting with suspicious content.

For example:

> Employee clicks suspicious link → Employee reports incident → Security team receives notification → Security team investigates.

This connects awareness behavior with the organization's incident response process.

The organization can determine whether employees understand:

* When to report.
* Where to report.
* What information to provide.
* What actions to take after clicking.
* How quickly they should report.

### Trend Analysis

Organizations should maintain historical simulation data.

This allows the security team to identify trends such as:

* Decreasing click rates.
* Increasing reporting rates.
* Faster reporting.
* Reduced repeat failures.
* Improved department performance.
* Persistent weaknesses in specific roles.

For example:

| Campaign | Click Rate | Reporting Rate |
| -------- | ---------: | -------------: |
| 1        |        24% |            15% |
| 2        |        19% |            21% |
| 3        |        15% |            28% |
| 4        |        11% |            36% |
| 5        |         9% |            42% |

The overall trend suggests improved phishing resilience.

However, the organization should ensure that campaign difficulty and employee populations are considered before concluding that the improvement is entirely attributable to training.

### Measuring Long-Term Behavior

The ultimate objective is sustainable behavioral change.

For example:

**Short-term result:**

Employees perform well immediately after training.

**Long-term result:**

Employees continue recognizing and reporting suspicious messages several months later.

The second result provides stronger evidence that the awareness program has produced lasting behavioral improvement.

Organizations can therefore conduct simulations at appropriate intervals to determine whether learning is retained.

### Continuous Improvement and the PDCA Model

The phishing simulation process can be aligned with the **Plan-Do-Check-Act (PDCA)** approach.

**Plan**

Define the risk, objective, scope, and simulation scenario.

**Do**

Conduct the phishing simulation and associated training.

**Check**

Analyze performance, trends, and behavioral outcomes.

**Act**

Improve training, processes, controls, and future campaigns.

The cycle then begins again.

This approach fits naturally within broader security governance and continual improvement programs.

### GRC Perspective

From a GRC perspective, continuous improvement demonstrates that the organization is not simply performing awareness activities but actively evaluating their effectiveness.

A mature process can be represented as:

**Risk → Control → Measurement → Finding → Corrective Action → Reassessment**

For example:

**Risk:** Employees may fall for business email compromise.

**Control:** Phishing simulation and awareness training.

**Measurement:** Click rate and reporting rate.

**Finding:** Finance employees have difficulty recognizing payment-related impersonation.

**Corrective Action:** Targeted finance awareness training.

**Reassessment:** Conduct another simulation.

This creates a traceable relationship between risk, control effectiveness, and improvement.

### Management Reporting

Simulation results should be communicated to management in a meaningful way.

Instead of reporting:

> "The security team conducted three phishing campaigns."

a stronger report might state:

> "Phishing reporting increased from 18% to 39% over the last three campaigns, while simulated credential submission decreased from 6% to 2%."

Management can then see the security outcome rather than simply the number of activities performed.

### Corrective Action Tracking

Significant weaknesses should be tracked through an appropriate corrective-action process.

For example:

| Finding                               | Risk   | Action                    | Owner              | Status      |
| ------------------------------------- | ------ | ------------------------- | ------------------ | ----------- |
| High interaction with BEC simulations | High   | Finance-specific training | Security Awareness | In Progress |
| Low reporting rate                    | Medium | Improve reporting process | SOC                | Completed   |
| Repeat failures                       | Medium | Targeted microlearning    | HR/Security        | Planned     |

This makes the phishing simulation program part of the broader governance process.

### Avoiding Overreaction

Not every simulation result requires a major corrective action.

For example, a temporary increase in click rate may be caused by:

* A particularly realistic scenario.
* A new workforce population.
* A change in working conditions.
* A campaign conducted during a busy period.

Organizations should analyze the context before concluding that the security awareness program has failed.

The objective is **risk-based improvement**, not reaction to every individual metric change.

### Integrating With the Broader Awareness Program

Phishing simulations should be integrated with other security awareness activities.

A mature program might operate as:

**Annual Training**

↓

**Microlearning**

↓

**Phishing Simulation**

↓

**Behavior Measurement**

↓

**Targeted Training**

↓

**Security Awareness Campaign**

↓

**Future Simulation**

This creates multiple opportunities for employees to learn and reinforce secure behavior.

### Key Takeaways

Continuous improvement transforms phishing simulations from isolated tests into an ongoing security improvement process.

The key principles are:

1. **Use every simulation as a learning opportunity.**
2. **Analyze results beyond simple click rates.**
3. **Identify root causes of risky behavior.**
4. **Use findings to improve awareness content.**
5. **Provide targeted remediation where appropriate.**
6. **Adjust future simulations according to organizational risk.**
7. **Improve reporting processes when necessary.**
8. **Use simulation results to identify technical control improvements.**
9. **Track trends over time.**
10. **Connect simulation results to GRC, risk management, and corrective actions.**
11. **Measure long-term behavioral change.**
12. **Maintain employee trust and avoid a punitive "gotcha" culture.**

A mature phishing simulation program is therefore not a repetitive cycle of **"send email → count clicks."** It is a structured improvement process in which simulation results are continuously converted into **better training, stronger security behaviors, improved controls, and reduced human-related risk**.

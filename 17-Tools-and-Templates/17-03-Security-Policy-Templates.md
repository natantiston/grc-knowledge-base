# 17.3 Security Policy Templates

## Part 1 – Information Security Policy Template

An **Information Security Policy** is one of the most important governance documents in an organization's cybersecurity program. It establishes management's expectations for protecting information, systems, technology, and business operations.

Unlike a technical security standard or procedure, an information security policy defines **what the organization requires and why**. It provides the governance foundation from which more detailed standards, procedures, guidelines, and controls are developed.

For a GRC professional, the Information Security Policy is particularly important because it connects:

* Business objectives.
* Security governance.
* Risk management.
* Regulatory requirements.
* Security controls.
* Employee responsibilities.
* Management accountability.

A well-designed policy should be understandable to management and employees while providing sufficient authority for the organization to enforce security requirements.

---

## 1.1 Purpose of an Information Security Policy

The primary purpose of the policy is to establish the organization's overall direction for information security.

It should communicate that information security is a business responsibility rather than solely an IT responsibility.

The policy should establish expectations for:

* Protecting confidential information.
* Maintaining information integrity.
* Ensuring availability of critical systems.
* Managing cybersecurity risks.
* Complying with applicable laws and regulations.
* Protecting company assets.
* Reporting security incidents.
* Managing third-party security.
* Supporting business continuity.
* Maintaining appropriate security awareness.

The policy should provide the foundation for the organization's broader security program.

---

## 1.2 Policy Hierarchy

A mature organization should distinguish between different types of governance documents.

A common hierarchy is:

```text
Information Security Policy
          ↓
Security Standards
          ↓
Security Procedures
          ↓
Security Guidelines
          ↓
Technical Configuration
```

For example:

### Policy

> All employees must protect organizational information from unauthorized access.

### Standard

> Passwords must meet defined complexity and authentication requirements.

### Procedure

> The service desk must follow the documented process for resetting a user's password.

### Guideline

> Employees should avoid using predictable passwords.

### Technical Configuration

> The identity platform enforces the approved password and authentication configuration.

This distinction prevents policies from becoming overly technical and difficult to maintain.

---

## 1.3 Why the Policy Is Important

The Information Security Policy provides formal management direction.

It helps the organization:

* Establish accountability.
* Define security expectations.
* Support regulatory compliance.
* Demonstrate management commitment.
* Establish a basis for security controls.
* Support audits.
* Communicate responsibilities.
* Provide enforcement authority.

It also provides a reference point when security decisions are disputed.

For example, if an employee asks:

> "Why am I required to use MFA?"

The organization can point to the applicable policy and supporting standard.

---

## 1.4 Policy Ownership

The policy should have a clearly identified owner.

A typical structure might be:

| Role                         | Responsibility                   |
| ---------------------------- | -------------------------------- |
| Board / Executive Management | Provides strategic support       |
| CISO                         | Policy owner                     |
| GRC Team                     | Develops and maintains policy    |
| Legal / Compliance           | Reviews regulatory requirements  |
| IT / Security                | Provides technical input         |
| HR                           | Supports employee accountability |
| Business Owners              | Review business applicability    |
| Employees                    | Comply with requirements         |

The exact ownership model depends on organizational structure.

However, the GRC team should not assume that it automatically owns every policy.

---

## 1.5 Policy Scope

The scope defines who and what the policy applies to.

A practical scope may include:

* Employees.
* Contractors.
* Consultants.
* Temporary workers.
* Third parties.
* Information systems.
* Cloud services.
* Corporate networks.
* End-user devices.
* Business applications.
* Organizational information.

Example:

> This policy applies to all employees, contractors, consultants, third parties, information systems, applications, devices, networks, and information assets operated or managed on behalf of the organization.

A clear scope prevents ambiguity.

---

## 1.6 Policy Structure

A practical Information Security Policy can contain:

```text
1. Purpose
2. Scope
3. Policy Statement
4. Roles and Responsibilities
5. Information Security Requirements
6. Risk Management
7. Access Control
8. Data Protection
9. Security Awareness
10. Incident Management
11. Third-Party Security
12. Business Continuity
13. Compliance
14. Exceptions
15. Enforcement
16. Monitoring
17. Policy Review
18. Approval
```

The exact structure may vary.

The important principle is that the policy should establish the organization's security expectations without becoming an operational procedure.

---

## 1.7 Section 1 – Purpose

The purpose section should explain why the policy exists.

Example:

> The purpose of this policy is to establish the organization's requirements for protecting information and information systems against unauthorized access, disclosure, modification, destruction, disruption, and other security threats.

A good purpose statement should be concise.

---

## 1.8 Section 2 – Scope

The scope should identify the people, systems, information, and environments covered by the policy.

Example:

> This policy applies to all employees, contractors, consultants, third parties, information systems, applications, cloud services, devices, networks, and information assets that are owned, operated, or managed by or on behalf of the organization.

---

## 1.9 Section 3 – Policy Statement

The policy statement establishes management's overall commitment.

Example:

> The organization is committed to protecting the confidentiality, integrity, and availability of its information assets and to maintaining an effective information security program aligned with business objectives, applicable legal and regulatory requirements, and identified cybersecurity risks.

This is the central governance statement.

---

## 1.10 Section 4 – Roles and Responsibilities

The policy should define high-level responsibilities.

### Executive Management

Provides leadership, resources, and support for information security.

### CISO

Provides security leadership and oversees the security program.

### GRC

Maintains governance documentation, risk processes, compliance activities, and security policy management.

### IT and Security Teams

Implement and operate technical security controls.

### HR

Supports security responsibilities throughout the employee lifecycle.

### Employees

Follow security policies and report suspected security incidents.

### Third Parties

Comply with applicable contractual and security requirements.

---

## 1.11 Section 5 – Information Security Requirements

This section establishes the major security expectations.

For example:

> The organization shall implement appropriate administrative, technical, and physical controls to protect information assets according to their classification, business criticality, and risk.

Requirements may address:

* Authentication.
* Access control.
* Encryption.
* Vulnerability management.
* Secure configuration.
* Logging and monitoring.
* Incident management.
* Backup.
* Security awareness.
* Third-party security.

Detailed technical requirements should normally be maintained in supporting standards.

---

## 1.12 Section 6 – Risk Management

The policy should establish that cybersecurity decisions are risk-based.

Example:

> Information security risks shall be identified, assessed, treated, accepted, and monitored in accordance with the organization's approved risk management methodology.

This connects the policy to the organization's risk management framework.

A policy should not simply say:

> "All risks must be eliminated."

That is unrealistic.

The objective is to manage risks to an acceptable level.

---

## 1.13 Section 7 – Access Control

The policy should establish high-level access requirements.

Example:

> Access to information systems shall be granted based on business need, least privilege, and appropriate authorization.

Additional requirements may include:

* Unique user accounts.
* MFA.
* Privileged access management.
* Access reviews.
* Timely removal of access.
* Segregation of duties.

Detailed requirements can be defined in the Access Control Policy and supporting standards.

---

## 1.14 Section 8 – Data Protection

The policy should establish expectations for protecting information.

Example:

> Information shall be classified and protected according to its sensitivity, business value, regulatory requirements, and associated risks.

Requirements may include:

* Data classification.
* Encryption.
* Secure transmission.
* Data retention.
* Secure disposal.
* Privacy protection.
* Data loss prevention.

This section should align with applicable privacy and data protection requirements.

---

## 1.15 Section 9 – Security Awareness

Employees should understand their security responsibilities.

The policy may require:

* Security awareness training.
* New employee training.
* Periodic refresher training.
* Phishing simulations.
* Role-based training.
* Security communications.

Example:

> All personnel shall complete security awareness training appropriate to their role and responsibilities.

This connects Chapter 16's security awareness program to the broader security policy framework.

---

## 1.16 Section 10 – Incident Management

The policy should establish the requirement to report and manage security incidents.

Example:

> Suspected or confirmed information security incidents shall be reported promptly through the organization's established incident reporting channels and managed in accordance with the incident response process.

The policy should not contain the complete incident response procedure.

Instead, it establishes the requirement and points to the supporting procedure.

---

## 1.17 Section 11 – Third-Party Security

Third parties can introduce significant cybersecurity risk.

The policy should therefore establish requirements such as:

* Security due diligence.
* Contractual security requirements.
* Risk assessments.
* Security clauses.
* Monitoring.
* Periodic reassessment.
* Incident notification.

Example:

> Third parties that access, process, store, or manage organizational information shall be subject to security requirements proportionate to the associated risk.

---

## 1.18 Section 12 – Business Continuity

The organization should establish requirements for maintaining critical operations during disruptions.

The policy may require:

* Business continuity planning.
* Disaster recovery.
* Backup.
* Recovery testing.
* Resilience measures.
* Critical system identification.

Example:

> Critical information systems shall have appropriate continuity and recovery measures based on business impact and risk assessments.

---

## 1.19 Section 13 – Compliance

The policy should establish the organization's obligation to comply with applicable requirements.

These may include:

* Laws.
* Regulations.
* Contracts.
* Industry requirements.
* Internal policies.
* Security standards.

For an organization operating in Europe, this may include requirements related to:

* GDPR.
* NIS2.
* Applicable national cybersecurity legislation.
* Contractual security obligations.

The policy should avoid claiming that a regulation applies unless applicability has actually been established.

---

## 1.20 Section 14 – Exceptions

Organizations sometimes require temporary deviations from policy requirements.

The policy should establish that exceptions must follow a formal process.

Example:

> Exceptions to this policy shall be formally documented, risk assessed, approved by an authorized individual, and reviewed periodically.

This connects directly with the Risk Acceptance and Exception Template covered in Part 4.

---

## 1.21 Section 15 – Enforcement

The policy should explain that violations may result in appropriate action.

Example:

> Violations of this policy may result in disciplinary action, contractual remedies, restriction of access, or other actions consistent with applicable law and organizational procedures.

The policy should not attempt to define every disciplinary consequence.

HR and legal requirements should be considered.

---

## 1.22 Section 16 – Monitoring

The organization should establish the right to monitor compliance.

Example:

> The organization may monitor compliance with this policy through security monitoring, control assessments, audits, risk assessments, technical reviews, and other appropriate assurance activities.

Monitoring should be conducted in accordance with applicable legal, privacy, employment, and organizational requirements.

---

## 1.23 Section 17 – Policy Review

The policy should have a defined review cycle.

For example:

> This policy shall be reviewed at least annually and whenever significant changes occur in the organization's business, technology, regulatory environment, or risk profile.

Review triggers may include:

* Major incidents.
* New regulations.
* Organizational restructuring.
* New technology.
* Major acquisitions.
* Significant changes to the threat landscape.
* Audit findings.

---

## 1.24 Section 18 – Approval

The policy should include formal approval.

Example:

| Role              | Responsibility       |
| ----------------- | -------------------- |
| Policy Owner      | CISO                 |
| Document Reviewer | GRC Manager          |
| Legal Reviewer    | Legal / Compliance   |
| Approver          | Executive Management |
| Effective Date    | 1 October 2026       |
| Review Date       | 1 October 2027       |
| Version           | 2.0                  |

This establishes document governance.

## 1.30 Policy Communication

Employees should be informed when important policy changes occur.

Communication methods may include:

* Email.
* Security awareness training.
* LMS.
* Intranet.
* Employee portal.
* Management briefings.
* Security campaigns.

For significant policy changes, organizations may require employees to acknowledge that they have read and understood the policy.

---

## 1.31 Policy Acknowledgment

A policy acknowledgment can provide evidence that employees were informed.

Example:

> "I acknowledge that I have received, reviewed, and understand the organization's Information Security Policy and agree to comply with applicable requirements."

The organization should be careful not to treat acknowledgment alone as proof that employees actually understand the requirements.

Training and awareness activities may also be necessary.

## 1.33 Completed Example

Consider a fictional company called **Example Technology Services**.

### Information Security Policy

**Document ID:** POL-SEC-001

**Version:** 1.0

**Owner:** CISO

**Approved By:** Executive Management

**Effective Date:** 1 October 2026

**Review Date:** 1 October 2027

### Purpose

> This policy establishes the organization's requirements for protecting information and information systems against unauthorized access, disclosure, modification, destruction, and disruption.

### Scope

> This policy applies to all employees, contractors, consultants, third parties, information systems, applications, devices, cloud services, networks, and information assets managed by or on behalf of the organization.

### Policy Statement

> The organization shall maintain an information security program designed to protect the confidentiality, integrity, and availability of information and to manage cybersecurity risks in accordance with business requirements and applicable obligations.

### Access Control

> Access shall be granted based on business need, least privilege, and appropriate authorization. Strong authentication shall be implemented for systems and accounts based on risk.

### Data Protection

> Information shall be classified and protected according to its sensitivity, business value, legal requirements, and associated risks.

### Security Awareness

> Personnel shall complete security awareness training appropriate to their role and responsibilities.

### Incident Management

> Suspected security incidents shall be reported promptly and managed according to the organization's incident response process.

### Third-Party Security

> Third parties with access to organizational information or systems shall be subject to security requirements proportionate to the associated risk.

### Exceptions

> Exceptions shall be formally documented, risk assessed, approved by an authorized individual, and periodically reviewed.

### Enforcement

> Violations may result in appropriate disciplinary, contractual, or other actions consistent with applicable requirements.

### Review

> The policy shall be reviewed at least annually and when significant changes occur to the organization's business, technology, regulatory environment, or risk profile.

This example demonstrates the appropriate level of detail for an enterprise-level policy.

---

## 1.34 What Should Not Be Included

A common mistake is making the policy too detailed.

Avoid putting highly specific operational instructions directly into the Information Security Policy.

For example, avoid:

> "The firewall administrator must configure TCP port 443 on interface Gi0/1 using the following command..."

That belongs in a technical procedure or standard.

Similarly:

> "The SOC analyst must investigate an alert within 15 minutes."

may belong in an incident response procedure or SOC standard.

The policy should establish the requirement:

> "Security events shall be monitored and investigated according to defined security procedures."

This makes the policy easier to maintain.

---

## 1.35 Policy vs Standard vs Procedure

Consider MFA.

### Policy

> Strong authentication shall be implemented based on security risk.

### Standard

> Privileged accounts shall use phishing-resistant MFA.

### Procedure

> The IAM team shall follow the documented workflow for registering a privileged user with the approved authentication platform.

### Technical Configuration

> The identity platform shall enforce the approved authentication policy for privileged accounts.

Each layer serves a different purpose.

---

## 1.36 Common Information Security Policy Mistakes

### Mistake 1 – Too Technical

The policy becomes difficult for management and employees to understand.

### Mistake 2 – Too Generic

The policy contains broad statements that cannot be implemented or measured.

### Mistake 3 – No Owner

Nobody is accountable for maintaining the policy.

### Mistake 4 – No Approval

The document has no formal management authority.

### Mistake 5 – No Review Date

The policy becomes outdated.

### Mistake 6 – No Exception Process

Employees and business units bypass requirements informally.

### Mistake 7 – No Enforcement

Requirements exist but violations have no defined governance mechanism.

### Mistake 8 – No Regulatory Alignment

Applicable legal and regulatory requirements are not considered.

### Mistake 9 – No Supporting Standards

The policy contains detailed requirements that should instead exist in standards and procedures.

### Mistake 10 – No Communication

The policy is published but employees are never informed or trained.

---

## 1.37 GRC Quality Review

Before approving an Information Security Policy, the GRC professional should ask:

### Governance

* Is there a clearly identified owner?
* Has the appropriate authority approved it?
* Is version control implemented?

### Scope

* Are employees and third parties covered?
* Are cloud environments included?
* Are relevant information assets included?

### Risk

* Does the policy establish a risk-based approach?
* Are exceptions connected to risk management?

### Security

* Are major security domains addressed?
* Are requirements sufficiently clear?

### Compliance

* Are applicable legal and regulatory requirements considered?
* Are contractual obligations addressed?

### Accountability

* Are responsibilities clearly defined?
* Is enforcement addressed?

### Lifecycle

* Is there a review cycle?
* Are review triggers defined?
* Is policy communication required?

### Implementation

* Are supporting standards and procedures identified?
* Can requirements be mapped to controls?

## 1.41 Professional GRC Perspective

An Information Security Policy is not simply a document that an organization publishes on its intranet.

It is a governance instrument.

A strong policy establishes:

**Management Direction**

↓

**Security Requirements**

↓

**Standards**

↓

**Procedures**

↓

**Controls**

↓

**Evidence**

↓

**Assurance**

This creates traceability throughout the security program.

For a GRC professional, one of the most important questions is:

> "Can we demonstrate that the requirements in our security policy are actually implemented and operating?"

If the answer is no, the policy may exist only as documentation rather than functioning governance.

The best policies are therefore:

* Clear.
* Business-aligned.
* Risk-based.
* Enforceable.
* Measurable.
* Auditable.
* Maintained.
* Supported by appropriate standards and procedures.

---

## Key Takeaways

1. The Information Security Policy establishes the organization's overall security direction.
2. It should define **what** the organization requires rather than detailed technical procedures.
3. Policy, standards, procedures, and technical configurations serve different purposes.
4. The policy should have a clearly defined owner and approval authority.
5. Scope should cover relevant people, systems, information, and third parties.
6. Security requirements should address major areas such as access control, data protection, awareness, incident management, and third-party security.
7. The policy should establish a risk-based approach to information security.
8. Exceptions should be formally managed through the organization's risk and exception process.
9. Applicable legal, regulatory, contractual, and internal requirements should be considered.
10. Policy requirements should be mapped to controls where appropriate.
11. Policies should have version control, effective dates, and defined review cycles.
12. Significant policy changes should be communicated to affected personnel.
13. A policy should be clear enough for employees and management to understand.
14. A policy should be specific enough to support implementation and auditability.
15. The real value of an Information Security Policy is not the document itself, but the **governance, accountability, and control structure it establishes**.

## Part 2 – Risk Assessment Template

A **Risk Assessment Template** is one of the most practical tools in a GRC professional's toolkit. It provides a consistent structure for identifying, analyzing, evaluating, and documenting risks across systems, processes, projects, vendors, and business activities.

Without a standardized template, different teams may assess similar risks using different terminology, scoring methods, assumptions, and levels of detail. This makes it difficult for management to compare risks and make consistent decisions.

A good risk assessment template creates a common language for risk.

---

## 2.1 Purpose of the Risk Assessment Template

The template should help the organization answer five fundamental questions:

1. **What can go wrong?**
2. **Why could it happen?**
3. **What would happen if it occurred?**
4. **How likely is it to happen?**
5. **What should the organization do about it?**

The template should capture enough information to support a defensible risk decision without becoming unnecessarily complicated.

---

## 2.2 When to Use a Risk Assessment Template

A standardized template can be used for:

* Enterprise risk assessments.
* Information security risk assessments.
* Application assessments.
* Infrastructure assessments.
* Cloud risk assessments.
* Project risk assessments.
* Vendor risk assessments.
* Third-party assessments.
* Data protection assessments.
* Change assessments.
* New technology assessments.
* AI system assessments.
* Business process assessments.
* Compliance assessments.

The same fundamental methodology can be adapted to different use cases.

---

## 2.3 Risk Assessment Lifecycle

A practical risk assessment follows a defined lifecycle:

```text
Establish Context
      ↓
Identify Assets / Processes
      ↓
Identify Threats
      ↓
Identify Vulnerabilities
      ↓
Identify Risk Events
      ↓
Assess Likelihood
      ↓
Assess Impact
      ↓
Determine Inherent Risk
      ↓
Assess Existing Controls
      ↓
Determine Residual Risk
      ↓
Evaluate Against Risk Appetite
      ↓
Select Risk Treatment
      ↓
Assign Risk Owner
      ↓
Monitor and Review
```

The template should support this entire lifecycle.

---

## 2.5 Step 1 – Establish Context

Before identifying risks, the assessor should understand the context.

Questions include:

* What business process is being assessed?
* What system or service is involved?
* Who owns it?
* What information does it process?
* Who uses it?
* What is its business importance?
* What regulations apply?
* What dependencies exist?
* What is the assessment objective?

Example:

> The customer portal provides online access to customer accounts and processes personal and financial information. It is classified as a business-critical application.

Without context, risk identification becomes superficial.

---

## 2.6 Step 2 – Identify Assets

Identify the assets involved.

Examples:

* Applications.
* Databases.
* Servers.
* Cloud services.
* Networks.
* Devices.
* Information.
* Business processes.
* People.
* Third-party services.

Example:

| Asset             | Type           | Criticality |
| ----------------- | -------------- | ----------- |
| Customer Portal   | Application    | High        |
| Customer Database | Information    | Critical    |
| API Gateway       | Infrastructure | High        |
| Identity Platform | Service        | Critical    |

Asset criticality helps establish the potential impact of a risk.

---

## 2.7 Step 3 – Identify Information

Determine what information is processed.

For example:

* Personal data.
* Financial information.
* Authentication information.
* Intellectual property.
* Customer records.
* Employee information.
* Operational information.

The sensitivity of the information can significantly influence the impact assessment.

---

## 2.8 Step 4 – Identify Threats

A threat is a potential source of harm.

Examples include:

* Phishing.
* Malware.
* Ransomware.
* Credential theft.
* Insider misuse.
* Exploitation of vulnerabilities.
* Denial-of-service attacks.
* Supply chain compromise.
* Cloud misconfiguration.
* Physical theft.
* Human error.

The assessment should identify threats relevant to the specific environment rather than simply copying a generic threat list.

---

## 2.9 Step 5 – Identify Vulnerabilities

A vulnerability is a weakness that could be exploited.

Examples:

* Missing security patches.
* Weak authentication.
* Excessive privileges.
* Poor network segmentation.
* Insecure configuration.
* Unsupported software.
* Lack of monitoring.
* Insufficient security awareness.
* Weak vendor controls.

The distinction is important:

> **Threat + Vulnerability + Exposure = Risk Scenario**

For example:

> Threat: Credential theft
> Vulnerability: No MFA
> Exposure: Internet-facing administrative interface

This creates a meaningful risk scenario.

---

## 2.10 Step 6 – Define the Risk Event

The risk event should describe what could actually happen.

Weak description:

> "Cybersecurity risk."

Better:

> "An attacker could obtain valid administrator credentials through phishing and use them to gain unauthorized access to the customer management platform."

The second description identifies the event and makes impact analysis easier.

---

## 2.11 Risk Statement

A useful risk statement can follow this structure:

> **Because of [cause/vulnerability], [risk event] could occur, resulting in [consequence].**

Example:

> Because privileged accounts do not consistently use phishing-resistant MFA, an attacker could compromise administrative credentials through social engineering, resulting in unauthorized access to critical production systems and customer information.

This provides a much stronger basis for assessment.

---

## 2.12 Step 7 – Identify Consequences

The assessor should identify what happens if the risk event occurs.

Consequences may include:

### Confidentiality

Unauthorized disclosure of information.

### Integrity

Unauthorized modification or destruction of information.

### Availability

Loss or interruption of systems or services.

### Financial

Revenue loss, recovery costs, penalties, or increased operational costs.

### Legal / Regulatory

Potential violations of applicable obligations.

### Reputational

Loss of customer or stakeholder trust.

### Operational

Disruption to business processes.

### Safety

Potential impact on physical safety where relevant.

---

## 2.13 CIA Impact

A practical assessment can evaluate:

| Dimension       | Impact |
| --------------- | ------ |
| Confidentiality | High   |
| Integrity       | High   |
| Availability    | Medium |
| Regulatory      | High   |
| Financial       | High   |
| Reputation      | Medium |

This creates a multidimensional view of impact.

---

## 2.14 Step 8 – Assess Likelihood

Likelihood estimates how probable the risk event is within the defined assessment period.

For example:

| Score | Rating         | Description                  |
| ----: | -------------- | ---------------------------- |
|     1 | Rare           | Highly unlikely              |
|     2 | Unlikely       | Could occur but not expected |
|     3 | Possible       | Could reasonably occur       |
|     4 | Likely         | Expected to occur            |
|     5 | Almost Certain | Very likely to occur         |

The organization should define these terms consistently.

---

## 2.15 Likelihood Factors

Likelihood should not be based purely on intuition.

Consider:

* Threat capability.
* Threat motivation.
* Exposure.
* Vulnerability severity.
* Existing controls.
* Attack frequency.
* Historical incidents.
* Industry intelligence.
* Environmental conditions.
* Human behavior.

Example:

An internet-facing critical vulnerability actively exploited in the wild should generally have a different likelihood assessment from an isolated internal vulnerability.

---

## 2.16 Step 9 – Assess Impact

Impact should also use a defined scale.

Example:

| Score | Rating        | Description                    |
| ----: | ------------- | ------------------------------ |
|     1 | Insignificant | Minimal business effect        |
|     2 | Minor         | Limited disruption             |
|     3 | Moderate      | Significant but manageable     |
|     4 | Major         | Serious business impact        |
|     5 | Severe        | Critical business consequences |

Organizations should define these categories based on their own business environment.

---

## 2.17 Step 10 – Calculate Inherent Risk

**Inherent risk** represents the level of risk before considering the effectiveness of existing controls.

A simple model is:

> **Inherent Risk = Likelihood × Impact**

Example:

Likelihood = 4

Impact = 5

Therefore:

> Inherent Risk = 4 × 5 = 20

The organization may classify 20 as Critical.

---

## 2.18 Example Risk Matrix

A simple 5×5 matrix may be used:

| Likelihood \ Impact |  1 |  2 |  3 |  4 |  5 |
| ------------------- | -: | -: | -: | -: | -: |
| 5                   |  5 | 10 | 15 | 20 | 25 |
| 4                   |  4 |  8 | 12 | 16 | 20 |
| 3                   |  3 |  6 |  9 | 12 | 15 |
| 2                   |  2 |  4 |  6 |  8 | 10 |
| 1                   |  1 |  2 |  3 |  4 |  5 |

The organization can then define thresholds.

Example:

| Score | Rating   |
| ----: | -------- |
|   1–4 | Low      |
|   5–9 | Medium   |
| 10–14 | High     |
| 15–25 | Critical |

These values are illustrative. The organization's approved methodology should determine the actual thresholds.

---

## 2.19 Step 11 – Identify Existing Controls

The next step is to determine what controls already exist.

Examples:

* MFA.
* Endpoint protection.
* Firewalls.
* Encryption.
* Logging.
* Security awareness.
* Vulnerability management.
* Access reviews.
* Backup.
* Network segmentation.
* Security monitoring.

The assessor should distinguish between:

> **Control exists**

and:

> **Control is effective.**

A control that exists but does not operate effectively should not receive full credit.

---

## 2.20 Control Effectiveness

A practical rating may be:

| Rating              | Description                                      |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ |
| Effective           | Control is implemented and operating effectively |
| Partially Effective | Control exists but has limitations               |
| Ineffective         | Control does not adequately reduce the risk      |
| Not Implemented     | No relevant control exists                       |

Evidence should support the assessment.

For example:

> MFA configured.

is evidence of implementation.

But:

> MFA configuration reviewed and tested; 98.7% of privileged accounts protected.

provides stronger evidence of effectiveness.

---

## 2.21 Step 12 – Determine Residual Risk

**Residual risk** is the risk remaining after considering existing controls.

Example:

```text
Inherent Risk
     ↓
Existing Controls
     ↓
Control Effectiveness
     ↓
Residual Risk
```

Suppose:

* Inherent likelihood = 4.
* Inherent impact = 5.
* Existing controls reduce likelihood to 2.
* Impact remains 5.

Residual risk:

> 2 × 5 = 10

The risk therefore decreases from 20 to 10.

---

## 2.22 Important Principle – Controls Do Not Always Reduce Impact

Some controls primarily reduce likelihood.

For example:

* MFA.
* Firewall.
* Security awareness.
* Vulnerability management.

Other controls primarily reduce impact.

For example:

* Backup.
* Disaster recovery.
* Incident response.
* Business continuity.

Therefore, the assessor should understand **how the control changes the risk**, rather than automatically reducing both likelihood and impact.

## 2.24 Risk Treatment Options

The template should provide clear treatment options.

### Avoid

Stop the activity that creates the risk.

Example:

> Do not deploy an application that cannot meet minimum security requirements.

### Mitigate

Implement additional controls.

Example:

> Implement MFA and network segmentation.

### Transfer

Transfer some financial or operational consequences to another party.

Examples:

* Insurance.
* Contractual arrangements.
* Outsourcing.

Risk transfer does not necessarily eliminate the underlying security risk.

### Accept

Management formally accepts the residual risk.

Acceptance should follow the organization's risk acceptance process.

---

## 2.25 Treatment Plan

Every risk requiring action should have a treatment plan.

Example:

| Action                     | Owner          | Due Date    | Status      |
| -------------------------- | -------------- | ----------- | ----------- |
| Implement MFA              | IAM Team       | 30 Sep 2026 | In Progress |
| Restrict admin access      | Infrastructure | 15 Sep 2026 | Open        |
| Enable enhanced monitoring | SOC            | 20 Sep 2026 | Complete    |

This turns the assessment into an actionable management tool.

---

## 2.26 Risk Owner vs Control Owner

These roles should not be confused.

### Risk Owner

Accountable for the risk and the decision regarding its treatment.

### Control Owner

Responsible for implementing and operating a specific control.

For example:

> CIO = Risk Owner

> IAM Manager = Control Owner

> SOC Manager = Control Owner

This distinction is important in GRC.

---

## 2.27 Risk Appetite

The assessment should compare residual risk against the organization's risk appetite.

Example:

```text
Residual Risk = 10
Risk Appetite Threshold = 12

Result:
Within Risk Appetite
```

Another example:

```text
Residual Risk = 18
Risk Appetite Threshold = 12

Result:
Above Risk Appetite
```

A risk above appetite should normally require additional treatment, escalation, or formally authorized acceptance according to organizational governance.

---

## 2.28 Risk Acceptance Decision

If residual risk remains above appetite, the template should document the decision.

Possible outcomes:

* Additional mitigation required.
* Escalation required.
* Risk accepted.
* Activity suspended.
* Risk transferred.
* Exception required.

This creates traceability between the assessment and management decision.

---

## 2.29 Evidence

A professional risk assessment should be supported by evidence.

Evidence may include:

* Vulnerability scan.
* Penetration test.
* Configuration review.
* Audit report.
* Access review.
* Incident records.
* Architecture diagrams.
* Vendor assessment.
* Security logs.
* Business impact analysis.
* Control testing results.

Evidence should be referenced rather than simply stating that a control exists.

---

## 2.30 Risk Assessment Example

Consider a customer-facing web application.

### Risk Scenario

> An attacker could exploit an unpatched web application vulnerability and gain unauthorized access to customer information.

### Likelihood

4 – Likely.

### Impact

5 – Severe.

### Inherent Risk

4 × 5 = 20 – Critical.

### Existing Controls

* Web application firewall.
* Vulnerability scanning.
* Endpoint monitoring.
* Security monitoring.
* Network segmentation.

### Control Effectiveness

Partially effective because patching is delayed.

### Residual Likelihood

3 – Possible.

### Residual Impact

5 – Severe.

### Residual Risk

3 × 5 = 15 – Critical.

### Treatment

* Apply security patch.
* Conduct validation testing.
* Increase monitoring.
* Restrict vulnerable component exposure.

### Risk Owner

Application Director.

### Target Date

30 September 2026.

This is much more useful than simply recording:

> "Web application vulnerability – High."

---

## 2.31 Risk Assessment Quality

A high-quality assessment should be:

### Consistent

Different assessors should produce reasonably comparable results.

### Evidence-Based

Ratings should have supporting evidence.

### Transparent

Assumptions should be documented.

### Reproducible

Another assessor should understand how the score was calculated.

### Actionable

The assessment should lead to a management decision or treatment action.

### Current

The assessment should reflect the current environment.

---

## 2.32 Common Risk Assessment Problems

### Problem 1 – Generic Risk Statements

> "Cyberattack risk."

This is too broad.

### Problem 2 – Arbitrary Scores

> Likelihood = 5 because the assessor "feels" it is high.

The methodology should define the reasoning.

### Problem 3 – Ignoring Existing Controls

The assessment calculates inherent risk but never evaluates controls.

### Problem 4 – Treating Controls as Automatically Effective

A control's existence does not prove effectiveness.

### Problem 5 – No Risk Owner

Nobody is accountable for the decision.

### Problem 6 – No Treatment Plan

The assessment identifies a problem but does not define what happens next.

### Problem 7 – No Review Date

Risk assessments become outdated.

### Problem 8 – Confusing Vulnerability With Risk

A vulnerability is not automatically the complete risk scenario.

### Problem 9 – Treating Risk Scores as Absolute Truth

A score is a decision-support mechanism, not a mathematical representation of reality.

---

## 2.33 Risk Assessment Assumptions

The assessor should document important assumptions.

Example:

> The likelihood rating assumes the application remains internet-facing throughout the assessment period.

Another:

> The assessment assumes the existing MFA implementation remains operational and covers all privileged accounts.

If assumptions change, the assessment may need to be updated.

## 2.34 Risk Assessment Confidence

Some organizations may also record confidence in the assessment.

Example:

| Confidence | Description                   |
| ---------- | ----------------------------- |
| High       | Strong evidence available     |
| Medium     | Some assumptions required     |
| Low        | Limited information available |

For example:

> Residual risk: High
> Confidence: Low

This tells management that the risk rating may change when better information becomes available.

## 2.35 Risk Assessment Review Triggers

The assessment should be reviewed when:

* A major system change occurs.
* A security incident occurs.
* A new vulnerability emerges.
* A new threat becomes relevant.
* A major control changes.
* A new regulation applies.
* A third-party relationship changes.
* Business criticality changes.
* Risk appetite changes.

Periodic review alone may not be sufficient.

The risk register provides the management-level view.

## 2.37 Connecting Risk Assessment to Controls

A mature GRC process should provide traceability:

```text

  ↓
Risk
  ↓
Control
  ↓
Control Test
  ↓
Evidence
  ↓

  ↓
```

This is particularly valuable during audits.

An auditor may ask:

> "How did you determine this risk was acceptable?"

The GRC professional should be able to trace the answer back through the assessment, controls, evidence, and management approval.

## 2.45 GRC Professional Perspective

A risk assessment template is more than a form.

It is a mechanism for creating **consistent and defensible risk decisions**.

The GRC professional should not simply ask:

> "What is the risk score?"

The better questions are:

> "What is the scenario?"

> "What evidence supports the rating?"

> "What controls are actually operating?"

> "What risk remains?"

> "Is the residual risk within appetite?"

> "Who is accountable?"

> "What decision needs to be made?"

This shifts the assessment from a documentation exercise into a real governance process.

A mature GRC professional should also be comfortable challenging risk assessments when:

* Ratings are unsupported.
* Controls are assumed to be effective.
* Impact is underestimated.
* Risk statements are vague.
* Important dependencies are ignored.
* Risk appetite is not considered.
* Management decisions are not documented.

The ultimate objective is not to produce a perfect numerical score.

The objective is to provide management with **reliable information for making informed risk decisions**.

## Key Takeaways

1. A Risk Assessment Template creates consistency across risk assessments.
2. The assessment should establish context before identifying risks.
3. Assets, threats, vulnerabilities, and risk events should be clearly distinguished.
4. Risk statements should describe a realistic scenario and consequence.
5. Likelihood and impact should use defined organizational criteria.
6. Inherent risk represents exposure before considering existing controls.
7. Residual risk represents the remaining exposure after considering controls.
8. Control existence does not automatically mean control effectiveness.
9. Evidence and assumptions should support risk ratings.
10. Risk owners and control owners have different responsibilities.
11. Residual risk should be compared against organizational risk appetite.
12. Risks requiring treatment should have owners, actions, and target dates.
13. Risk assessments should be reviewed when significant changes occur.
14. Risk assessments should feed the enterprise risk register.
15. The same methodology can be adapted for applications, vendors, projects, cloud services, and AI systems.
16. A risk score is a decision-support tool, not an absolute measurement of reality.
17. The strongest assessments are **consistent, evidence-based, transparent, reproducible, and actionable**.

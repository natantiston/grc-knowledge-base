## Governance and Ownership

A successful enterprise security awareness program requires more than training content, phishing simulations, and awareness campaigns.

It needs **clear governance, accountability, ownership, authority, and executive support**.

Without governance, security awareness can easily become a collection of disconnected activities:

* Annual training.
* Occasional phishing simulations.
* Security newsletters.
* Posters.
* Compliance exercises.

These activities may exist, but the organization may not know:

* Who owns the program.
* Who approves it.
* Which risks it addresses.
* How effectiveness is measured.
* Who is accountable for improvement.
* How much funding it requires.

A mature program treats security awareness as an **enterprise security capability**, not simply an HR training activity.

> **Governance establishes who makes decisions, who is accountable, how the program is controlled, and how its effectiveness is demonstrated.**

---

# Why Governance Matters

Security awareness affects almost every part of an organization.

Employees interact with:

* Corporate systems.
* Customer information.
* Financial information.
* Personal data.
* Cloud services.
* Suppliers.
* Physical facilities.
* Business processes.

Therefore, security awareness cannot realistically be owned by the security team alone.

For example:

**Cybersecurity**

Provides security expertise.

↓

**HR**

Supports employee training and onboarding.

↓

**Legal / Privacy**

Provides regulatory and privacy requirements.

↓

**IT**

Supports technical controls and platforms.

↓

**Business Units**

Identify role-specific risks.

↓

**Executive Management**

Provides direction and resources.

↓

**Employees**

Apply secure behaviors.

This requires a coordinated governance structure.

---

# Program Ownership

The organization should designate a clear **program owner**.

The owner is accountable for coordinating and managing the security awareness program.

Depending on the organization, the owner could report to:

* CISO.
* Chief Information Security Officer's organization.
* Cybersecurity department.
* Information Security Governance function.
* Risk and Compliance function.

In smaller organizations, the responsibility may belong to an information security manager or GRC manager.

The important principle is:

> **There must be one clearly identified owner accountable for the overall program.**

---

# Program Owner Responsibilities

The program owner may be responsible for:

* Developing the awareness strategy.
* Maintaining the program roadmap.
* Conducting awareness needs assessments.
* Coordinating training.
* Managing phishing simulations.
* Defining metrics.
* Reporting results.
* Coordinating stakeholders.
* Managing awareness technology.
* Maintaining policies and procedures.
* Supporting audits.
* Managing the awareness budget.
* Driving continuous improvement.

The owner does not necessarily perform every activity personally.

Instead, the owner coordinates the overall capability.

---

# CISO Role

The CISO or equivalent security executive provides strategic leadership.

The CISO should:

* Establish security expectations.
* Approve the overall strategy.
* Support funding.
* Communicate the importance of security.
* Review significant metrics.
* Remove organizational barriers.
* Support enforcement when necessary.

The CISO should not necessarily manage every training session.

Instead, the CISO provides:

> **Executive sponsorship and strategic direction.**

---

# Executive Sponsorship

Executive sponsorship is one of the strongest factors supporting awareness-program success.

Employees are more likely to take security seriously when senior management demonstrates that security matters.

For example, the CEO can communicate:

> "Security is part of everyone's responsibility. Completing required training and reporting suspicious activity are essential business responsibilities."

This message is more powerful than sending another automated training reminder.

---

# Board-Level Oversight

For larger organizations, security awareness may also be relevant to board oversight.

The board does not need to manage the awareness program operationally.

Instead, it should understand significant human-related cybersecurity risks.

Board reporting might include:

* Major human-risk trends.
* Phishing performance.
* Significant security-awareness gaps.
* High-risk employee populations.
* Material security incidents involving human behavior.
* Regulatory concerns.
* Program maturity.
* Improvement plans.

The board's role is primarily **oversight and risk governance**.

---

# Governance Committee

A security awareness steering committee can help coordinate the program.

Members may include representatives from:

* Cybersecurity.
* GRC.
* HR.
* IT.
* Legal.
* Privacy.
* Compliance.
* Internal Audit.
* Communications.
* Business units.

The committee can review:

* Program strategy.
* Risk priorities.
* Training requirements.
* Metrics.
* Major campaigns.
* Regulatory changes.
* Program improvements.

---

# Example Governance Structure

A simplified structure could look like:

**Board / Risk Committee**

↓

**Executive Management**

↓

**CISO**

↓

**Security Awareness Steering Committee**

↓

**Security Awareness Program Owner**

↓

**Security Awareness Team**

↓

**Business Unit Security Champions**

↓

**Employees and Contractors**

This creates clear lines of responsibility.

# Roles and Responsibilities

A **RACI matrix** can help clarify responsibilities.

RACI means:

* **R – Responsible**
* **A – Accountable**
* **C – Consulted**
* **I – Informed**

Example:

| Activity                | CISO | Awareness Owner | HR  | IT | Business |
| ----------------------- | ---- | --------------- | --- | -- | -------- |
| Awareness Strategy      | A    | R               | C   | C  | C        |
| Training Delivery       | I    | A/R             | R   | C  | C        |
| Phishing Simulation     | A    | R               | C   | C  | I        |
| Metrics                 | A    | R               | C   | C  | I        |
| Role-Based Training     | C    | A               | C   | R  | R        |
| Compliance Requirements | C    | R               | C   | C  | A        |
| Employee Onboarding     | I    | C               | A/R | R  | C        |

The exact responsibilities will vary by organization.

The purpose is to eliminate ambiguity.

---

# Security Awareness Policy

The organization should establish a formal security awareness policy or related governance document.

The policy should define:

* Purpose.
* Scope.
* Roles.
* Responsibilities.
* Training requirements.
* Frequency.
* Audience.
* Exceptions.
* Measurement.
* Enforcement.
* Reporting.
* Governance.

The policy provides the formal authority for the program.

---

# Scope of the Program

Governance should clearly define who is covered.

The program may include:

* Permanent employees.
* Temporary employees.
* Contractors.
* Consultants.
* Interns.
* Remote workers.
* Privileged users.
* Executives.
* Board members.
* Third parties.

Not every audience needs identical training.

However, the organization should define the applicable requirements for each group.

---

# Mandatory vs. Recommended Training

Governance should distinguish between:

### Mandatory

Training required by:

* Policy.
* Regulation.
* Contract.
* Risk assessment.
* Role requirements.

### Recommended

Additional education that improves security knowledge but may not be mandatory.

For example:

**Mandatory**

Annual security awareness training.

**Role-Based Mandatory**

Privileged administrator security training.

**Recommended**

Advanced security workshops for security champions.

---

# Training Requirements

Governance should define:

* Who must complete training.
* When training must be completed.
* How often it must be repeated.
* What happens when training is missed.
* How completion is recorded.
* Who receives escalation reports.

For example:

> All employees must complete annual security awareness training within 30 days of assignment.

New employees may have a shorter deadline because they require awareness before obtaining access to sensitive systems.

---

# Onboarding and Offboarding

Security awareness governance should extend across the employee lifecycle.

### Onboarding

Employees should receive security awareness before or shortly after obtaining system access.

Topics may include:

* Acceptable use.
* Passwords.
* MFA.
* Data protection.
* Phishing.
* Incident reporting.
* Physical security.

### Role Change

Additional training may be required when an employee moves into a higher-risk position.

### Offboarding

Employees should understand:

* Information-return requirements.
* Confidentiality obligations.
* Acceptable use restrictions.
* Access termination.

---

# Role-Based Governance

Different roles create different security risks.

Governance should establish requirements for groups such as:

### Executives

Executive impersonation, business email compromise, deepfakes.

### Finance

Payment fraud, financial information, BEC.

### HR

Employee information and privacy.

### IT Administrators

Privileged access and credential security.

### Developers

Secure development and software supply-chain security.

### Security Personnel

Advanced security operations and incident response.

This ensures that awareness aligns with actual risk.

---

# Security Champions

Large organizations may establish a **Security Champions Program**.

Security champions are employees within business units who help promote security locally.

They can:

* Communicate security messages.
* Help identify local risks.
* Encourage reporting.
* Support awareness campaigns.
* Provide feedback.
* Help coordinate training.

They do not replace the central security team.

Instead:

> **They extend the reach of the security function into the business.**

---

# Business Unit Ownership

Central cybersecurity should not carry all responsibility.

Business-unit leaders should understand that they are accountable for security within their areas.

For example:

The Finance Director should care about:

* Payment fraud.
* Financial-data protection.
* Finance-specific phishing.

The HR Director should care about:

* Employee-data protection.
* Recruitment fraud.
* Confidential records.

This creates a **shared responsibility model**.

---

# HR's Role

Human Resources can be a critical governance partner.

HR may support:

* Employee onboarding.
* Training assignments.
* Training records.
* Employee communications.
* Policy acknowledgement.
* Disciplinary processes where appropriate.
* Role information.

However, HR should not automatically become the owner of cybersecurity awareness.

Security expertise should remain with the appropriate security function.

---

# IT's Role

IT supports the program through technology and operational controls.

Examples include:

* Learning Management Systems.
* Identity systems.
* Email security.
* Endpoint controls.
* MFA.
* Security notifications.
* Access management.

IT can also provide data that helps evaluate human-risk trends.

---

# Legal and Privacy's Role

Legal and privacy teams can help ensure awareness activities comply with applicable requirements.

They may advise on:

* Employee monitoring.
* Privacy.
* Data protection.
* Phishing simulations.
* Recording.
* Personal data processing.
* Third-party requirements.

This becomes especially important when the organization collects individual-level behavioral data.

---

# Internal Audit

Internal Audit provides independent assurance.

It may evaluate whether:

* The program has appropriate governance.
* Policies are implemented.
* Training requirements are followed.
* Metrics are reliable.
* Controls are operating effectively.
* Regulatory requirements are addressed.

Internal Audit should generally remain independent from day-to-day program ownership.

---

# Compliance Function

Compliance may help identify awareness requirements arising from:

* Laws.
* Regulations.
* Industry standards.
* Contracts.
* Customer requirements.

The awareness program should translate these requirements into practical training and behavioral expectations.

---

# Security Awareness Steering Committee

The steering committee can meet periodically to review the program.

Typical agenda items include:

1. Current human-risk profile.
2. Training completion.
3. Phishing results.
4. Security incidents.
5. Regulatory changes.
6. New threats.
7. Role-based training needs.
8. Program performance.
9. Budget.
10. Improvement initiatives.

The committee provides a governance mechanism for continuous improvement.

---

# Decision Rights

Good governance requires clear decision rights.

For example:

### Who approves the annual strategy?

CISO.

### Who manages the program?

Security Awareness Manager.

### Who assigns mandatory training?

Awareness owner + HR.

### Who approves major phishing campaigns?

CISO or designated security authority.

### Who provides role-specific requirements?

Business and security stakeholders.

### Who validates compliance?

Compliance/Internal Audit.

These decisions should be documented.

---

# Exception Management

Organizations will sometimes have legitimate reasons why an employee cannot complete a requirement.

Examples include:

* Extended leave.
* Technical problems.
* Accessibility requirements.
* Temporary assignment.
* Special business circumstances.

Governance should define:

* Who can approve exceptions.
* How exceptions are documented.
* How long they remain valid.
* Whether compensating controls are required.

Exceptions should not become a mechanism for permanently avoiding security requirements.

---

# Escalation

Governance should define what happens when employees do not comply.

For example:

**Training overdue**

↓

Reminder

↓

Manager notification

↓

Escalation to HR

↓

Potential access restriction or disciplinary action according to policy

The exact process should reflect organizational policy and applicable employment law.

The important principle is:

> **Consequences should be defined before enforcement is required.**

---

# Metrics Governance

Governance should define what is measured and who reviews the results.

Metrics may include:

* Training completion.
* Phishing failure.
* Reporting rate.
* Repeat failures.
* Incident reporting.
* High-risk populations.
* Behavioral improvement.

Metrics should have:

* Defined owners.
* Consistent definitions.
* Reporting frequency.
* Targets.
* Escalation thresholds.

This prevents the organization from producing dashboards that look impressive but do not support decisions.

---

# Risk-Based Governance

The program should be driven by enterprise risk rather than training convenience.

For example:

If threat intelligence shows an increase in:

> AI-generated executive impersonation

the awareness program may prioritize:

* Executive training.
* Finance training.
* Verification procedures.
* Deepfake awareness.
* Payment controls.

This is more effective than continuing the same awareness campaign simply because it is on the annual calendar.

---

# Governance and Continuous Improvement

Governance should create a feedback loop:

**Risk Assessment**

↓

**Awareness Strategy**

↓

**Training and Campaigns**

↓

**Behavioral Measurement**

↓

**Incident Data**

↓

**Management Review**

↓

**Improvement**

↓

**Updated Strategy**

This ensures that the program evolves with the organization's risk environment.

---

A mature program may maintain:

* Security Awareness Policy.

* Program Charter.
* Annual Awareness Plan.
* Training Matrix.
* RACI Matrix.
* Awareness Risk Register.
* Metrics Definition Document.
* Phishing Simulation Procedure.
* Exception Procedure.
* Reporting Procedure.
* Third-Party Awareness Requirements.

These documents provide evidence of governance and support audit readiness.

---

# Governance and ISO 27001

From an ISO/IEC 27001 perspective, security awareness should be integrated into the organization's information security management system.

The organization should be able to demonstrate that:

* Relevant personnel receive appropriate awareness.
* Responsibilities are defined.
* Competence requirements are addressed.
* Security objectives are established.
* Risks are assessed.
* Controls are implemented.
* Effectiveness is evaluated.
* Improvement occurs.

The awareness program should therefore not operate as an isolated initiative.

It should be part of the broader **ISMS governance structure**.

---

# Governance and NIST

The NIST Cybersecurity Framework also supports a governance-oriented approach.

Human-related cybersecurity activities can be connected to:

* Governance.
* Identify.
* Protect.
* Detect.
* Respond.
* Recover.

For example:

**Govern**

Define security responsibilities.

↓

**Identify**

Understand human-related risks.

↓

**Protect**

Provide awareness and controls.

↓

**Detect**

Monitor relevant security events.

↓

**Respond**

Report and respond to human-driven incidents.

↓

**Recover**

Learn from incidents and improve awareness.

This demonstrates how security awareness supports the wider cybersecurity program.

# Governance and Human Risk Management

The previous lesson introduced Human Risk Management.

Governance provides the organizational structure needed to operate HRM effectively.

For example:

**CISO**

Accountable for cybersecurity risk.

↓

**HRM / Awareness Owner**

Manages human-risk program.

↓

**Business Units**

Own local human risks.

↓

**Security**

Provides controls and expertise.

↓

**HR / Legal / Privacy**

Provide supporting governance.

↓

**Employees**

Apply expected behaviors.

This transforms human risk from an informal concern into a managed enterprise capability.

# Practical Example

Consider a multinational company with 15,000 employees.

Initially, the company has:

* Annual security training.
* Quarterly phishing simulations.
* No formal awareness owner.
* No steering committee.
* Limited metrics.
* No role-based training.
* No defined escalation process.

The company creates a formal governance model.

### Step 1

CISO becomes executive sponsor.

### Step 2

A Security Awareness Manager becomes program owner.

### Step 3

A steering committee is established.

### Step 4

A RACI matrix defines responsibilities.

### Step 5

Mandatory training requirements are documented.

### Step 6

Role-based training is introduced.

### Step 7

Metrics and reporting are standardized.

### Step 8

Human-risk information is incorporated into enterprise risk reporting.

The organization has now moved from:

> **Awareness activities**

to:

> **An enterprise security awareness program.**

---

# Common Governance Mistakes

Organizations should avoid:

### No Clear Owner

Everyone assumes someone else is responsible.

### Security Owns Everything

Business units do not take responsibility for their own risks.

### HR Owns Everything

Cybersecurity requirements may become disconnected from security risk.

### No Executive Sponsor

The program struggles to obtain authority and funding.

### No RACI

Responsibilities become unclear.

### No Escalation Process

Non-compliance is handled inconsistently.

### No Metrics Ownership

Different teams calculate results differently.

### No Risk Connection

Training becomes a compliance exercise instead of a risk-reduction program.

### Excessive Governance

Too many committees and approvals can make the program slow and ineffective.

Good governance should provide **clarity without unnecessary bureaucracy**.

---

# Key Takeaways

The governance and ownership structure is the foundation of an enterprise security awareness program.

The key principles are:

1. **Security awareness should be governed as an enterprise security capability.**
2. **A clearly identified program owner should be accountable for the overall program.**
3. **The CISO or equivalent executive should provide strategic sponsorship.**
4. **Senior management should actively communicate the importance of secure behavior.**
5. **The board should receive appropriate oversight information about significant human-related cyber risks.**
6. **A steering committee can coordinate cybersecurity, HR, IT, legal, privacy, compliance, and business stakeholders.**
7. **A RACI matrix can clearly define responsibilities and accountability.**
8. **Security awareness policies should establish formal requirements.**
9. **The program should cover employees, contractors, executives, privileged users, and relevant third parties.**
10. **Training requirements should be risk-based and role-specific.**
11. **Business-unit leaders should own security risks within their areas.**
12. **HR, IT, Legal, Privacy, Compliance, and Internal Audit should have clearly defined supporting roles.**
13. **Exception and escalation procedures should be documented.**
14. **Metrics should have clear definitions, owners, targets, and reporting requirements.**
15. **Security awareness governance should be connected to enterprise risk management.**
16. **Human Risk Management should be incorporated into the broader governance structure.**
17. **The program should align with the organization's security framework and ISMS.**
18. **Governance should support continuous improvement rather than create unnecessary bureaucracy.**

The most important concept is:

> **A security awareness program succeeds when accountability is clearly assigned, leadership supports it, business units participate, and human risk is managed as part of enterprise cybersecurity governance.**

The governance model can ultimately be summarized as:

**Executive Sponsorship → Clear Ownership → Defined Responsibilities → Risk-Based Strategy → Measurement → Management Oversight → Continuous Improvement**

A well-designed governance structure transforms security awareness from **"mandatory training"** into a sustainable enterprise cybersecurity capability.

## ISO/IEC 27001 Security Awareness Requirements

Security awareness is a fundamental component of an effective Information Security Management System (ISMS). **ISO/IEC 27001** recognizes that information security depends not only on technical safeguards but also on ensuring that people understand their responsibilities and have the competence necessary to perform their roles securely.

An organization can implement sophisticated security technologies, but those controls can still fail because of human behavior.

For example:

* An employee may disclose credentials.
* A manager may approve inappropriate access.
* A developer may introduce an insecure dependency.
* A user may open a malicious attachment.
* A contractor may mishandle confidential information.
* An administrator may make an unsafe configuration change.

ISO/IEC 27001 therefore places importance on ensuring that people who work under the organization's control understand their information-security responsibilities.

> **Information security is not only a technology problem; it is also a people, process, and governance responsibility.**

## ISO/IEC 27001 and the ISMS

ISO/IEC 27001 specifies requirements for establishing, implementing, maintaining, and continually improving an **Information Security Management System (ISMS)**.

An ISMS provides a structured approach for managing information-security risk.

A simplified model is:

**Context**

↓

**Risk Assessment**

↓

**Risk Treatment**

↓

**Security Controls**

↓

**People + Processes + Technology**

↓

**Monitoring**

↓

**Continual Improvement**

Security awareness supports this entire system.

## Why Awareness Is Important to an ISMS

An ISMS is ultimately operated by people.

Employees and other personnel:

* Follow policies.
* Operate systems.
* Process information.
* Approve access.
* Respond to incidents.
* Manage suppliers.
* Develop applications.
* Perform business processes.

If personnel do not understand their security responsibilities, the ISMS may exist on paper without producing the intended security outcomes.

## Competence and Awareness

A key distinction is between **competence** and **awareness**.

### Competence

Competence concerns whether an individual has the knowledge and ability required to perform a task effectively.

For example:

> A security administrator must understand how to securely configure a firewall.

### Awareness

Awareness concerns whether personnel understand:

* Security policies.
* Their responsibilities.
* Security risks.
* The consequences of failing to follow requirements.

For example:

> An employee understands that sharing their MFA code is prohibited and knows how to report a suspicious authentication request.

Both are important.

## Security Awareness vs. Security Training

These concepts are related but not identical.

**Security awareness** focuses on creating understanding and appropriate behavior.

**Security training** develops specific knowledge and skills.

For example:

### Awareness

> Employees understand why phishing is dangerous.

### Training

> Employees learn how to identify and report phishing emails.

A mature security program combines both.

## ISO/IEC 27001 Clause 7.2 – Competence

One of the important requirements related to people is **Clause 7.2 – Competence**.

The organization needs to determine the necessary competence of people whose work affects information-security performance.

It should:

* Determine required competence.
* Ensure people are competent.
* Take appropriate action where competence gaps exist.
* Retain appropriate documented evidence of competence.

This means organizations should not simply assume that employees are capable of performing security-related responsibilities.

## Competence Determination

The organization should identify what competence is required for relevant roles.

For example:

| Role                 | Required Competence                       |
| -------------------- | ----------------------------------------- |
| SOC Analyst          | Security monitoring and incident analysis |
| System Administrator | Secure system administration              |
| Developer            | Secure coding                             |
| Privacy Officer      | Privacy and data protection               |
| GRC Analyst          | Risk and compliance management            |
| Executive            | Security risk awareness                   |

This creates a foundation for role-based security training.

## Addressing Competence Gaps

If a person does not have the required competence, the organization may take actions such as:

* Training.
* Mentoring.
* Coaching.
* Supervised work.
* Reassignment.
* Hiring qualified personnel.

For example:

> A new administrator lacks experience with privileged access management.

The organization may provide training and supervised practice before granting extensive privileges.

## Evaluating Training Effectiveness

Simply completing training does not necessarily demonstrate competence.

An organization should consider whether training actually produced the desired outcome.

Possible methods include:

* Knowledge assessments.
* Practical exercises.
* Phishing simulations.
* Performance reviews.
* Technical assessments.
* Incident exercises.

This is particularly important for high-risk roles.

## Clause 7.3 – Awareness

**ISO/IEC 27001 Clause 7.3 – Awareness** is directly relevant to security awareness programs.

People working under the organization's control should be aware of relevant:

* Information security policies.
* Their contribution to the effectiveness of the ISMS.
* Benefits of improved security performance.
* Implications of not conforming to ISMS requirements.

This creates a direct connection between the organization's ISMS and its security awareness program.

## Understanding the Security Policy

Personnel should understand the organization's information-security policies that are relevant to their responsibilities.

This does not necessarily mean employees must memorize every policy.

Instead, they should understand:

* Which policies apply to them.
* What behavior is expected.
* Where to find the policies.
* What happens if requirements are not followed.

## Example

Suppose an organization has a policy prohibiting the use of unauthorized cloud storage.

Awareness should help employees understand:

**Requirement**

Do not upload organizational information to unapproved cloud services.

↓

**Reason**

The organization may lose control over sensitive information.

↓

**Expected Behavior**

Use approved storage platforms.

↓

**Alternative**

Contact IT or security if an approved solution is unavailable.

This is more effective than simply asking employees to acknowledge a policy.

## Understanding Individual Responsibilities

Employees should understand how their actions contribute to information security.

For example:

### Employee

Protect credentials.

### Manager

Approve access appropriately.

### IT Administrator

Secure systems.

### Developer

Develop secure applications.

### Procurement

Consider supplier security.

### Executive

Provide governance and risk oversight.

Security awareness should therefore be connected to job responsibilities.

## Consequences of Nonconformity

Personnel should understand that failing to follow security requirements can have consequences.

These may include:

* Security incidents.
* Data loss.
* Service disruption.
* Regulatory violations.
* Financial losses.
* Reputational damage.

The purpose should not be to create fear.

Instead, personnel should understand:

> **Why security requirements exist and why compliance matters.**

## Annex A and Security Awareness

ISO/IEC 27001:2022 includes **Annex A controls** that are particularly relevant to people and awareness.

One important control is:

### A.6.3 – Information Security Awareness, Education and Training

This control focuses on ensuring that personnel receive appropriate information-security awareness, education, and training.

The organization should establish a program appropriate to its needs and risks.

## Risk-Based Awareness

Security awareness should be based on the organization's risk environment.

For example, an organization operating a public-facing web application may emphasize:

* Phishing.
* Credential theft.
* Secure development.
* Vulnerability management.

A manufacturing organization may place greater emphasis on:

* Operational technology.
* Physical security.
* Removable media.
* Safety-related cyber risks.

A healthcare organization may emphasize:

* Patient information.
* Privacy.
* Phishing.
* Data disclosure.

The awareness program should therefore reflect the organization's actual risk profile.

## Security Awareness Policy

Organizations may establish a formal security awareness policy or procedure defining:

* Awareness objectives.
* Roles and responsibilities.
* Training frequency.
* Target audiences.
* Delivery methods.
* Records.
* Measurement.
* Reporting.

This helps establish governance over the awareness program.

## Awareness Program Ownership

Responsibilities should be clearly assigned.

For example:

### CISO / Security Leadership

Provides strategic direction.

### GRC

Maps awareness requirements to:

* Policies.
* Standards.
* Risks.
* Regulations.

### HR

Supports employee onboarding and training records.

### IT

Supports technical awareness and systems.

### Business Managers

Ensure personnel participate.

### Employees

Complete required training and follow security requirements.

## Security Awareness During Onboarding

New employees should receive appropriate security awareness as part of onboarding.

Topics may include:

* Security policies.
* Passwords.
* MFA.
* Phishing.
* Data handling.
* Acceptable use.
* Incident reporting.
* Physical security.

This establishes security expectations from the beginning of employment.

## Ongoing Awareness

Awareness should not be limited to onboarding.

Security threats change continuously.

Organizations should provide periodic awareness activities covering:

* Emerging threats.
* New policies.
* Recent incidents.
* Regulatory changes.
* New technologies.

This supports continual reinforcement.

## Security Awareness for High-Risk Roles

Some personnel require enhanced awareness or training.

Examples include:

* Privileged administrators.
* Developers.
* Security personnel.
* Executives.
* Finance employees.
* HR personnel.
* Privacy teams.

For example, finance personnel may receive additional training on:

* Business Email Compromise.
* Payment fraud.
* Vendor impersonation.

## Role-Based Training

ISO/IEC 27001 supports the broader concept that competence and awareness should be appropriate to people's responsibilities.

For example:

### Executive

Security governance and risk.

### General Employee

Phishing and data protection.

### Developer

Secure development.

### System Administrator

Secure configuration and privileged access.

### GRC Professional

Risk, compliance, and control requirements.

This improves relevance and effectiveness.

## Security Awareness for Third Parties

The organization's security environment can include:

* Contractors.
* Consultants.
* Vendors.
* Service providers.

Where relevant, awareness requirements should extend to external personnel working under the organization's control or having access to its information and systems.

This connects ISO/IEC 27001 awareness with **third-party risk management**.

## Security Awareness Records

Organizations should maintain appropriate evidence demonstrating that awareness and training activities have occurred.

Examples include:

* Training completion records.
* Attendance records.
* Assessment results.
* Policy acknowledgments.
* Awareness campaign records.

These records can help demonstrate implementation during audits.

## Audit Evidence

During an ISO/IEC 27001 audit, an organization may need to demonstrate that its awareness and competence processes are operating effectively.

Possible evidence includes:

* Security awareness policy.
* Training plans.
* Training materials.
* Attendance records.
* Learning Management System reports.
* Phishing simulation results.
* Competence assessments.
* Role-based training matrices.

The auditor may be interested in whether the process actually works, not merely whether a policy exists.

## Example Audit Question

An auditor might ask:

> "How do you ensure employees understand their information-security responsibilities?"

A weak answer would be:

> "Employees sign the security policy."

A stronger answer would demonstrate:

1. Defined awareness requirements.
2. Role-based training.
3. Periodic awareness activities.
4. Training completion monitoring.
5. Effectiveness measurement.
6. Corrective actions for gaps.

## Security Awareness and Risk Treatment

Security awareness can be a risk treatment.

Consider:

> **Risk:** Employees may fall victim to credential phishing.

Possible treatment:

* Phishing awareness.
* MFA.
* Email security.
* Phishing simulations.
* Reporting mechanisms.

Awareness therefore becomes one component of a broader risk treatment strategy.

## Awareness Is Not a Substitute for Technical Controls

An important principle is:

> **Security awareness should complement technical controls, not replace them.**

For example, telling employees:

> "Never click phishing links."

is useful.

But the organization should also consider:

* Email filtering.
* URL protection.
* MFA.
* Endpoint protection.
* Monitoring.

Human behavior is one security layer among several.

## Measuring Awareness Effectiveness

Organizations should measure more than training completion.

Possible metrics include:

### Completion

Percentage of personnel completing required training.

### Knowledge

Assessment scores.

### Behavior

Phishing simulation results.

### Reporting

Number of suspicious emails reported.

### Incidents

Human-related security incidents.

### Risk

Reduction in human-related security risk.

## Example Metrics

| Metric                           | Result |    Target |
| -------------------------------- | -----: | --------: |
| Security training completion     |    98% |      >95% |
| Average assessment score         |    91% |      >85% |
| Phishing click rate              |     4% |       <5% |
| Phishing reporting rate          |    76% |      >70% |
| Human-related security incidents |      8 | Declining |

These metrics provide stronger evidence than completion rates alone.

## Continual Improvement

ISO/IEC 27001 follows a continual-improvement philosophy.

Security awareness should therefore be periodically reviewed.

The organization should ask:

* Are employees learning?
* Are behaviors changing?
* Are incidents decreasing?
* Are new threats emerging?
* Are high-risk groups receiving appropriate training?
* Are regulatory requirements changing?

The answers should influence future awareness activities.

## Corrective Actions

If awareness measurements identify weaknesses, the organization should take corrective action.

For example:

**Phishing failure rate increases**

↓

Analyze affected group

↓

Identify knowledge gap

↓

Provide targeted training

↓

Conduct additional simulation

↓

Measure improvement

This demonstrates a data-driven approach.

## Security Awareness and the PDCA Model

The **Plan-Do-Check-Act (PDCA)** approach can be applied to awareness.

### Plan

Identify risks and awareness requirements.

### Do

Deliver training and campaigns.

### Check

Measure knowledge and behavior.

### Act

Improve the program based on results.

Then the cycle repeats.

## Common Mistakes

Organizations should avoid:

### Treating Training Completion as the Objective

Completion does not prove behavior change.

### Providing the Same Training to Everyone

Different roles have different risks.

### Conducting Training Only Once a Year

Awareness requires continuous reinforcement.

### Ignoring Third Parties

External personnel may have significant access.

### Failing to Maintain Evidence

Lack of records can make it difficult to demonstrate implementation.

### Focusing Only on Policies

Employees need practical guidance, not just policy documents.

### Ignoring Effectiveness

Organizations should evaluate whether awareness actually improves security behavior.

### Treating Awareness as an IT-Only Responsibility

Security awareness requires coordination across:

* Security.
* GRC.
* HR.
* IT.
* Business management.

## Key Takeaways

ISO/IEC 27001 treats people as an important component of information-security management.

The key principles are:

1. **Security awareness supports the effectiveness of the ISMS.**
2. **Competence and awareness are related but distinct concepts.**
3. **Personnel should understand relevant security policies and responsibilities.**
4. **Training should be appropriate to organizational risk and job responsibilities.**
5. **ISO/IEC 27001 Clause 7.2 addresses competence.**
6. **ISO/IEC 27001 Clause 7.3 addresses awareness.**
7. **Annex A includes A.6.3, Information Security Awareness, Education and Training.**
8. **Security awareness should be risk-based and role-based.**
9. **Third parties should be considered where they fall within the organization's security requirements and risk environment.**
10. **Training completion alone does not demonstrate effectiveness.**
11. **Organizations should measure knowledge, behavior, incidents, and risk outcomes.**
12. **Training and awareness records provide important evidence for audits and assurance.**
13. **Awareness should be continuously reinforced and improved.**
14. **Security awareness should complement technical and procedural controls.**
15. **GRC teams play an important role in connecting awareness with risk, compliance, and ISMS requirements.**

The ultimate objective is not:

> **"Everyone completed security training."**

The stronger objective is:

> **"People understand their information-security responsibilities, demonstrate appropriate security behavior, and contribute effectively to the organization's ISMS."**

An effective ISO/IEC 27001-aligned awareness program therefore transforms security awareness from a **compliance checkbox into an active component of information-security governance, risk management, and continual improvement**.

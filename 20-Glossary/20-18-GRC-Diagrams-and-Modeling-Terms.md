# 20.18 GRC Diagram and Modeling Terms

This section defines the terminology used to **visualize, model, map, analyze, communicate, and document GRC relationships** across governance, risk, compliance, cybersecurity, privacy, business continuity, AI governance, third-party risk, and internal audit.

The objective is to help a GRC professional understand not only individual diagrams, but also **how GRC information is connected and modeled**.

---

## 20.18.1 Core GRC Modeling Concepts

| Term                            | Definition                                                                                                                              |
| ------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **GRC Model**                   | Structured representation of governance, risk, compliance, controls, processes, responsibilities, and their relationships.              |
| **GRC Modeling**                | Process of representing GRC structures, relationships, dependencies, processes, risks, controls, and information visually or logically. |
| **GRC Diagram**                 | Visual representation of GRC processes, structures, relationships, dependencies, or information.                                        |
| **GRC Visual Model**            | Visual representation designed to communicate a GRC concept, structure, process, or relationship.                                       |
| **GRC Architecture Diagram**    | Diagram showing the high-level architecture of an organization's GRC environment.                                                       |
| **GRC Process Model**           | Representation of activities, decisions, inputs, outputs, owners, and controls within a GRC process.                                    |
| **GRC Relationship Model**      | Model showing relationships among risks, controls, requirements, processes, assets, owners, and other GRC objects.                      |
| **GRC Operating Model Diagram** | Visual representation of how GRC responsibilities, functions, governance bodies, processes, and technology interact.                    |
| **GRC Capability Model**        | Representation of the capabilities required to perform effective GRC.                                                                   |
| **GRC Maturity Model**          | Model used to represent progression from basic to advanced levels of GRC capability or maturity.                                        |
| **GRC Reference Model**         | Reusable conceptual model describing common GRC components and relationships.                                                           |
| **GRC Conceptual Model**        | High-level representation of major GRC concepts and how they relate to each other.                                                      |
| **GRC Logical Model**           | More detailed representation of GRC entities, attributes, relationships, and logical dependencies.                                      |
| **GRC Physical Model**          | Representation of how GRC information and processes are implemented within actual systems and technologies.                             |

# 20.18.5 Bow-Tie Modeling

The **Bow-Tie Model** is particularly useful in cybersecurity, operational risk, business continuity, and safety-related GRC.

A simplified model is:

**Threats / Causes**

↓

**Preventive Controls**

↓

### **Top Event**

↓

**Detective / Mitigating Controls**

↓

**Consequences**

For example:

**Phishing Attack**

→ Security Awareness

→ Email Security

→ MFA

→ **Account Compromise**

→ Incident Detection

→ SOC Response

→ Password Reset

→ **Business / Data Impact**

The left side focuses primarily on **prevention**, while the right side focuses primarily on **consequence reduction and response**.

**Business Objectives**

↓

**Governance**

↓

**Risk Management**

↓

**Compliance**

↓

**Control Management**

↓

**Assurance**

↓

**Technology**

↓

**Data**

↓

**Reporting**

↓

**Decision-Making**

The model should also show **feedback loops**.

For example:

**Audit Finding**

→ Risk Assessment

→ Control Improvement

→ Policy Update

→ Training

→ Monitoring

→ New Evidence

→ Assurance

This demonstrates that GRC is a **continuous management system**, rather than a collection of static documents.

# 20.18.21 GRC Diagram Quality Principles

A professional GRC diagram should be:

### 1. Purpose-driven

Every diagram should answer a specific question.

### 2. Audience-appropriate

A board diagram should not contain the same level of technical detail as an engineering diagram.

### 3. Traceable

Important objects should connect to their corresponding:

**Risk → Control → Requirement → Evidence → Finding → Remediation**

### 4. Consistent

Use consistent terminology, symbols, naming conventions, and relationships.

### 5. Layered

Avoid putting governance, technical architecture, detailed workflows, and evidence into one overloaded diagram.

### 6. Maintainable

The diagram should be easy to update when processes, systems, regulations, or controls change.

### 7. Version-controlled

Important GRC diagrams should have:

* owner;
* version;
* approval;
* effective date;
* review date;
* classification;
* change history.

### 8. Auditable

Where appropriate, diagram elements should be traceable to documented requirements, controls, processes, or systems.

# 20.18.22 Common GRC Diagram Errors

### Overloaded Diagram

Trying to put the entire GRC environment into one diagram.

### Missing Ownership

Showing processes without identifying who is responsible.

### Missing Control Relationships

Showing risks without showing how they are controlled.

### Missing Dependencies

Ignoring dependencies between systems, suppliers, processes, and controls.

### No Traceability

Showing a requirement without demonstrating how it becomes an implemented control.

### Inconsistent Terminology

Using different names for the same GRC object.

### Excessive Technical Detail

Providing technical architecture when the audience needs governance information.

### Excessive Simplification

Removing important risk, control, or accountability relationships.

### Static Diagrams

Failing to update diagrams when the underlying environment changes.

# Key GRC Modeling Takeaway

The purpose of GRC modeling is to make **relationships visible**.

A strong GRC professional should be able to move from:

> **What is the requirement?**

to:

> **What risk does it address?**

to:

> **What control manages the risk?**

to:

> **Which process and system implement the control?**

to:

> **What evidence proves it operated?**

to:

> **Who tested it?**

to:

> **What finding resulted?**

to:

> **How was it remediated?**

to:

> **Who accepted the remaining risk?**

That is the essence of **GRC traceability and GRC modeling**.

In an advanced GRC environment, diagrams are therefore not merely presentation artifacts. They become **management and assurance models** that help executives, auditors, risk owners, security teams, compliance teams, and regulators understand how the organization's **risks, obligations, controls, processes, technology, and accountability are interconnected**.

## Introduction

Effective incident management requires more than technical expertise—it requires a structured, repeatable, and well-governed approach to preparing for, detecting, responding to, recovering from, and learning from information security incidents. To help organizations establish such an approach, the **ISO/IEC 27035** family of standards provides internationally recognized guidance for planning, implementing, operating, maintaining, and continually improving an Information Security Incident Management capability.

ISO/IEC 27035 complements the Information Security Management System (ISMS) established under **ISO/IEC 27001** by providing detailed guidance on how organizations should manage information security incidents throughout their lifecycle. Rather than focusing solely on incident response, the standard promotes a comprehensive incident management program that includes governance, planning, preparation, detection, reporting, assessment, response, recovery, post-incident activities, and continual improvement.

The standard applies to organizations of all sizes and sectors and supports integration with business continuity management, disaster recovery, enterprise risk management, digital forensics, legal requirements, and regulatory obligations. Organizations implementing ISO/IEC 27035 improve their ability to respond consistently to incidents while reducing business disruption, protecting information assets, and strengthening organizational resilience.

Within Governance, Risk, and Compliance (GRC), ISO/IEC 27035 provides a governance framework that aligns incident management with organizational objectives, enterprise risk management, and compliance obligations. It also serves as an important reference during internal audits, certification activities, regulatory assessments, and cybersecurity maturity evaluations.

# What is ISO/IEC 27035?

**ISO/IEC 27035** is an international standard that provides guidance for establishing, implementing, operating, maintaining, and continually improving an Information Security Incident Management capability.

Its primary objectives are to:

- Prepare organizations for security incidents.
- Detect and report incidents promptly.
- Assess incident severity.
- Coordinate effective response activities.
- Recover affected services.
- Capture lessons learned.
- Support continual improvement.
- Reduce business risk.

The standard promotes a consistent and repeatable approach to incident management.

# NIST SP 800-61 Revision 2

The **National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) Special Publication (SP) 800-61 Revision 2**, titled **Computer Security Incident Handling Guide**, is one of the most widely adopted cybersecurity references for establishing and operating an effective incident response capability. Although originally developed for U.S. federal agencies, its principles and best practices have been adopted globally by government organizations, critical infrastructure operators, multinational enterprises, financial institutions, healthcare providers, and private organizations of all sizes.

NIST SP 800-61 Rev. 2 provides practical guidance for preparing for cybersecurity incidents, detecting and analyzing threats, containing attacks, eradicating malicious activity, recovering affected systems, and continuously improving incident response capabilities. Unlike standards that focus primarily on governance, NIST SP 800-61 provides operational guidance for incident handling teams, including Security Operations Centers (SOCs), Computer Security Incident Response Teams (CSIRTs), and Information Technology (IT) operations.

The publication emphasizes that incident response is not an isolated security function but an integral part of enterprise risk management. Effective incident handling requires collaboration between technical teams, executive leadership, legal counsel, human resources, business continuity, disaster recovery, communications, and external stakeholders. Organizations should also integrate incident response with vulnerability management, threat intelligence, risk management, and security awareness programs.

NIST SP 800-61 aligns closely with other internationally recognized frameworks such as **ISO/IEC 27035**, **ISO/IEC 27001**, the **NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF)**, **ISO 22301 Business Continuity Management Systems**, and the **Center for Internet Security (CIS) Controls**. Within Governance, Risk, and Compliance (GRC), it provides practical operational guidance that strengthens governance, supports enterprise risk management, and demonstrates cybersecurity maturity.

# The Four Phases of the NIST Incident Response Lifecycle

NIST SP 800-61 defines four major phases of incident handling.

### 1. Preparation

Organizations establish the people, processes, technologies, and governance required to respond effectively to incidents.

Activities include:

- Developing incident response policies.
- Defining response procedures.
- Establishing CSIRTs.
- Deploying security tools.
- Conducting training and exercises.

---

### 2. Detection and Analysis

Organizations identify potential security events, determine whether they are incidents, assess their severity, and begin investigation.

Activities include:

- Monitoring security events.
- Validating alerts.
- Classifying incidents.
- Assessing business impact.
- Collecting evidence.

---

### 3. Containment, Eradication, and Recovery

Organizations limit the spread of the incident, remove the threat, restore systems, and return business operations to normal.

Activities include:

- Isolating affected systems.
- Removing malicious software.
- Applying security patches.
- Restoring systems from backups.
- Validating system integrity.

---

### 4. Post-Incident Activity

Organizations review the incident to identify lessons learned and improve future response capabilities.

Activities include:

- Conducting post-incident reviews.
- Documenting root causes.
- Updating policies and procedures.
- Improving security controls.
- Tracking corrective actions.

# CIS Controls and Incident Response

The **Center for Internet Security (CIS) Controls** are a globally recognized set of prioritized cybersecurity best practices designed to help organizations defend against the most common cyber threats. Developed by cybersecurity practitioners from government, industry, and academia, the CIS Controls provide practical and actionable guidance for implementing effective security measures. While the controls address a broad range of cybersecurity domains, several are directly related to incident detection, response, recovery, and continual improvement.

Unlike comprehensive management standards such as **ISO/IEC 27001** or operational guidance such as **NIST SP 800-61** and **ISO/IEC 27035**, the CIS Controls focus on implementing specific technical and administrative safeguards that reduce cyber risk. They help organizations build a strong security foundation that supports effective incident management by improving visibility, strengthening defensive capabilities, and enabling faster detection and response to security incidents.

The current version, **CIS Controls v8**, consists of **18 controls** organized into three Implementation Groups (IG1, IG2, and IG3), allowing organizations to adopt security controls based on their size, complexity, and risk profile. Several of these controls directly support incident response by enhancing logging, monitoring, vulnerability management, malware defenses, access control, data protection, and security awareness.

Within Governance, Risk, and Compliance (GRC), the CIS Controls provide measurable security practices that strengthen governance, support enterprise risk management, improve operational resilience, and help organizations demonstrate compliance with industry standards and regulatory requirements.

# Mapping Incident Response Standards

Organizations rarely rely on a single cybersecurity framework or standard to build their incident management capability. Instead, they typically adopt multiple standards and best practices that complement one another. For example, an organization may implement **ISO/IEC 27001** to establish its Information Security Management System (ISMS), **ISO/IEC 27035** to manage information security incidents, **NIST SP 800-61 Revision 2** to guide operational incident handling, and the **Center for Internet Security (CIS) Controls v8** to implement practical technical and administrative safeguards. Understanding how these frameworks align enables organizations to create a comprehensive, integrated, and efficient incident management program.

**Mapping incident response standards** is the process of identifying relationships, similarities, and complementary requirements across multiple cybersecurity standards and frameworks. Rather than treating each framework as an independent requirement, organizations can harmonize controls, policies, procedures, governance activities, and compliance obligations. This reduces duplication of effort, improves consistency, simplifies audits, and supports enterprise-wide cybersecurity governance.

Although each framework has a different purpose, they share common objectives, including preparing for incidents, detecting and analyzing threats, responding effectively, recovering business operations, documenting lessons learned, and continually improving cybersecurity capabilities. Mapping these standards enables organizations to build a mature incident management capability while satisfying multiple regulatory and business requirements simultaneously.

Within Governance, Risk, and Compliance (GRC), standards mapping strengthens governance by creating a unified control environment, improves enterprise risk management through consistent security practices, and supports regulatory compliance across multiple legal, contractual, and industry obligations.

# Practical Example

A multinational telecommunications company maintains ISO/IEC 27001 certification for its Information Security Management System (ISMS), adopts ISO/IEC 27035 for incident management governance, uses NIST SP 800-61 Revision 2 as the operational guide for its Computer Security Incident Response Team (CSIRT), and implements CIS Controls v8 to strengthen technical security controls. Rather than maintaining separate procedures for each framework, the organization maps common requirements into a single incident response policy, unified playbooks, centralized evidence management process, and integrated control library. During internal and external audits, the organization demonstrates how one incident management process satisfies the requirements of multiple standards, reducing audit complexity while improving governance and operational efficiency.

This example illustrates how mapping cybersecurity standards enables organizations to build a cohesive, efficient, and mature incident management program while meeting diverse business and regulatory requirements.

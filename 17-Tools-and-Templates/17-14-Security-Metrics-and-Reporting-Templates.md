# 17.14 Security Metrics and Reporting Templates

## Part 1 – Cybersecurity KPI Template

Cybersecurity Key Performance Indicators (KPIs) are measurable indicators used to determine whether cybersecurity processes, controls, programs, and activities are performing according to defined objectives.

KPIs focus primarily on performance. They help GRC professionals and security management determine whether planned activities are being completed effectively and whether security processes are operating within established expectations.

A cybersecurity KPI should be measurable, clearly defined, relevant to a business or security objective, assigned to an owner, and measured consistently over time.

A practical Cybersecurity KPI Template can contain:

```text
CYBERSECURITY KPI TEMPLATE

KPI ID:

KPI Name:

KPI Description:

Business Objective:

Security Objective:

Related Risk:

Related Control:

Related Framework Requirement:

KPI Owner:

Data Owner:

Measurement Frequency:

Reporting Frequency:

Measurement Period:

Calculation Method:

Data Source:

Unit of Measurement:

Baseline:

Target:

Threshold:

Current Result:

Previous Result:

Trend:

Performance Status:

Management Commentary:

Required Action:

Action Owner:

Target Completion Date:

Evidence Source:

Review Date:

Approval:
```

The KPI should begin with a clear **KPI name**.

For example:

```text
KPI Name:

Critical Vulnerability Remediation Rate
```

The KPI description should explain exactly what is being measured.

For example:

```text
KPI Description:

Percentage of critical vulnerabilities
remediated within the organization's
defined remediation timeframe.
```

The KPI should identify the relevant **business objective**.

For example:

```text
Business Objective:

Reduce operational and cybersecurity
risk associated with vulnerable systems.
```

The KPI should identify the related **security objective**.

For example:

```text
Security Objective:

Ensure critical vulnerabilities are
identified and remediated within
approved risk-based timeframes.
```

Where appropriate, the KPI should be linked to a specific **risk**.

For example:

```text
Related Risk:

Exploitation of unpatched critical
vulnerabilities may result in unauthorized
access, service disruption, or data
compromise.
```

The KPI should also be linked to relevant **security controls**.

For example:

```text
Related Control:

Vulnerability Management

Patch Management

Security Monitoring
```

The KPI may also be mapped to relevant frameworks or standards.

For example:

```text
Related Framework Requirement:

ISO/IEC 27001
NIST Cybersecurity Framework
Organizational Security Policy
```

The KPI should have a clearly assigned **owner**.

For example:

```text
KPI Owner:

Vulnerability Management Manager
```

The KPI owner is responsible for ensuring that the metric is properly measured, reviewed, and acted upon.

The KPI should identify the **data owner**.

For example:

```text
Data Owner:

Security Operations
```

The organization should define the **measurement frequency**.

Examples include:

```text
Daily
Weekly
Monthly
Quarterly
Annually
```

For example:

```text
Measurement Frequency:

Weekly
```

The organization should also define the **reporting frequency**.

For example:

```text
Reporting Frequency:

Monthly
```

The KPI should define the **measurement period**.

For example:

```text
Measurement Period:

1 July 2026 – 31 July 2026
```

The KPI should document the **calculation method**.

For example:

```text
Calculation:

Number of critical vulnerabilities
remediated within SLA
÷
Total number of critical vulnerabilities
due for remediation
× 100
```

A clearly defined calculation method ensures that the KPI is consistently measured.

The KPI should identify its **data source**.

Examples include:

```text
SIEM
Vulnerability Management Platform
GRC Platform
Ticketing System
IT Service Management Platform
Endpoint Management Platform
Audit Management System
```

For example:

```text
Data Source:

Vulnerability Management Platform
```

The KPI should identify the **unit of measurement**.

Examples include:

```text
Percentage
Number
Days
Hours
Ratio
Score
Rate
```

For example:

```text
Unit of Measurement:

Percentage
```

The organization should establish a **baseline**.

The baseline represents the starting or historical performance level against which improvement can be measured.

For example:

```text
Baseline:

78%
```

The KPI should define a measurable **target**.

For example:

```text
Target:

95%
```

The target should be realistic, risk-based, and aligned with organizational objectives.

The KPI should also define **thresholds**.

For example:

```text
Green:

≥ 95%

Amber:

90% – 94%

Red:

< 90%
```

Thresholds allow management to quickly identify performance that requires attention.

The KPI should record the **current result**.

For example:

```text
Current Result:

92%
```

The KPI should compare the current result with the **previous result**.

For example:

```text
Previous Result:

88%
```

This enables the organization to determine whether performance is improving or deteriorating.

The KPI should record the **trend**.

For example:

```text
Trend:

Improving
```

Possible trend values include:

```text
Improving
Stable
Deteriorating
```

The KPI should have a clear **performance status**.

For example:

```text
Performance Status:

Amber – Target Not Yet Achieved
```

A KPI should not simply report a number. It should help management understand what the number means.

For example:

```text
KPI:

Critical Vulnerability Remediation Rate

Target:
95%

Current:
92%

Status:
Amber

Interpretation:

Performance has improved from the previous
month but remains below the approved target.
```

The KPI should include **management commentary** where appropriate.

For example:

```text
Management Commentary:

Performance improved due to increased
patching capacity. Remaining delays are
primarily associated with legacy systems
requiring additional testing.
```

The KPI should identify any **required action**.

For example:

```text
Required Action:

Develop remediation plans for remaining
critical vulnerabilities and escalate
overdue remediation activities.
```

The action should have an assigned **owner**.

For example:

```text
Action Owner:

Infrastructure Security Manager
```

A **target completion date** should be assigned.

For example:

```text
Target Completion Date:

30 September 2026
```

The KPI should identify the **evidence source** supporting the reported result.

For example:

```text
Evidence Source:

Monthly Vulnerability Management Report

GRC Evidence Repository:
VM-2026-07
```

This is particularly important when KPI results are used for audits, regulatory assessments, management reviews, or certification activities.

The KPI should have a defined **review date**.

For example:

```text
Review Date:

15 August 2026
```

The KPI should be reviewed periodically to confirm that it remains relevant and continues to measure something meaningful.

A GRC professional should avoid creating KPIs simply because data is available.

The KPI should exist because it helps answer a meaningful management question.

For example:

```text
Weak Metric:

Number of Security Tickets

Better KPI:

Percentage of High-Severity Security
Tickets Resolved Within SLA
```

The second KPI provides more information about performance.

Another example:

```text
Weak Metric:

Number of Employees Trained

Better KPI:

Percentage of Employees Completing
Required Security Awareness Training
Within the Defined Training Period
```

Another example:

```text
Weak Metric:

Number of Security Incidents

Better KPI:

Percentage of Security Incidents
Contained Within the Defined Response
Time Objective
```

The GRC professional should distinguish between **activity metrics** and **performance KPIs**.

An activity metric measures what was done.

For example:

```text
Number of Security Assessments Completed
```

A KPI measures performance against an objective.

For example:

```text
Percentage of High-Risk Findings Remediated
Within the Approved Remediation Period
```

Both may be useful, but they serve different purposes.

Cybersecurity KPIs may be established across different security and GRC domains.

Examples include:

```text
Vulnerability Management

Patch Management

Security Incident Management

Access Management

Security Awareness

Third-Party Risk Management

Compliance Management

Risk Treatment

Audit Remediation

Business Continuity

Disaster Recovery

Security Operations

Data Protection

Privacy

Cloud Security

Application Security
```

Example KPI:

```text
KPI:

Target:

100%

Calculation:

Completed privileged access reviews
÷
Required privileged access reviews
× 100
```

Example KPI:

```text
KPI:

Security Incident Response SLA Compliance

Target:

≥ 95%

Calculation:

Incidents responded to within SLA
÷
Total incidents requiring response
× 100
```

Example KPI:

```text
KPI:

Third-Party Security Assessment Completion

Target:

100%

Calculation:

Completed assessments
÷
Required assessments
× 100
```

Example KPI:

```text
KPI:

High-Risk Audit Finding Closure Rate

Target:

≥ 90%

Calculation:

High-risk findings closed within
approved timeframe
÷
High-risk findings due
× 100
```

Example KPI:

```text
KPI:

Risk Treatment Action Completion Rate

Target:

≥ 95%

Calculation:

Completed treatment actions
÷
Treatment actions due
× 100
```

Example KPI:

```text
KPI:

Security Awareness Training Completion

Target:

≥ 98%

Calculation:

Employees completing required training
÷
Employees required to complete training
× 100
```

KPIs should also be designed with data quality in mind.

The GRC professional should verify:

```text
Data Is Accurate

Data Is Complete

Data Is Timely

Data Is Consistent

Calculation Is Reproducible

Data Source Is Reliable

Measurement Period Is Defined
```

Poor-quality data can produce misleading management conclusions.

For example:

```text
Reported KPI:

98% Compliance

Problem:

20% of required records were missing
from the source system.

Conclusion:

KPI Result Is Not Reliable
```

The GRC professional should therefore validate the data source before relying on the KPI.

KPIs should also be reviewed periodically to ensure that they remain aligned with organizational objectives.

A KPI may become ineffective when:

```text
Business Objectives Change

Risk Profile Changes

Regulatory Requirements Change

Technology Changes

Security Strategy Changes

Control Objectives Change

The Metric No Longer Drives Useful Decisions
```

For example, an organization may initially measure:

```text
Number of Security Incidents
```

As its security program matures, it may introduce more meaningful measures such as:

```text
Mean Time to Detect
Mean Time to Respond
Percentage of Incidents Contained Within SLA
Percentage of Recurring Incidents
```

KPIs should ultimately support decision-making.

A useful KPI should help management determine:

```text
Are We Meeting Our Objectives?

Are Security Controls Performing?

Is Risk Increasing or Decreasing?

Are Remediation Activities Effective?

Where Is Management Attention Required?

Are Resources Being Used Effectively?

Are Security Investments Producing Results?
```

The relationship between objectives, controls, KPIs, and management action can be represented as:

```text
Business Objective
        ↓
Security Objective
        ↓
Risk
        ↓
Security Control
        ↓
KPI
        ↓
Measurement
        ↓
Performance Result
        ↓
Management Decision
        ↓
Corrective or Improvement Action
```

The GRC professional should ensure that KPIs are incorporated into appropriate governance and reporting processes.

KPIs may be reported to:

```text
Security Management

Risk Committee

Executive Management

Board or Board Committees

Audit Committee

Compliance Committee

Business Owners
```

The level of detail should depend on the audience.

Operational teams may require detailed KPI data.

Senior management may require summarized trends, exceptions, risks, and required decisions.

For example:

```text
Operational View:

Critical Vulnerability Remediation:
92%

Target:
95%

Overdue:
17 Systems

Top Cause:
Legacy Infrastructure
```

Executive view:

```text
Vulnerability Management KPI:

Status:
Amber

Trend:
Improving

Target:
95%

Current:
92%

Management Attention:

17 systems remain overdue for
critical vulnerability remediation.
```

The GRC professional should ensure that KPI reporting does not create a false impression of security effectiveness.

A high KPI result does not necessarily mean that the organization is secure.

For example:

```text
KPI:

Patch Compliance:
98%

Potential Issue:

The remaining 2% may contain the
organization's most critical systems.
```

Metrics should therefore be interpreted together with risk information.

The relationship can be represented as:

```text
KPI Result
    +
Risk Context
    +
Control Effectiveness
    +
Threat Environment
    ↓
Management Decision
```

The GRC professional should also distinguish KPIs from **Key Risk Indicators (KRIs)**.

KPIs primarily measure performance against objectives.

KRIs provide indicators of changing risk exposure.

For example:

```text
KPI:

Percentage of critical vulnerabilities
remediated within SLA.

KRI:

Number of critical vulnerabilities
exceeding the approved remediation period.
```

Both should be considered together.

The GRC professional should verify that:

```text
KPIs Have Defined Objectives

KPIs Have Assigned Owners

KPIs Have Defined Calculations

KPIs Use Reliable Data

KPIs Have Defined Targets

KPIs Have Defined Thresholds

KPIs Are Measured Consistently

KPIs Are Reviewed Regularly

KPI Results Are Supported by Evidence

Poor Performance Generates Action

KPI Trends Are Reported

KPIs Remain Aligned With Risk

KPIs Support Management Decisions
```

The key principle is:

> **A cybersecurity KPI should transform security and GRC performance data into a measurable indicator that helps management determine whether objectives are being achieved, where performance is falling short, and where corrective or improvement action is required.**


**Part 2 – Cybersecurity KRI Template**

Cybersecurity Key Risk Indicators (KRIs) are measurable indicators used to identify changes in an organization's cybersecurity risk exposure. While Key Performance Indicators (KPIs) primarily measure performance against objectives, KRIs focus on conditions that may indicate increasing, decreasing, or emerging risk.

A KRI should provide an early warning that a risk may be approaching or exceeding an organization's defined tolerance or appetite.

A practical Cybersecurity KRI Template can contain:

```text

KRI Name:

KRI Description:

Related Risk:

Risk Category:

Risk Owner:

Business Owner:

Security Objective:

Related Control:

Related Framework Requirement:

Measurement Frequency:

Reporting Frequency:

Measurement Period:

Data Source:

Calculation Method:

Unit of Measurement:

Risk Appetite:

Amber Threshold:

Trend:

Risk Status:

Potential Impact:

Management Commentary:

Required Action:

Action Owner:

Evidence Source:

Approval:
```

For example:

```text

Critical Vulnerabilities Exceeding
Remediation SLA
```

For example:


The KRI should identify the **related risk**.

The KRI should consider the organization's **risk appetite**.

Risk appetite represents the level and type of risk the organization is generally willing to accept in pursuit of its objectives.

For example:

```text
Risk Appetite:

Very low tolerance for unresolved
critical vulnerabilities affecting
internet-facing systems.
```

The KRI should also define a **risk tolerance**.

Risk tolerance establishes the acceptable variation around the organization's risk position.

For example:

```text
Risk Tolerance:

No more than 5 critical vulnerabilities

remediation period.
```

The organization should define **green, amber, and red thresholds**.

For example:

```text
Green:

0–5 critical vulnerabilities

Amber:

6–10 critical vulnerabilities

Red:

More than 10 critical vulnerabilities
```

These thresholds provide a simple way to communicate changing risk exposure.

The GRC professional should distinguish between **KPIs and KRIs**.

A KPI answers:

```text
Are we performing as expected?
```

A KRI answers:

```text
Is our risk exposure changing?
```

For example:

```text
KPI:

Percentage of critical vulnerabilities
remediated within SLA.

KRI:

Number of critical vulnerabilities
remaining beyond SLA.
```

The KPI may show that remediation performance is improving while the KRI may still show that overall exposure remains high.

For example:

```text
KPI:

Remediation SLA Compliance:
96%

KRI:

Critical Vulnerabilities Above SLA:
18

Interpretation:

The remediation process is performing well
against its target, but the organization
still has significant residual exposure.
```

This demonstrates why KPIs and KRIs should not be viewed independently.

The relationship can be represented as:

```text
KPI
Performance Measurement
        +
KRI
Risk Exposure Measurement
        ↓
Combined Management View
        ↓
Risk-Based Decision
```

KRIs should be connected to the organization's **risk appetite and tolerance**.

For example:

```text
Risk Appetite:

Low tolerance for critical third-party
security exposure.

KRI:

Critical Suppliers Without Current
Security Assessment

Tolerance:

0

Current:

3

Result:

Risk Exceeds Tolerance
```

When a KRI exceeds tolerance, the organization should have a defined escalation process.

For example:

```text
KRI Threshold Exceeded
        ↓
Risk Owner Notified
        ↓
Risk Assessment
        ↓
Management Review
        ↓
Risk Treatment / Mitigation
        ↓
Risk Acceptance if Appropriate
        ↓
Monitoring
```

The GRC professional should determine whether KRI threshold breaches require escalation to:

```text
Risk Owner

CISO

Business Owner

Risk Committee

Compliance Committee

Executive Management

Board or Board Committee
```

The escalation level should depend on the severity and potential impact of the risk.

KRIs should also support **early warning**.

For example:


The GRC professional should avoid excessive numbers of KRIs.

A large number of poorly designed indicators can make risk reporting less effective.

A practical KRI portfolio should focus on the organization's most significant risks.

For example:

```text
Top Cybersecurity Risks:

Ransomware
Third-Party Compromise
Privileged Account Abuse
Data Breach
Cloud Misconfiguration
Critical Vulnerability Exploitation
Regulatory Non-Compliance
```

Each major risk may have one or more KRIs.

For example:

```text
Risk:

Ransomware

KRIs:

Critical vulnerabilities above SLA

Percentage of endpoints without
effective protection

Backup restoration failures

Percentage of critical systems
without tested recovery capability
```

The GRC professional should verify:

```text

```

The organization should also document how KRI data is validated.

For example:



```text

```

The KRI should not be manually adjusted simply to achieve a desired status.

Any change to the calculation methodology should be documented and approved.

The GRC professional should also consider the difference between **leading and lagging indicators**.

A leading KRI provides an indication that risk may increase before an adverse event occurs.

For example:

```text
Leading KRI:

Percentage of critical systems
without current security patches
```

A lagging KRI measures a condition that has already occurred.

For example:

```text
Lagging KRI:

Number of confirmed data breaches
```

Both can be useful, but leading indicators can provide greater opportunity for preventive action.

A mature cybersecurity risk monitoring program should use a combination of leading and lagging indicators.

The relationship can be represented as:

```text
Threat / Change
      ↓
Leading KRI
      ↓
Increasing Risk Exposure
      ↓
Management Intervention
      ↓
Risk Treatment
      ↓
Reduced Exposure
```

If intervention does not occur:

```text
Increasing Risk Exposure
      ↓
Security Event
      ↓
Incident
      ↓
Impact
      ↓
Lagging KRI
```

The GRC professional should ensure that KRI thresholds are periodically reviewed.

Thresholds should reflect:

```text
Risk Appetite

Risk Tolerance

Business Impact

Threat Environment

Regulatory Requirements

Security Objectives

Historical Performance

Management Expectations
```

The GRC professional should verify that:

```text
KRIs Are Linked to Material Risks

Risk Owners Are Assigned

KRI Definitions Are Clear

Calculations Are Documented

Data Sources Are Reliable

Baselines Are Established

Risk Appetite Is Defined

Risk Tolerance Is Defined

Thresholds Are Established

Current Results Are Recorded

Trends Are Monitored

Threshold Breaches Are Escalated

Management Actions Are Documented

Evidence Is Retained

KRI Definitions Are Reviewed

KRIs Support Risk Decisions
```

The key principle is:

> **A cybersecurity KRI should provide management with an early and measurable indication of changing risk exposure, allowing the organization to identify when risk is approaching or exceeding defined tolerance and take appropriate action before the risk results in significant business impact.**

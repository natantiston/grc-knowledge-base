# 18.5 GRC Process and Workflow Diagrams

## Part 1 – GRC Process Lifecycle Diagram

A **GRC Process Lifecycle Diagram** shows how governance, risk management, and compliance activities operate as an interconnected and continuous process rather than as isolated activities.

The fundamental GRC relationship is:

```text
Governance
     ↓
Risk Management
     ↓
Compliance
     ↓
Monitoring
     ↓
Reporting
     ↓
Management Decision
     ↓
Continuous Improvement
     ↺
```

GRC connects organizational objectives with risk and compliance requirements.

```text
Business Objectives
       ↓
Governance
       ↓
Risk Management
       ↓
Controls
       ↓
Compliance
       ↓
Assurance
       ↓
Reporting
       ↓
Management Decisions
```

The purpose of the GRC lifecycle is to help an organization answer three fundamental questions:

```text
Governance:
Are we making the right decisions?

Risk:
Are we managing uncertainty appropriately?

Compliance:
Are we meeting applicable requirements?
```

These three areas should work together.

```text
              GOVERNANCE
                 /   \
                /     \
               ↓       ↓
             RISK ←→ COMPLIANCE
```

A mature GRC program integrates all three.

```text
Governance
     ↓
Defines Direction
     ↓
Risk Management
     ↓
Identifies and Manages Uncertainty
     ↓
Compliance
     ↓
Demonstrates Requirements Are Met
     ↓
Assurance
     ↓
Provides Confidence
```

The complete GRC lifecycle can be represented as:

```text
                  BUSINESS OBJECTIVES
                         ↓
                    GOVERNANCE
                         ↓
                  RISK IDENTIFICATION
                         ↓
                   RISK ASSESSMENT
                         ↓
                 RISK TREATMENT
                         ↓
                    CONTROLS
                         ↓
                 COMPLIANCE ASSESSMENT
                         ↓
                      MONITORING
                         ↓
                     REPORTING
                         ↓
                  MANAGEMENT DECISION
                         ↓
                  CONTINUOUS IMPROVEMENT
                         ↺
```

Governance establishes the organization's direction.

```text
Board / Executive Management
          ↓
Strategy
          ↓
Policies
          ↓
Risk Appetite
          ↓
Accountability
```

This creates the foundation for risk management.

```text
Governance
     ↓
Risk Appetite
     ↓
Risk Management
```

Risk management then identifies uncertainty that could affect organizational objectives.

```text
Business Objective
       ↓
Potential Threat / Opportunity
       ↓
Risk Identification
       ↓
Risk Assessment
       ↓
Risk Treatment
```

The organization then implements controls to manage identified risks.

```text
Risk
 ↓
Risk Treatment
 ↓
Control Objective
 ↓
Control
 ↓
Control Operation
```

Compliance provides another layer.

```text
External Requirements
        ↓
Internal Policies
        ↓
Controls
        ↓
Compliance Assessment
        ↓
Compliance Status
```

These processes should not operate independently.

For example:

```text
New Regulation
      ↓
Compliance Requirement
      ↓
Risk Assessment
      ↓
Control Requirement
      ↓
Control Implementation
      ↓
Compliance Testing
      ↓
Management Reporting
```

This demonstrates how a regulatory change can affect the entire GRC lifecycle.

A GRC process can also begin with a business change.

```text
Business Change
      ↓
Risk Assessment
      ↓
Control Review
      ↓
Compliance Review
      ↓
Management Approval
      ↓
Implementation
      ↓
Monitoring
```

Examples include:

```text
New Technology
New Product
New Supplier
Cloud Migration
Acquisition
New Regulation
New Geographic Market
Organizational Restructuring
```

Each may trigger multiple GRC activities.

The GRC lifecycle should therefore include **change management**.

```text
Change
  ↓
Impact Assessment
  ↓
Risk Assessment
  ↓
Compliance Assessment
  ↓
Control Assessment
  ↓
Approval
  ↓
Implementation
  ↓
Post-Implementation Review
```

Another important component is **policy management**.

```text

       ↓
Policy Requirement
       ↓
Policy Development
       ↓
Policy Approval
       ↓
Policy Communication
       ↓
Policy Implementation
       ↓
Policy Review
```

Policies should be connected to controls.

```text
Policy
  ↓
Control Objective
  ↓
Control
  ↓
Control Activity
```

And controls should be connected to risks.

```text
Risk
 ↓
Control
 ↓
Control Effectiveness
 ↓
Residual Risk
```

This creates the broader relationship:

```text
Requirement
     ↓
Policy
     ↓
Risk
     ↓
Control
     ↓
Evidence
     ↓
Assessment
     ↓
Reporting
```

The GRC lifecycle also requires **accountability**.

A RACI structure can support this.

```text
Governance
     ↓
Accountability
     ↓
RACI
     ↓
Responsible
Accountable
Consulted
Informed
```

For example:

```text
Risk Assessment

Risk Owner       → Accountable
Risk Analyst     → Responsible
Security         → Consulted
Management       → Informed
```

Clear accountability prevents GRC activities from becoming "everyone's responsibility and nobody's responsibility."

The lifecycle also includes **evidence management**.

```text
Control
   ↓
Control Activity
   ↓
Evidence
   ↓
Evidence Validation
   ↓
Assessment
```

Evidence should be traceable.

```text
Evidence
   ↓
Control
   ↓
Requirement
   ↓
Assessment
```

This creates auditability.

The GRC lifecycle should also include **issue and remediation management**.

```text
Assessment
    ↓
Finding
    ↓
Risk
    ↓
Remediation
    ↓
Action Owner
    ↓
Due Date
    ↓
Validation
    ↓
Closure
```

An issue should not simply disappear after remediation is marked complete.

```text
Remediation Complete
        ↓
Validation
        ↓
Effective?
     ↙      ↘
   Yes       No
    ↓         ↓
  Close     Reopen
```

Monitoring provides continuous visibility.

```text
Controls
   ↓
Metrics
   ↓
KPIs / KRIs
   ↓
GRC Dashboard
   ↓
Management Reporting
```

For example:

```text

      ↓
95% Effective

Open High Risks
      ↓
8

Overdue Actions
      ↓
4

Compliance
      ↓
91%
```

These metrics can then be escalated according to thresholds.

```text
Metric
  ↓
Threshold
  ↓
Threshold Breached?
 ↙              ↘
No               Yes
 ↓                 ↓
Monitor          Escalate
```

This is where GRC becomes an active management system rather than simply a documentation function.

The lifecycle also includes **risk appetite**.

```text
Risk Assessment
      ↓
Risk Rating
      ↓
Risk Appetite
      ↓
Management Decision
```

For example:

```text
Risk:
High

Risk Appetite:
Medium

        ↓

Outside Appetite
        ↓
Treatment Required
```

If the risk is within appetite:

```text
Risk:
Medium

Risk Appetite:
Medium

        ↓

Management May Accept
        ↓
Monitor
```

Risk appetite therefore influences treatment and escalation.

The relationship can be shown as:

```text
Risk
 ↓
Risk Rating
 ↓
Risk Appetite
 ↓
Treatment / Acceptance
 ↓
Residual Risk
 ↓
Monitoring
```

Compliance requirements can also affect risk appetite decisions.

```text
Regulatory Requirement
       ↓
Mandatory Obligation
       ↓
Risk Treatment
       ↓
Control
```

In some cases, the organization cannot simply accept the risk because the underlying requirement is mandatory.

For example:

```text
Legal Requirement
       ↓
Mandatory Control
       ↓
Non-Compliance
       ↓
Remediation Required
```

This is an important distinction between ordinary business risk and regulatory obligations.

The GRC lifecycle also connects to **internal audit**.

```text
GRC Processes
      ↓
Control Environment
      ↓
Internal Audit
      ↓
Independent Assurance
      ↓
Audit Findings
      ↓
Remediation
```

External audits and regulatory examinations may follow a similar pathway.

```text
Control Environment
      ↓
External Assessment
      ↓
Findings
      ↓
Management Response
      ↓
Remediation
      ↓
Validation
```

The overall assurance model can therefore be represented as:

```text
First Line
Operations
     ↓
Second Line
GRC / Risk / Compliance
     ↓
Third Line
Internal Audit
     ↓
External Assurance
     ↓
Board / Governing Body
```

The GRC lifecycle should also incorporate **security incidents**.

```text
Security Incident
       ↓
Incident Management
       ↓
Root Cause Analysis
       ↓
Control Weakness
       ↓
Risk Reassessment
       ↓
Remediation
       ↓
Control Improvement
```

For example:

```text
Data Breach
    ↓
Investigation
    ↓
Root Cause
    ↓
Weak Access Control
    ↓
Risk Increased
    ↓
Additional Controls
    ↓
Retesting
```

This allows lessons learned from incidents to improve the GRC environment.

The same applies to audit findings.

```text
Audit Finding
      ↓
Control Weakness
      ↓
Risk
      ↓
Remediation
      ↓
Control Improvement
```

And to changes in threat intelligence.

```text
New Threat
    ↓
Threat Assessment
    ↓
Risk Assessment
    ↓
Control Review
    ↓
Additional Treatment
```

This creates a dynamic GRC environment.

A mature GRC lifecycle therefore looks like:

```text
                    BUSINESS OBJECTIVES
                           ↓
                       GOVERNANCE
                           ↓
                      RISK APPETITE
                           ↓
                     RISK MANAGEMENT
                           ↓
                       CONTROLS
                           ↓
                      COMPLIANCE
                           ↓
                     MONITORING
                           ↓
                      ASSURANCE
                           ↓
                       REPORTING
                           ↓
                  MANAGEMENT DECISIONS
                           ↓
                  CONTINUOUS IMPROVEMENT
                           ↓
                        REASSESS
                           ↺
```

The lifecycle is supported by several interconnected GRC processes:

```text
              ┌──────────────────────┐
              │      GOVERNANCE      │
              └──────────┬───────────┘
                         ↓
              ┌──────────────────────┐
              │   RISK MANAGEMENT    │
              └──────────┬───────────┘
                         ↓
              ┌──────────────────────┐
              │   CONTROL MANAGEMENT │
              └──────────┬───────────┘
                         ↓
              ┌──────────────────────┐
              │     COMPLIANCE       │
              └──────────┬───────────┘
                         ↓
              ┌──────────────────────┐
              │      ASSURANCE       │
              └──────────┬───────────┘
                         ↓
              ┌──────────────────────┐
              │      REPORTING       │
              └──────────┬───────────┘

              ┌──────────────────────┐

              └──────────┬───────────┘
                         ↓
                 CONTINUOUS
                 IMPROVEMENT
                         │
                         └──────────────→ REASSESS
```

The most important concept is that GRC is **cyclical rather than linear**.

```text
Assess
  ↓
Decide
  ↓
Implement
  ↓
Monitor
  ↓
Report
  ↓
Improve
  ↓
Reassess
  ↺
```

The cycle continues because the organization, technology, threats, regulations, and business objectives continuously change.

A mature GRC professional should therefore understand the complete connection:

```text
Business Strategy
      ↓
Governance
      ↓
Risk Appetite
      ↓
Risk
      ↓
Controls
      ↓
Compliance
      ↓
Evidence
      ↓
Assessment
      ↓
Findings
      ↓
Remediation
      ↓
Assurance
      ↓

      ↓
Management Decision
      ↓
Continuous Improvement
      ↺
```

The key principle is:

> **GRC is an integrated management cycle that connects business objectives, governance, risk, controls, compliance, assurance, reporting, and decision-making into a continuous process of managing and improving organizational performance and resilience.**

# 18.5 GRC Process and Workflow Diagrams

A **GRC Workflow Diagram** illustrates how a specific governance, risk, or compliance activity moves from initiation through review, approval, execution, monitoring, and closure.

A workflow converts a GRC process into a sequence of activities, decisions, responsibilities, and outcomes.

The basic workflow structure is:

```text
Trigger
   ↓
Request / Activity
   ↓
Assessment
   ↓
Decision
   ↓
Approval
   ↓
Execution
   ↓
Validation
   ↓
Closure
```

A GRC workflow can be triggered by many events:

```text
New Regulation
New Risk
Audit Finding
Security Incident
New Vendor
Policy Review
Control Failure
Business Change
Risk Assessment
Compliance Assessment
```

For example:

```text
New Regulation
      ↓
Regulatory Impact Assessment
      ↓
Applicable?
   ↙       ↘
 Yes        No
  ↓          ↓
Control    Document
Assessment  Rationale
```

The workflow should clearly identify the **owner of each activity**.

For example:

```text
Business
   ↓
GRC
   ↓
Risk Owner
   ↓
Control Owner
   ↓
Management
   ↓
Internal Audit
```

A RACI model can be incorporated into the workflow.

```text
Activity
   ↓
Responsible
   ↓
Accountable
   ↓
Consulted
   ↓
Informed
```

For example:

```text
Risk Assessment

Risk Analyst
    ↓
Responsible

Risk Owner
    ↓
Accountable

Security / GRC
    ↓
Consulted

Management
    ↓
Informed
```

A workflow should also contain **decision points**.

```text
Assessment
    ↓
Risk Acceptable?
   ↙          ↘
 Yes           No
  ↓             ↓
Accept       Treat
```

This is one of the most important elements of a workflow diagram because decisions determine which path the process follows.

A risk acceptance workflow may look like:

```text
Risk Identified
      ↓
Risk Assessment
      ↓
Risk Rating
      ↓
Within Risk Appetite?
    ↙              ↘
  Yes               No
   ↓                 ↓
Accept          Treatment Required
   ↓                 ↓
Monitor       Treatment Plan
                     ↓
                Residual Risk
                     ↓
              Within Appetite?
                 ↙       ↘
               Yes        No
                ↓          ↓
             Accept     Escalate
```

Approval workflows are particularly important in GRC.

```text
Request
   ↓
Initial Review
   ↓
Risk Assessment
   ↓
Business Approval
   ↓
GRC Approval
   ↓
Management Approval
   ↓
Implementation
```

The exact approval chain should depend on the organization's governance structure and risk level.

A high-risk request may require additional approval.

```text
Low Risk
   ↓
Manager Approval
   ↓
Implementation

High Risk
   ↓
Manager
   ↓
GRC
   ↓
Risk Owner
   ↓
Executive Approval
   ↓
Implementation
```

Workflows should therefore support **risk-based routing**.

```text
Request
   ↓
Risk Assessment
   ↓
Risk Level
 ┌──────┼──────┐
 ↓      ↓      ↓
Low   Medium   High
 ↓      ↓       ↓
Basic  GRC    Executive
Review Review  Review
```

This prevents every request from going through an unnecessarily complicated process.

A **policy approval workflow** can be represented as:

```text
Policy Draft
     ↓
Policy Owner Review
     ↓
GRC Review
     ↓
Legal / Compliance Review
     ↓
Management Approval
     ↓
Publication
     ↓
Communication
     ↓
Implementation
     ↓
Periodic Review
```

A policy may be rejected during review.

```text
Draft
  ↓
Review
  ↓
Approved?
 ↙       ↘
No        Yes
↓          ↓
Revise    Publish
↓
Review Again
```

This creates a feedback loop.

```text
Draft
 ↓
Review
 ↓
Revisions
 ↓
Review
 ↺
```

A **risk assessment workflow** can be represented as:

```text
Risk Identification
       ↓
Risk Description
       ↓
Asset / Process Identification
       ↓
Threat Identification
       ↓

       ↓

       ↓

       ↓

       ↓
Existing Controls
       ↓
Control Effectiveness
       ↓

       ↓

```

The treatment decision can follow several paths:

```text
Residual Risk
      ↓
Treatment Decision
 ┌────┼─────┬──────┐
 ↓    ↓     ↓      ↓
Avoid Reduce Transfer Accept
```

Each treatment option produces a different workflow.

```text
Avoid
  ↓
Stop / Change Activity
```

```text
Reduce
  ↓
Implement Controls
```

```text
Transfer
  ↓
Insurance / Contract / Third Party
```

```text
Accept
  ↓
Formal Approval
  ↓
Monitor
```

A **control assessment workflow** may look like:

```text

      ↓

      ↓

      ↓

   ↙        ↘
 Yes         No
  ↓           ↓

              ↓
          Remediation
              ↓
```

```text
   ↓          ↓
```

```text
      ↓

  ↓
```

```text
 ↓      ↓       ↓
```


For example:

```text

   ↓

   ↓
```

```text

   ↓

   ↓
```

A workflow can also contain **automated notifications**.

```text
Workflow Trigger
      ↓
System Notification
      ↓
Assigned User
      ↓
Action
      ↓
System Records Result
      ↓

```

For example:

```text

      ↓
Automatic Email
      ↓

      ↓
Evidence Uploaded
      ↓

      ↓

```

This is where GRC platforms can reduce manual administrative work.

A mature workflow may therefore combine:

```text
People
   +
Processes
   +
Technology
   +
Data
   +
Approvals
   +
Evidence
```

The workflow should maintain an **audit trail**.

```text
Workflow Action
      ↓
User
      ↓
Date / Time
      ↓
Action Taken
      ↓
Decision
      ↓
Evidence
```

For example:

```text

Risk Owner

Date:
15 Aug 2026

Medium

Rationale:

```

This information can be important during audits and investigations.

Workflow automation can also enforce segregation of duties.

```text
Requestor
    ↓
Reviewer
    ↓
Approver
    ↓
Implementer
```

The same person should not necessarily perform every step.

For example:

```text

      ↓
Provides Evidence

GRC Reviewer
      ↓
Assesses Evidence

Management
      ↓
Approves Risk Acceptance

Internal Audit
      ↓
Provides Independent Assurance
```

This improves governance and reduces conflicts of interest.

A **workflow exception** should also have a defined path.

```text
Normal Workflow
      ↓
Exception
      ↓
Exception Assessment
      ↓
Alternative Path
      ↓
Approval
      ↓
Return to Normal Workflow
```

For example:

```text

      ↓
Evidence Not Available
      ↓
Exception Raised
      ↓
Reason Documented
      ↓
Temporary Extension
      ↓
New Due Date
      ↓

```

The workflow should prevent exceptions from becoming permanent process bypasses.

Another important concept is **workflow status visibility**.

```text
Workflow Dashboard
       ↓

In Progress
Pending Approval
Pending Evidence
Overdue
Completed
Rejected

```

This allows GRC teams to identify bottlenecks.

For example:

```text

      ↓

```

The GRC team can then focus on the overdue or blocked activities.

Workflow performance can also be measured.

```text
Average Completion Time
Approval Time
Remediation Time
Evidence Submission Time
Overdue Percentage

```

These metrics can feed into GRC dashboards.

```text
Workflow Data
      ↓
Process Metrics
      ↓
GRC Dashboard
      ↓
Management Reporting
```

A complete GRC workflow can therefore be represented as:

```text
                 TRIGGER
                    ↓
              REQUEST / ISSUE
                    ↓
                CLASSIFY
                    ↓
                ASSESS RISK
                    ↓
                ASSIGN OWNER
                    ↓
               PERFORM ACTION
                    ↓
                 REVIEW
                    ↓
              DECISION POINT
               ↙        ↘
            APPROVE     REJECT
               ↓          ↓
          IMPLEMENT     CLOSE
               ↓
             EVIDENCE
               ↓
            VALIDATION
               ↓
             EFFECTIVE?
             ↙       ↘
           Yes        No
            ↓          ↓
          CLOSE      REMEDIATE
                       ↓
                      RETEST
                       ↺
```

The key principle is:

> **A GRC workflow should clearly define what triggers the process, who performs each activity, what decisions must be made, what evidence is required, who approves the outcome, and how exceptions, escalation, validation, and closure are handled.**

```text
  ↺
```

This workflow-based approach is fundamental to **GRC process automation, ServiceNow GRC, Archer, compliance management, risk management, audit management, and enterprise governance operations**.

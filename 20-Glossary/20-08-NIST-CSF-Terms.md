# 20.8 NIST CSF Terms

This section defines the core terminology associated with the **NIST Cybersecurity Framework (NIST CSF)**, with emphasis on **NIST CSF 2.0**. The glossary covers the CSF Core, Functions, Categories, Subcategories, Organizational Profiles, Tiers, Current and Target Profiles, Cybersecurity Risk, and implementation concepts.

> **Terminology note:** NIST CSF 2.0 was released in February 2024 and reorganized the framework around **six Functions: Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, and Recover**. CSF 2.0 is intended for organizations of all types and does not prescribe a single implementation method or technology.

# NIST CSF 2.0 Six Functions

A critical part of the NIST CSF 2.0 vocabulary is the six-function structure:

| Function     | Primary Purpose                                                                                                               |
| ------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **GOVERN**   | Establish, communicate, and monitor cybersecurity risk management strategy, expectations, and policy.                         |
| **IDENTIFY** | Understand cybersecurity risks affecting the organization's assets, people, processes, technology, suppliers, and objectives. |
| **PROTECT**  | Implement safeguards to manage cybersecurity risks.                                                                           |
| **DETECT**   | Find and analyze possible cybersecurity attacks and compromises.                                                              |
| **RESPOND**  | Take action regarding detected cybersecurity incidents.                                                                       |
| **RECOVER**  | Restore affected assets and operations and communicate recovery activities.                                                   |

The important change in **CSF 2.0** is the addition of **GOVERN** as a dedicated Function.

The structure can therefore be remembered as:

> **GOVERN → IDENTIFY → PROTECT → DETECT → RESPOND → RECOVER**

# NIST CSF 2.0 Categories

The six Functions are divided into **Categories**.

### GOVERN

* **GV.OC — Organizational Context**
* **GV.RM — Risk Management Strategy**
* **GV.RR — Roles, Responsibilities, and Authorities**
* **GV.PO — Policy**
* **GV.OV — Oversight**
* **GV.SC — Cybersecurity Supply Chain Risk Management**

### IDENTIFY

* **ID.AM — Asset Management**
* **ID.RA — Risk Assessment**
* **ID.IM — Improvement**

### PROTECT

* **PR.AA — Identity Management, Authentication, and Access Control**
* **PR.AT — Awareness and Training**
* **PR.DS — Data Security**
* **PR.PS — Platform Security**
* **PR.IR — Technology Infrastructure Resilience**

### DETECT

* **DE.CM — Continuous Monitoring**
* **DE.AE — Adverse Event Analysis**

### RESPOND

* **RS.MA — Incident Management**
* **RS.AN — Incident Analysis**
* **RS.CO — Incident Response Reporting and Communication**
* **RS.MI — Incident Mitigation**

### RECOVER

* **RC.RP — Incident Recovery Plan Execution**
* **RC.CO — Incident Recovery Communication**

# NIST CSF Core Hierarchy

The NIST CSF Core can be understood hierarchically:

**Function**
↓
**Category**
↓
**Subcategory**
↓
**Outcome**

For example:

**PROTECT**
→ **PR.AA Identity Management, Authentication, and Access Control**
→ Specific cybersecurity outcomes

This hierarchy makes the CSF useful for GRC because organizations can map high-level business and governance requirements down to specific cybersecurity outcomes and controls.

# Current Profile vs. Target Profile

One of the most useful NIST CSF concepts for GRC practitioners is the **Profile**.

### Current Profile

Describes:

> **Where are we now?**

It represents the cybersecurity outcomes currently achieved by the organization.

### Target Profile

Describes:

> **Where do we want to be?**

It represents the cybersecurity outcomes the organization wants to achieve.

### Gap

The difference becomes:

> **Target Profile − Current Profile = Cybersecurity Improvement Gap**

The resulting gap can be converted into:

* remediation initiatives;
* security projects;
* control improvements;
* policy changes;
* technology investments;
* training requirements;
* risk-treatment actions; and
* GRC roadmap priorities.

# NIST CSF Tiers

NIST CSF 2.0 uses four Tiers to characterize the rigor of an organization's cybersecurity risk governance and management practices.

| Tier                       | Description                                                                                                                                                     |
| -------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Tier 1 — Partial**       | Cybersecurity risk management practices are limited, ad hoc, or reactive. Organizational awareness may be limited.                                              |
| **Tier 2 — Risk Informed** | Risk management practices are informed by organizational objectives and risk awareness, but may not be organization-wide or consistently implemented.           |
| **Tier 3 — Repeatable**    | Cybersecurity risk management practices are formally approved, consistently implemented, regularly updated, and integrated across the organization.             |
| **Tier 4 — Adaptive**      | Cybersecurity practices are continuously adapted based on lessons learned, predictive indicators, advanced technologies, and changes in the threat environment. |

**Important:** CSF Tiers are **not simply maturity levels that every organization must progress through sequentially**. They describe the characteristics of an organization's cybersecurity risk governance and management practices.

# NIST CSF vs. ISO/IEC 27001

For a GRC professional, this distinction is especially important.

| NIST CSF                                     | ISO/IEC 27001                                                   |
| -------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Cybersecurity framework                      | Management-system standard                                      |
| Outcome-oriented                             | Requirement-oriented                                            |
| Flexible implementation                      | Formal ISMS requirements                                        |
| Six Functions                                | Clauses 4–10 + Annex A reference controls                       |
| Profiles                                     | ISMS scope and organizational objectives                        |
| Tiers                                        | Characterization of risk-management rigor                       |
| Categories/Subcategories                     | Requirements and controls are structured differently            |
| No certification requirement inherent to CSF | Certification available through accredited certification bodies |
| Strong cybersecurity risk communication      | Strong management-system governance                             |
| Highly adaptable                             | Formal conformity requirements                                  |

A useful way to remember the relationship is:

> **NIST CSF = What cybersecurity outcomes should we achieve?**

> **ISO/IEC 27001 = How do we establish and manage a formal information security management system to achieve and continually improve those outcomes?**

They can therefore be used together rather than treated as competing frameworks.

# NIST CSF in a GRC Operating Model

A practical GRC implementation can connect the CSF as follows:

**Business Objectives**
↓
**Cybersecurity Governance**
↓
**Cybersecurity Risk Assessment**
↓
**Current Profile**
↓
**Target Profile**
↓
**Gap Analysis**
↓
**Risk-Based Prioritization**
↓
**Control / Capability Improvements**
↓
**Metrics & KRIs**
↓
**Continuous Monitoring**
↓
**Management Reporting**
↓
**Continual Improvement**

This makes NIST CSF particularly valuable for **CISO dashboards, cybersecurity maturity assessments, risk reporting, control mapping, security roadmaps, and executive communication**.

### Key GRC takeaway

The most important NIST CSF 2.0 concepts to remember are:

**GOVERN → IDENTIFY → PROTECT → DETECT → RESPOND → RECOVER**

combined with:

**Current Profile → Target Profile → Gap → Risk-Based Improvement**

This provides a practical bridge between **enterprise governance, cybersecurity risk, controls, compliance, operational security, and executive decision-making**.

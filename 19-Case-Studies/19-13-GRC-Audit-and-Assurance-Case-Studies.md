# 19.13 GRC Audit and Assurance Case Studies

## Part 1 – Planning a GRC Internal Audit

A GRC internal audit provides independent and systematic assurance that governance, risk-management, and control processes are:

* Properly designed
* Implemented
* Operating effectively
* Aligned with organizational objectives
* Compliant with applicable requirements
* Producing reliable evidence

The purpose of an internal GRC audit is not simply to find mistakes.

Its purpose is to answer:

> **"Can management reasonably rely on the organization's governance, risk, and control environment?"**

The audit lifecycle is:

**Audit Universe → Risk Assessment → Audit Planning → Scope → Objectives → Criteria → Resources → Audit Program → Evidence Plan → Fieldwork → Findings → Reporting → Follow-up**

---

# Case Study: GlobalConnect Telecom

GlobalConnect operates telecommunications, cloud, digital, and customer-facing services across several countries.

Its GRC environment includes:

* ISO 27001
* NIST CSF
* Enterprise Risk Management
* Third-Party Risk Management
* Privacy
* Business Continuity
* Regulatory compliance
* Cybersecurity governance
* Internal controls

The Internal Audit function has selected:

> **Cybersecurity Governance and Risk Management**

as a high-priority audit area for 2026.

You are the **GRC Audit Manager** responsible for planning the audit.

---

# 1. Why Audit Planning Matters

A poorly planned audit can result in:

* Excessive scope
* Insufficient evidence
* Inadequate resources
* Missed high-risk areas
* Weak findings
* Delayed reporting
* Management disagreement

A well-planned audit ensures that audit effort is concentrated where it creates the greatest assurance value.

---

# 2. Audit vs Assessment vs Compliance Review

These activities are related but different.

### Internal Audit

Provides independent assurance.

### Risk Assessment

Identifies and evaluates risks.

### Compliance Assessment

Determines whether requirements are being met.

### Control Assessment

Evaluates whether controls are designed and operating effectively.

### Consulting

Provides advice or recommendations.

The auditor must preserve sufficient independence when performing assurance work.

# 5. Step 3 – Determine Audit Priority

* Implemented a new cybersecurity operating model.

* Expanded its third-party ecosystem.

> **Cybersecurity Governance and Risk Management**

# 6. Step 4 – Define the Audit Objective

A good audit objective should be clear and testable.

Example:

> **To determine whether GlobalConnect has established and maintained an effective cybersecurity governance and risk-management framework that appropriately identifies, assesses, treats, monitors, and reports cybersecurity risk.**

This is better than:

> "Audit cybersecurity."

The objective defines what assurance the audit is expected to provide.

The audit may exclude:

* Detailed penetration testing

# 20. Step 11 – Evaluate Control Design

The first question is:

> **Is the control appropriately designed?**

Example:

Policy requires:

> "Cybersecurity risks must be reviewed quarterly."

But the process only reviews risks annually.

The control design may be inadequate.

---

# 21. Design Effectiveness

A control is design-effective when it is capable of addressing the relevant risk.

Example:

**Risk:**

Critical cybersecurity risks remain unidentified.

**Control:**

Quarterly enterprise risk assessment.

Potentially:

**Design Effective = Yes**

But this does not mean the control actually operates.

---

# 22. Step 12 – Test Operating Effectiveness

Now ask:

> **Did the control actually operate as designed?**

Suppose the policy requires:

**Quarterly risk review**

Audit examines four quarters.

Evidence:

* Q1: Completed
* Q2: Completed
* Q3: Not completed
* Q4: Completed

The control did not operate consistently.

---

# 23. Design vs Operating Effectiveness

This distinction is essential.

### Design Effectiveness

> Would the control work if properly performed?

### Operating Effectiveness

> Did the control actually operate as intended?

A control may be:

**Well designed but poorly operated.**

or:

**Poorly designed but consistently performed.**

Both situations matter.

---

> "Management confirms that the review occurs."

> Approved quarterly risk review records showing date, participants, risks reviewed, decisions, and follow-up actions.

# 38. Step 24 – Validate Findings

Before issuing a finding, auditors discuss it with the control owner.

Questions include:

* Is the observation accurate?
* Is there additional evidence?
* Is there a legitimate exception?
* Is the control currently being redesigned?
* Is the issue already being remediated?

This prevents factual errors.

---

# 39. Step 25 – Root Cause Analysis

A strong audit finding identifies more than the symptom.

Example:

### Observation

Risk acceptance exceeded approval authority.

### Root Cause

The GRC workflow does not enforce approval thresholds.

### Impact

High risks may be accepted without appropriate executive oversight.

### Recommendation

Implement automated approval routing based on risk rating.

This is much stronger than:

> "Managers need more training."

---

### Medium

Severity should consider:

* Impact
* Likelihood
* Regulatory implications
* Control failure
* Exposure
* Existing compensating controls

# 44. Auditor Independence

Internal Audit should maintain sufficient independence.

For example, if an auditor previously designed GlobalConnect's cybersecurity risk-management framework, assigning that same person to provide independent assurance over the framework may create a self-review concern.

The principle is:

> **You should not independently assure controls you personally designed or operated without appropriate safeguards.**

# 50. Key GRC Lesson

The central lesson is:

> **A strong internal audit begins before the auditor tests a single control.**

Good planning establishes:

* Why the audit matters
* What risks are being examined
* What controls should address those risks
* What evidence will demonstrate effectiveness
* What level of assurance management requires

The most important principle is:

> **Audit the organization's most important risks—not simply the easiest controls to test.**

A mature GRC auditor therefore starts with:

**Risk**

and works toward:

**Control → Evidence → Testing → Finding → Assurance.**

That is the foundation for an effective **GRC internal audit program**.

# 19.13 GRC Audit and Assurance Case Studies

## Part 2 – Testing Controls and Evaluating Evidence

After an internal audit has been properly planned, the next stage is **fieldwork**—testing whether controls are appropriately designed, implemented, and operating effectively.

The auditor's fundamental question is:

> **Can sufficient, reliable, and relevant evidence demonstrate that the control actually reduces the intended risk?**

The core process is:

**Risk → Control Objective → Control → Population → Sample → Evidence → Test → Exception → Evaluation → Conclusion**

---

# Case Study: GlobalConnect Telecom

GlobalConnect's Internal Audit team is conducting the cybersecurity GRC audit from Part 1.

The audit covers:

* Cybersecurity governance
* Cybersecurity risk management
* Risk acceptance
* Risk treatment
* GRC reporting
* Control ownership
* Management oversight

The audit team now begins detailed control testing.

---

# 1. What Is a Control?

A control is an action, process, mechanism, or activity designed to:

* Prevent a risk
* Detect a problem
* Correct an issue
* Reduce the likelihood or impact of an event

Examples include:

* Quarterly risk reviews
* Access reviews
* Approval workflows
* Security monitoring
* Vulnerability management
* Risk acceptance approvals
* Management reporting

---

# 2. Control Types

Controls can be classified in several ways.

### Preventive Controls

Designed to prevent an event.

Example:

> GRC workflow prevents a user from accepting a critical risk without executive approval.

### Detective Controls

Identify an event after it occurs.

Example:

> Monthly report identifies overdue risk treatments.

### Corrective Controls

Correct an identified problem.

Example:

> Overdue risk automatically escalates to management.

---

# 3. Manual vs Automated Controls

### Manual

A person performs the control.

Example:

> GRC Manager reviews the risk register monthly.

### Automated

Technology performs the control.

Example:

> GRC platform automatically blocks unauthorized risk acceptance.

### Hybrid

Technology generates information and a person makes a decision.

Example:

> GRC platform identifies overdue risks and the Risk Manager reviews and escalates them.

---

# 4. Key Audit Question

For every control, the auditor should determine:

> **What risk is this control supposed to address?**

A control without a clear risk relationship may indicate weak control design.

Example:

**Risk:**

Critical cybersecurity risks remain unidentified.

**Control:**

Quarterly enterprise cybersecurity risk assessment.

The relationship is clear.

---

# 5. Control Objective

The auditor defines what the control should accomplish.

Example:

### Risk

Unauthorized acceptance of critical cybersecurity risk.

### Control Objective

Ensure that critical risks are accepted only by appropriately authorized executives.

### Control

GRC workflow requires CIO approval for critical risk acceptance.

This provides a basis for testing.

---

# 6. Step 1 – Understand the Control

Before testing, the auditor should understand:

* Who performs it?
* What is performed?
* When is it performed?
* How is it performed?
* What system is used?
* What evidence is generated?
* Who reviews the result?
* What happens when an exception occurs?

This is often accomplished through:

**Interview + Walkthrough + Documentation Review**

---

# 7. Step 2 – Perform a Walkthrough

The auditor selects one transaction or risk and follows it from beginning to end.

Example:

**Risk Identified**

↓

**Risk Assessment**

↓

**Risk Recorded**

↓

**Risk Owner Assigned**

↓

**Treatment Selected**

↓

**Approval**

↓

**Monitoring**

↓

**Reporting**

The walkthrough helps determine whether the documented process matches reality.

---

# 8. Documented Process vs Actual Process

Suppose the policy says:

> "All high risks require executive approval."

During the walkthrough, the auditor discovers that managers routinely approve high risks through email.

This creates a potential control gap.

The documented process says:

**GRC workflow**

The actual process is:

**Email approval**

This difference must be investigated.

---

# 9. Step 3 – Define the Population

Before selecting a sample, the auditor needs to understand the population.

Example:

GlobalConnect has:

**1,250 cybersecurity risks**

The population for testing may be:

> All cybersecurity risks recorded during 2026.

But depending on the control, a narrower population may be appropriate.

For example:

> All high and critical risks accepted during 2026.

---

# 10. Population Integrity

The auditor should determine whether the population is:

* Complete
* Accurate
* Current
* Relevant

Suppose management provides:

**100 accepted high risks**

But the GRC database contains:

**127**

The auditor must reconcile the difference before sampling.

Otherwise:

> The audit sample may be based on an incomplete population.

---

# 11. Step 4 – Select the Sample

Sampling allows auditors to draw conclusions without testing every item.

Possible approaches include:

### Random Sampling

Items selected randomly.

### Systematic Sampling

Every nth item is selected.

### Judgmental Sampling

Auditor selects items based on professional judgment.

### Risk-Based Sampling

Higher-risk items receive greater attention.

---

# 12. Example Risk-Based Sample

Population:

**1,250 risks**

The auditor selects:

* 10 critical risks
* 15 high risks
* 10 medium risks
* 5 low risks

Total:

**40 samples**

The sample is deliberately weighted toward higher-risk items.

---

# 13. Why Sampling Requires Judgment

Sampling is not simply:

> "Pick 40 records."

The auditor should consider:

* Population size
* Risk
* Control frequency
* Control consistency
* Historical failures
* Expected error rate
* Required assurance level

The audit methodology should define the organization's preferred approach.

---

# 14. Step 5 – Define the Test Procedure

A test procedure should be specific.

Weak:

> "Review risk management."

Strong:

> "Select a sample of high and critical cybersecurity risks and verify that each has a documented risk owner, approved risk score, treatment plan, residual risk rating, and evidence of management approval."

The second procedure can be objectively executed.

---

# 15. Example Control Test

### Control

High-risk acceptance requires executive approval.

### Population

All high-risk acceptances during 2026.

### Sample

20 accepted high risks.

### Test

Verify:

1. Risk classified as high.
2. Formal risk acceptance exists.
3. Authorized executive approved it.
4. Approval occurred before acceptance.
5. Acceptance has an expiration/review date.

---

# 16. Step 6 – Collect Evidence

Evidence may include:

* GRC records
* System reports
* Approval logs
* Meeting minutes
* Emails
* Tickets
* Policies
* Risk registers
* Dashboards
* Configuration reports

The auditor must evaluate evidence quality—not simply collect large quantities of documents.

---

# 17. Evidence Hierarchy

Generally, stronger evidence includes:

### Strong

System-generated records with reliable controls.

### Strong

Independent third-party evidence.

### Moderate

Approved organizational records.

### Moderate

Management reports.

### Weak

Unaudited spreadsheets.

### Weak

Verbal statements.

### Very Weak

Unsupported management assertions.

The hierarchy can vary depending on the specific audit test.

---

# 18. Evidence Characteristics

Good audit evidence should generally be:

### Sufficient

Enough evidence to support the conclusion.

### Appropriate

Relevant and reliable.

### Authentic

Actually originating from the claimed source.

### Complete

Not selectively presented.

### Timely

Relevant to the period under audit.

---

# 19. Evidence Example

Management provides:

> "All high risks have been reviewed."

This is an assertion.

The auditor requests:

* Risk-review meeting minutes
* GRC workflow records
* Risk register
* Approval records

The assertion is then tested against evidence.

---

# 20. Step 7 – Evidence Authenticity

The auditor should consider:

> **Could this evidence have been altered?**

For example, a spreadsheet may be manually edited.

A system-generated audit log may provide stronger evidence.

However, even system-generated evidence requires consideration of:

* Access controls
* System reliability
* Report logic
* Completeness
* Time period

---

# 21. Step 8 – Test One Control

Suppose the control requires:

> Quarterly cybersecurity risk review.

The auditor obtains:

| Quarter | Evidence        |
| ------- | --------------- |
| Q1      | Meeting minutes |
| Q2      | Meeting minutes |
| Q3      | No evidence     |
| Q4      | Meeting minutes |

Result:

**3 of 4 quarters evidenced**

Control operation:

**75%**

The auditor must determine whether this represents a control failure.

---

# 22. Don't Automatically Treat Every Exception as a Finding

An exception may be:

* Isolated
* Administrative
* Compensated
* Caused by a system migration
* Supported by alternative evidence

The auditor should investigate the cause and significance.

Example:

Q3 meeting was held but the minutes were not retained.

If attendance records and documented decisions exist elsewhere, the evidence gap may be less severe than a completely missed review.

---

# 23. Step 9 – Determine Exception Criteria

Before evaluating results, auditors should know what constitutes failure.

Example:

Control requirement:

> All critical risks must have an assigned owner.

Sample:

**30 critical risks**

Results:

**29 have owners**

**1 does not**

The auditor must determine whether:

* One exception is acceptable under methodology.
* The exception represents a systemic weakness.
* The control failed for that item.

---

# 24. Exception vs Control Failure

This distinction matters.

### Exception

An individual instance where the control requirement was not met.

### Control Failure

The control did not operate effectively.

### Systemic Failure

The process is consistently failing across the organization.

One exception does not automatically prove systemic control failure.

---

# 25. Step 10 – Evaluate Control Design

Suppose the organization requires:

> High-risk acceptance must be approved by an executive.

But the GRC platform allows anyone with "Risk Manager" access to approve high risks.

The control is poorly designed.

Why?

Because the system does not enforce the policy requirement.

---

# 26. Design Effectiveness Example

### Intended Control

Critical risk acceptance requires CIO approval.

### Actual Control

Risk Manager can approve critical risks.

### Conclusion

**Design effectiveness: Ineffective**

Even if the Risk Manager happens to obtain CIO approval informally, the formal control design remains weak.

---

# 27. Step 11 – Evaluate Operating Effectiveness

Suppose the system has correctly implemented:

> CIO approval required.

The auditor tests 25 critical risk acceptances.

Results:

**24**

have CIO approval.

**1**

does not.

The auditor evaluates whether the exception indicates:

* Isolated error
* Process breakdown
* System bypass
* Unauthorized approval

---

# 28. Operating Effectiveness Example

A control may be:

**Design Effective = Yes**

but:

**Operating Effective = No**

if users consistently bypass the required process.

This distinction should appear in the audit conclusion.

---

# 29. Step 12 – Test Frequency

Controls operate at different frequencies.

Examples:

* Real-time
* Daily
* Weekly
* Monthly
* Quarterly
* Annually
* Event-driven

The auditor must test enough occurrences to evaluate whether the control operates consistently.

---

# 30. Example Monthly Control

Control:

> GRC Manager reviews overdue cybersecurity risks monthly.

Population:

**12 months**

Evidence:

* Jan ✓
* Feb ✓
* Mar ✓
* Apr ✓
* May ✓
* Jun ✗
* Jul ✓
* Aug ✓
* Sep ✓
* Oct ✓
* Nov ✓
* Dec ✓

The auditor should investigate the June exception.

---

# 31. Step 13 – Test Evidence Completeness

Suppose management provides:

**30 risk acceptance forms**

The auditor knows the population contains:

**35**

The auditor should ask:

> Where are the remaining five?

This may reveal:

* Missing documentation
* Incomplete population
* Unauthorized acceptance
* Reporting error

---

# 32. Step 14 – Reperformance

Reperformance is one of the strongest audit techniques.

The auditor independently performs the control or calculation.

Example:

The GRC system calculates risk:

**Likelihood × Impact**

Management reports:

**Risk Score = 12**

The auditor independently recalculates:

**4 × 5 = 20**

The result does not match.

This identifies a control or system issue.

---

# 33. Recalculation Example

Suppose 20 sampled risks have:

| Risk | System Score | Auditor Score |
| ---- | -----------: | ------------: |
| R1   |           12 |            20 |
| R2   |           16 |            16 |
| R3   |            9 |            12 |
| R4   |           20 |            20 |
| R5   |           15 |            15 |

The auditor investigates the differences.

Possible causes:

* Incorrect configuration
* Wrong methodology
* Data-entry errors
* Outdated scoring model

---

# 34. Step 15 – Inquiry

Inquiry means asking personnel questions.

Examples:

> Who approves critical risk acceptance?

> How are overdue risks escalated?

> What happens when a risk owner leaves?

Inquiry is useful for understanding processes.

But:

> **Inquiry alone is generally weaker than corroborated evidence.**

---

# 35. Step 16 – Observation

The auditor observes a control being performed.

Example:

The auditor observes the monthly GRC review meeting.

The auditor sees:

* Risk dashboard
* Risk discussions
* Decisions
* Escalations
* Assignments

Observation provides useful evidence but represents only the observed instance.

---

# 36. Step 17 – Inspection

Inspection involves reviewing:

* Documents
* Records
* Configurations
* Logs
* Tickets
* Reports

Example:

The auditor inspects GRC workflow configuration to determine whether approval thresholds are enforced.

---

# 37. Four Main Audit Techniques

A GRC auditor commonly uses:

**Inquiry**

↓

**Observation**

↓

**Inspection**

↓

**Reperformance**

The strongest audit conclusions often combine multiple techniques.

---

# 38. Step 18 – Test a Risk Register Control

Control:

> All critical cybersecurity risks must have an owner.

Population:

**35 critical risks**

Sample:

**35 — full population**

Results:

* 33 assigned
* 2 unassigned

The exception rate:

**2 / 35 = 5.7%**

But percentage alone does not determine severity.

If the two unassigned risks involve critical customer infrastructure, the impact could be significant.

---

# 39. Step 19 – Evaluate Control Dependency

Some controls depend on other controls.

Example:

**Executive dashboard**

depends on:

**GRC risk register**

which depends on:

**Risk assessments**

which depend on:

**Risk identification**

If the underlying data is inaccurate, the dashboard may also be unreliable.

This creates a **control dependency chain**.

---

# 40. Step 20 – Test Automated Controls

Suppose the GRC platform automatically prevents a risk from being accepted without approval.

The auditor should test:

* Configuration
* User roles
* Workflow logic
* Exception paths
* Override capabilities
* System logs

The auditor may also test a controlled transaction to confirm that the system actually blocks unauthorized approval.

---

# 41. Automated Control Example

Expected:

**Critical Risk**

↓

**CIO Approval Required**

↓

**No Approval**

↓

**System Blocks Acceptance**

If the system permits:

**Critical Risk**

↓

**Risk Manager Approval**

↓

**Accepted**

then the automated control is ineffective.

---

# 42. Step 21 – Test IT-Dependent Controls

A GRC control may depend on:

* Identity management
* Database integrity
* Application configuration
* Interfaces
* Report generation

Therefore, auditors may need to consider relevant IT general controls.

For example:

> If a risk dashboard relies on automated data feeds, changes to the feed may affect reporting accuracy.

---

# 43. Step 22 – Evaluate Management Evidence

Management may provide an impressive amount of evidence.

But the auditor should ask:

> Does this evidence actually prove the control operated?

For example:

**100-page cybersecurity policy**

does not prove:

> Quarterly risk reviews occurred.

The correct evidence might be:

* Review records
* Meeting minutes
* GRC workflow
* Risk decisions
* Follow-up actions

---

# 44. Evidence Relevance

Evidence must relate directly to the test.

### Audit Question

Did management review critical risks quarterly?

### Relevant evidence

Quarterly review records.

### Irrelevant evidence

Cybersecurity strategy document.

The document may be valuable context but does not prove the control operated.

---

# 45. Step 23 – Evaluate Evidence Consistency

Suppose:

Risk register:

**35 critical risks**

Executive dashboard:

**31 critical risks**

Risk committee minutes:

**33 critical risks**

These numbers should reconcile.

Inconsistent records may indicate:

* Data-quality problems
* Reporting weaknesses
* Manual manipulation
* Timing differences

The auditor should investigate.

---

# 46. Step 24 – Test Previous Findings

Internal Audit should not ignore historical findings.

Suppose the previous audit found:

> Risk treatment plans were frequently overdue.

The current audit tests:

**40 high-risk treatment plans**

Results:

**14 overdue**

This indicates the previous issue may not have been effectively remediated.

---

# 47. Repeat Finding

A repeat finding is especially important because it may demonstrate:

* Ineffective remediation
* Weak management accountability
* Insufficient root-cause analysis
* Poor corrective action

Repeat findings often deserve elevated management attention.

---

# 48. Step 25 – Evaluate Compensating Controls

Suppose a primary control fails.

Example:

> Automated risk-acceptance workflow is not working.

But:

* Weekly manual review exists.
* CISO reviews all critical acceptances.
* Exception reports are generated.

These may reduce the residual risk.

The auditor should determine whether the compensating controls are:

* Designed appropriately
* Operating consistently
* Sufficiently evidenced

---

# 49. Step 26 – Determine Control Effectiveness

A practical evaluation could be:

### Effective

Control is appropriately designed and operates consistently.

### Partially Effective

Control exists but has limited or inconsistent weaknesses.

### Ineffective

Control is absent, poorly designed, or fails materially.

Organizations may use different rating scales, but the criteria should be defined before testing.

---

# 50. Example Control Test Summary

| Control                   | Design    | Operation   | Conclusion          |
| ------------------------- | --------- | ----------- | ------------------- |
| Quarterly risk review     | Effective | Effective   | Effective           |
| Risk ownership            | Effective | Partial     | Partially Effective |
| Risk acceptance           | Partial   | Ineffective | Ineffective         |
| Risk treatment monitoring | Effective | Partial     | Partially Effective |
| Executive reporting       | Effective | Partial     | Partially Effective |

This creates an objective foundation for audit conclusions.

---

# 51. Step 27 – Document Exceptions

For every exception, document:

* Control
* Requirement
* Population
* Sample
* Expected condition
* Actual condition
* Evidence
* Cause
* Impact
* Conclusion

Example:

> **Expected:** All critical risks require CIO approval.
> **Actual:** 2 of 25 sampled critical-risk acceptances lacked CIO approval.
> **Evidence:** GRC workflow and approval records.
> **Impact:** Critical risks may be accepted without appropriate executive oversight.

---

# 52. Step 28 – Determine Root Cause

Potential root causes include:

### People

Insufficient training.

### Process

Poor procedure.

### Technology

Workflow does not enforce policy.

### Governance

Unclear accountability.

### Data

Incomplete risk information.

### Culture

Management bypasses controls.

Identifying the correct root cause leads to better recommendations.

---

# 53. Step 29 – Evaluate Impact

The auditor should consider:

* Financial impact
* Operational impact
* Security impact
* Regulatory impact
* Privacy impact
* Reputational impact

Example:

Unauthorized risk acceptance may result in:

> Critical cybersecurity risks remaining untreated.

That is potentially significant even if no incident has occurred.

---

# 54. Important Audit Principle

> **A control failure does not necessarily mean an incident occurred.**

Internal Audit evaluates whether the control was capable of managing the risk.

For example:

If a backup control fails but no data is lost, the control can still be ineffective.

The absence of an incident does not prove the control worked.

---

# 55. Step 30 – Form the Audit Conclusion

After testing, the auditor evaluates all evidence.

Example:

> Cybersecurity risk-management controls are generally established; however, weaknesses were identified in risk ownership, risk acceptance approval, and treatment monitoring.

This becomes the basis for the audit report.

---

A strong audit workpaper demonstrates:

**Requirement**

↓

**Risk**

↓

**Control**

↓

**Population**

↓

**Sample**

↓

**Evidence**

↓

**Test**

↓

**Result**

↓

↓

**Conclusion**

Every major finding should be traceable through this chain.

# 57. Practical GRC Exercise

GlobalConnect has a control:

> **All high and critical cybersecurity risks must be formally reviewed by the Cybersecurity Risk Committee every quarter.**

Population:

**40 high/critical risks**

Evidence shows:

* 36 reviewed quarterly
* 2 reviewed late
* 1 reviewed only twice
* 1 has no evidence of review

The risk committee claims:

> "All risks were discussed informally."

As the auditor, determine:

1. Is the control design effective?
2. Is operating effectiveness demonstrated?
3. What additional evidence should be requested?
4. Are the four exceptions findings?
5. What is the likely root cause?
6. What is the potential business impact?
7. What should management do?
8. Should this become a formal audit finding?

# 58. Second Practical Exercise

The organization requires:

> **CISO approval for critical cybersecurity risk acceptance.**

Population:

**18 critical accepted risks**

Audit results:

* 15 have CISO approval.
* 2 have CIO approval.
* 1 has Risk Manager approval.

The policy clearly states:

> CISO approval is mandatory.

Determine:

* Is the control operating effectively?
* Are the CIO approvals valid substitutes?
* Is the Risk Manager approval acceptable?

* What evidence should be retained?
* What is the root cause?
* Should the GRC workflow be redesigned?

# 59. Advanced Audit Exercise

The GRC dashboard reports:

**8 critical cybersecurity risks**

The underlying risk register contains:

**13**

The audit team discovers:

* 3 risks are excluded by dashboard filters.
* 1 risk is classified incorrectly.
* 1 risk was created but not synchronized.

The dashboard therefore understates critical-risk exposure.

The auditor should consider:

### Control Design

Are dashboard rules appropriately designed?

### Operating Effectiveness

Did the reporting process operate correctly?

### Data Quality

Is the source data complete?

### Governance

Are executives receiving reliable information?

This could become a significant governance finding.

# 60. Key GRC Lesson

The most important principle in control testing is:

> **Do not test whether documentation exists. Test whether the control actually addresses the risk and operates effectively.**

A mature GRC auditor does not stop at:

> "The policy exists."

Instead, the auditor asks:

> **Does the control exist?**

> **Is it appropriately designed?**

> **Did it operate?**

> **Did it operate consistently?**

> **Is there reliable evidence?**

> **Did it actually address the risk?**

> **What happens when the control fails?**

# 19.13 GRC Audit and Assurance Case Studies

## Part 3 – Managing Significant Audit Findings

A significant audit finding represents a material weakness, deficiency, or control failure that requires management attention and corrective action.

The purpose of finding management is not simply to document:

> "The control failed."

A mature GRC process determines:

> **What failed, why it failed, what risk it creates, who is accountable, how it will be corrected, when it must be corrected, and how Internal Audit will verify that the corrective action actually worked.**

The lifecycle is:

**Exception → Validation → Finding → Risk Assessment → Root Cause → Impact → Rating → Recommendation → Management Action → Ownership → Due Date → Tracking → Validation → Closure**

---

# Case Study: GlobalConnect Telecom

In Part 2, Internal Audit tested GlobalConnect's cybersecurity GRC controls.

The audit identified several exceptions:

1. Critical risks without appropriate ownership
2. Risk acceptance without required approval
3. Overdue risk treatment
4. Inaccurate executive risk reporting
5. Repeat findings from the previous audit

The audit team now needs to determine which exceptions should become formal audit findings.

---

# 1. What Is an Audit Finding?

An audit finding communicates a condition where:

> **The organization's actual control environment does not meet an established requirement or expected condition, creating a risk that requires management attention.**

A strong finding connects:

**Criteria → Condition → Cause → Risk/Effect → Recommendation**

This is sometimes referred to as the **5 Cs** of an audit finding.

---

# 2. The Five Elements of a Strong Finding

## 1. Criteria

What should happen?

## 2. Condition

What actually happened?

## 3. Cause

Why did it happen?

## 4. Consequence

What could happen because of it?

## 5. Corrective Action

What should management do?

These elements make the finding actionable.

---

# 3. Example

### Criteria

Critical cybersecurity risks must receive approval from the CISO before acceptance.

### Condition

3 of 18 sampled critical-risk acceptances lacked documented CISO approval.

### Cause

The GRC workflow does not enforce approval thresholds.

### Consequence

Critical risks could be accepted without appropriate executive oversight.

### Corrective Action

Configure mandatory approval routing and implement periodic exception monitoring.

This is much stronger than:

> "Risk acceptance process needs improvement."

---

# 4. Step 1 – Validate the Exception

Before creating a formal finding, Internal Audit confirms:

* Evidence
* Population
* Sample
* Requirement
* Control
* Exception
* Management explanation

This prevents inaccurate findings.

---

# 5. Management Challenge

Management may respond:

> "The CISO verbally approved the risks."

The auditor should ask:

* Is verbal approval permitted?
* Is there evidence?
* Was approval given before acceptance?
* Does policy require documented approval?
* Who can approve the risk?
* Was the approval within delegated authority?

The auditor should evaluate the evidence against the established criteria.

---

# 6. Step 2 – Determine Whether the Issue Is a Finding

Not every exception becomes a formal finding.

Consider:

* Frequency
* Severity
* Risk impact
* Control importance
* Root cause
* Regulatory implications
* Recurrence
* Compensating controls

An isolated documentation error may not have the same significance as a systemic approval failure.

---

# 7. Example: Minor Exception

Suppose:

**1 of 50**

risk records lacks a review date.

The risk is low.

A manual review was nevertheless completed.

This may be recorded as:

> **Minor control exception**

rather than a major audit finding.

---

# 8. Example: Significant Finding

Suppose:

**8 of 30**

critical risks lack appropriate approval.

That is:

**26.7%**

of the sample.

More importantly, the issue indicates a systemic governance weakness.

This should likely become a formal finding.

---

# 9. Step 3 – Determine Finding Severity

GlobalConnect uses four levels:

### Critical

Potential for severe enterprise impact.

### High

Significant weakness requiring senior management attention.

### Medium

Material weakness requiring management remediation.

### Low

Limited control deficiency.

The organization should define these ratings in its audit methodology.

---

# 10. Factors Affecting Severity

Severity should consider:

* Impact
* Likelihood
* Control failure
* Scope
* Regulatory exposure
* Data sensitivity
* Business criticality
* Repeat nature
* Duration
* Existing compensating controls

A finding should not be rated solely by counting exceptions.

---

# 11. Impact vs Exception Rate

Suppose two controls each have:

**5% exception rate.**

### Control A

One missing low-risk documentation record.

### Control B

One unauthorized approval for a critical cybersecurity risk.

The percentages are identical.

The risk is not.

Therefore:

> **Audit severity must reflect business risk, not merely statistical frequency.**

---

# 12. Step 4 – Determine Root Cause

A finding becomes much more valuable when the root cause is identified.

Possible causes:

### People

Insufficient training.

### Process

Poorly defined procedures.

### Technology

GRC workflow does not enforce policy.

### Governance

Unclear ownership.

### Data

Incomplete or inaccurate information.

### Culture

Management routinely bypasses controls.

---

# 13. Five Whys Example

### Problem

Critical risks are accepted without required approval.

### Why?

Managers can approve them.

### Why?

The GRC workflow does not enforce approval hierarchy.

### Why?

Approval rules were never configured.

### Why?

The implementation focused on workflow migration rather than policy enforcement.

### Why?

There was no formal control-design review after GRC implementation.

### Root Cause

> **Insufficient governance over GRC workflow design and control configuration.**

This is more useful than blaming individual users.

---

# 14. Step 5 – Determine Consequence

The finding should explain the potential consequence.

For example:

> Inadequate risk-acceptance controls may allow material cybersecurity risks to remain untreated without appropriate executive awareness, potentially increasing exposure to operational disruption, data compromise, regulatory penalties, and financial loss.

The consequence should be:

* Specific
* Risk-based
* Credible

Avoid exaggerated statements.

---

# 15. Step 6 – Determine Inherent Risk

Suppose:

Likelihood:

**4 / 5**

Impact:

**5 / 5**

Inherent risk:

**20 / 25**

Classification:

**Critical**

This represents the risk before considering controls.

---

# 16. Step 7 – Consider Control Effectiveness

Suppose the organization has:

* CISO review
* Risk Committee oversight
* GRC workflow
* Monthly reporting

These controls reduce the likelihood.

Residual risk may therefore be:

**High**

rather than Critical.

The audit finding should consider the control environment as it actually operates.

---

# 17. Inherent vs Residual Risk

### Inherent Risk

Risk before controls.

### Residual Risk

Risk after controls.

Example:

**Inherent Risk = Critical**

↓

Controls

↓

**Residual Risk = High**

If controls fail:

**Residual Risk may increase toward Critical.**

---

# 18. Step 8 – Identify the Risk Owner

The audit finding needs a responsible executive.

For example:

**CISO**

may own cybersecurity governance.

But depending on the issue:

**CIO**

may be the accountable executive.

The finding should not simply be assigned to:

> "IT."

---

# 19. Step 9 – Assign Action Owner

The person responsible for fixing the issue may be different from the risk owner.

Example:

**Risk Owner:** CISO

**Action Owner:** GRC Platform Manager

**Control Owner:** Enterprise Risk Manager

This distinction improves accountability.

---

# 20. Step 10 – Develop Corrective Action

A good corrective action addresses the root cause.

Weak:

> "Provide additional training."

Stronger:

> "Configure the GRC platform to enforce approval thresholds based on risk severity and prevent acceptance until the required approval is recorded."

The second action addresses the actual control weakness.

---

# 21. Corrective vs Preventive Action

### Corrective

Fix the current issue.

Example:

Review all existing critical-risk acceptances.

### Preventive

Prevent recurrence.

Example:

Automate approval routing.

A strong action plan may require both.

---

# 22. Step 11 – Establish Management Action Plan

Example:

| Action                               | Owner              | Due Date |
| ------------------------------------ | ------------------ | -------- |
| Review all critical-risk acceptances | CISO               | 15 days  |
| Correct unauthorized approvals       | GRC Manager        | 30 days  |
| Configure workflow                   | GRC Platform Owner | 60 days  |
| Perform control testing              | Internal Controls  | 75 days  |
| Report results                       | CISO               | 90 days  |

---

# 23. SMART Corrective Actions

Actions should be:

**Specific**

**Measurable**

**Achievable**

**Relevant**

**Time-bound**

Weak:

> "Improve risk management."

Strong:

> "Configure mandatory CISO approval for all critical risk acceptance workflows by October 31, 2026, and validate 100% of critical-risk acceptance transactions during the following quarter."

---

# 24. Step 12 – Set Due Dates

Due dates should reflect:

* Risk severity
* Complexity
* Resources
* Dependencies
* Regulatory requirements

Example:

### Critical

15–30 days

### High

30–60 days

### Medium

60–90 days

### Low

90–180 days

These are illustrative and should follow the organization's approved methodology.

---

# 25. Step 13 – Management Response

Management should formally respond to each finding.

A response should include:

* Agreement/disagreement
* Root cause
* Corrective action
* Owner
* Due date
* Resources
* Expected completion

Example:

> Management agrees with the finding and will redesign the GRC approval workflow to enforce CISO approval for critical-risk acceptance.

---

# 26. Management Disagreement

Management may state:

> "We disagree with the finding."

Internal Audit should evaluate the response objectively.

Possible outcomes:

### Finding remains

Evidence supports the finding.

### Finding modified

New evidence changes the scope or wording.

### Finding withdrawn

Evidence demonstrates the original conclusion was incorrect.

Audit should not change a finding merely because management disagrees.

---

# 27. Step 14 – Risk Acceptance During Audit

Management may say:

> "We accept the risk instead of implementing the recommendation."

Internal Audit should distinguish:

**Management risk acceptance**

from:

**Audit finding closure.**

A finding may remain open even if management formally accepts the underlying risk.

Internal Audit determines whether the control deficiency has been addressed.

---

# 28. Step 15 – Finding Register

All significant findings should be tracked centrally.

Example:

| ID      | Finding                      | Severity | Owner | Due | Status      |
| ------- | ---------------------------- | -------- | ----- | --- | ----------- |
| AUD-001 | Risk ownership gaps          | High     | CISO  | 30d | Open        |
| AUD-002 | Unauthorized risk acceptance | Critical | CIO   | 15d | Open        |
| AUD-003 | Overdue treatment            | High     | CISO  | 45d | In Progress |
| AUD-004 | Reporting inaccuracies       | Medium   | GRC   | 60d | In Progress |

This creates audit accountability.

---

# 29. Step 16 – Track Aging

Audit findings should be monitored by age.

Example:

| Age         | Findings |
| ----------- | -------: |
| 0–30 days   |        5 |
| 31–60 days  |        4 |
| 61–90 days  |        3 |
| 91–180 days |        2 |
| >180 days   |        1 |

A large number of old findings may indicate:

> **Weak remediation governance.**

---

# 30. Step 17 – Monitor Overdue Findings

Suppose a high-risk finding is due:

**September 30**

On October 1:

**Overdue**

The audit function should determine:

* Why is it overdue?
* Has risk increased?
* Does management need escalation?
* Is temporary risk acceptance required?

---

# 31. Finding Escalation

A possible escalation path is:

**Action Owner**

↓

**Control Owner**

↓

**CISO**

↓

**CIO**

↓

**Risk Committee**

↓

**Audit Committee**

The escalation level should reflect the significance of the finding.

---

# 32. Step 18 – Audit Committee Reporting

Significant findings should be reported to the appropriate governance body.

A Board/Audit Committee report may show:

### Critical

**2**

### High

**7**

### Medium

**14**

### Overdue High/Critical

**3**

The committee should focus on:

> **What management is doing about the highest-risk issues.**

---

# 33. Step 19 – Finding Trend Analysis

Internal Audit can analyze trends.

Example:

| Year | High/Critical Findings |
| ---- | ---------------------: |
| 2023 |                     18 |
| 2024 |                     15 |
| 2025 |                     12 |
| 2026 |                      8 |

This suggests improving control maturity.

But another metric is important:

> **Repeat findings**

---

# 34. Repeat Findings

Suppose:

**2025:** Risk treatment overdue.

**2026:** Risk treatment overdue again.

This is not simply another finding.

It may indicate:

> **Management's corrective actions were ineffective.**

Repeat findings should generally receive increased attention.

---

# 35. Step 20 – Validate Remediation

Management says:

> "The issue is fixed."

Internal Audit should request evidence.

For example:

* Updated policy
* GRC configuration
* Workflow test
* New approval records
* Risk register
* Management report

Then Internal Audit tests whether the corrective action actually works.

---

# 36. Remediation Validation Example

Finding:

> Critical risk acceptance lacked required approval.

Management implements:

**Automated approval workflow.**

Internal Audit tests:

**20 new critical risk acceptances**

Results:

**20 / 20**

contain valid CISO approval.

Conclusion:

> Corrective action appears effective.

---

# 37. Step 21 – Do Not Close Too Early

Management may complete:

> "Workflow configuration."

But the control has not yet operated long enough to demonstrate effectiveness.

Internal Audit may therefore classify the finding as:

**Remediation implemented – effectiveness validation pending**

rather than:

**Closed**

---

# 38. Implementation vs Effectiveness

### Implementation

The corrective action was completed.

### Effectiveness

The corrective action works in practice.

Example:

**PAM deployed**

does not automatically prove:

**Privileged access is controlled effectively.**

Testing is still required.

---

# 39. Step 22 – Finding Closure

A finding can normally be closed when:

1. Corrective action is complete.
2. Evidence is sufficient.
3. Root cause has been addressed.
4. Control operates effectively.
5. Residual risk is acceptable.
6. Internal Audit validates remediation.

The closure decision should be documented.

---

# 40. Finding Closure Example

### Original Finding

Unauthorized acceptance of critical risks.

### Root Cause

GRC workflow did not enforce approval hierarchy.

### Remediation

Approval workflow redesigned.

### Evidence

* New workflow configuration
* Test results
* 20 successful approvals
* Updated procedure

### Validation

Internal Audit independently tested the process.

### Conclusion

**Finding Closed**

---

# 41. Step 23 – Residual Risk After Remediation

Even after remediation, some risk may remain.

Example:

Before:

**Residual Risk = Critical**

After remediation:

**Residual Risk = Medium**

The finding may be closed because:

> The control deficiency has been corrected and residual risk is within appetite.

Finding closure does not mean:

> **Zero risk exists.**

---

# 42. Step 24 – Risk Transfer vs Risk Reduction

Management may try to resolve a finding by:

> "We transferred the service to another supplier."

This does not automatically eliminate risk.

The organization may still retain:

* Third-party risk
* Contractual risk
* Concentration risk
* Data risk
* Operational dependency

The audit should evaluate whether the underlying risk actually changed.

---

# 43. Step 25 – Finding Communication

A strong finding should be:

* Clear
* Concise
* Evidence-based
* Non-emotional
* Risk-oriented
* Actionable

Avoid:

> "Management has completely failed to manage cybersecurity risk."

Prefer:

> "The cybersecurity risk-acceptance process does not consistently enforce the organization's required approval thresholds."

The second is objective and defensible.

# 44. Example Significant Finding

## Finding AUD-002 – Critical Risk Acceptance Governance Weakness

### Rating

**Critical**

### Criteria

Critical cybersecurity risks require CISO approval before formal risk acceptance.

### Condition

Testing identified 3 of 18 critical-risk acceptances without documented CISO approval.

### Cause

The GRC workflow does not technically enforce the required approval hierarchy.

### Risk

Critical cybersecurity risks may be accepted without appropriate executive oversight, increasing the organization's exposure to significant operational, regulatory, financial, and security consequences.

### Recommendation

Configure mandatory approval routing and implement periodic monitoring of critical-risk acceptance transactions.

### Management Action

Management will redesign the GRC workflow and review all existing critical-risk acceptances.

### Owner

CISO

### Due Date

30 days

# 19.13 GRC Audit and Assurance Case Studies

## Part 4 – Building an Integrated Assurance Program

An **Integrated Assurance Program** brings together the organization's different assurance activities so that management and the Board can understand, in a coordinated way:

* What risks are being assured
* Which controls are being tested
* Who is performing the assurance
* What evidence is available
* Where assurance overlaps
* Where assurance gaps exist
* Which significant issues remain unresolved

The objective is not to make every assurance function perform the same work.

The objective is:

> **Provide sufficient, coordinated assurance over the organization's most important risks while minimizing duplication, conflicting conclusions, and unnecessary audit effort.**

The integrated assurance lifecycle is:

**Enterprise Risks → Assurance Universe → Assurance Mapping → Assurance Providers → Control Coverage → Testing → Findings → Aggregation → Executive Reporting → Assurance Gaps → Improvement**

---

# 1. What Is Integrated Assurance?

An organization may have several groups evaluating controls:

### First Line

Business and operational management.

### Second Line

Risk, Compliance, GRC, Privacy, Security, BCM, and other oversight functions.

### Third Line

Internal Audit.

### External Assurance

External auditors, certification bodies, regulators, customers, and independent assessors.

Without coordination, several teams may test the same control while another important risk receives no assurance.

Integrated assurance addresses this problem.

---

* Privacy

Each function performs assurance activities independently.

The result is:

**Control A**

tested by:

* Internal Audit
* ISO auditor
* Compliance
* Customer audit

while:

**Control B**

receives almost no independent assurance.

This creates an assurance imbalance.

# 59. Assurance Coordination vs Independence

This is critical.

Integration should mean:

**Coordinate**

not:

**Control everyone.**

Internal Audit must maintain its independent assurance role.

It can:

* Share plans
* Share information
* Review other assurance work
* Coordinate schedules

But should retain:

* Independent judgment
* Independent conclusions
* Independent reporting

# 60. Three Levels of Assurance

A useful structure is:

### Management Assurance

> "We believe our controls are working."

### Second-Line Assurance

> "We have independently challenged and tested management's control environment."

### Internal Audit Assurance

> "We independently assessed the governance, risk management, and controls."

### External Assurance

> "An independent external party assessed the defined scope."

Together they provide layered assurance.

# 64. Key Metrics

A mature Integrated Assurance Program should measure:

### Risk Coverage

Percentage of critical risks with adequate assurance.

### Control Coverage

Percentage of critical controls independently tested.

### Assurance Duplication

Number of overlapping assurance activities.

### Assurance Gaps

High-risk areas without sufficient assurance.

### Finding Recurrence

Percentage of repeat findings.

### Remediation

Percentage of high-risk findings closed on time.

### Assurance Cost

Resources spent on assurance.

# 70. Final Principle

The ultimate objective of GRC audit and assurance is not:

> **"Find as many problems as possible."**

It is:

> **"Provide reliable, risk-based, independent assurance that enables management and the Board to understand whether the organization's governance, risk management, and controls are working as intended."**

The progression is therefore:

**Audit Planning**

→ **Control Testing**

→ **Finding Management**

→ **Integrated Assurance**

→ **Enterprise Confidence**

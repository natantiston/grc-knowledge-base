# Lesson 16.9 – Measuring Security Awareness Effectiveness

> **Topic:** Measuring Security Awareness Effectiveness
>
> **Difficulty:** Beginner

> **Estimated Reading Time:** 10–15 minutes

## Introduction

A security awareness program should not be judged only by how much training an organization delivers.

An organization may have:

* 100% training completion.
* Hundreds of awareness emails.
* Multiple security workshops.
* Regular phishing simulations.

Yet employees may still make security mistakes or fail to report suspicious activity.

This creates an important distinction:

> **Training activity does not automatically equal security effectiveness.**

Measuring security awareness effectiveness allows an organization to determine whether its awareness activities are actually improving employee knowledge, behavior, and security outcomes.

## Why Measure Security Awareness?

Measurement helps an organization answer important questions:

* Are employees completing required training?
* Do employees understand security requirements?
* Are employees changing their behavior?
* Are phishing reporting rates improving?
* Are security-related mistakes decreasing?
* Which departments require additional support?
* Are awareness investments reducing human-related risk?
* Is the program meeting its objectives?

Without measurement, security awareness can become a collection of activities without evidence that those activities are producing meaningful results.

## From Activity to Effectiveness

A mature awareness program should progress through several levels of measurement:

**Activity → Knowledge → Behavior → Risk Reduction**

For example:

**Activity**

Employees complete phishing awareness training.

↓

**Knowledge**

Employees demonstrate that they understand phishing indicators.

↓

**Behavior**

Employees correctly identify and report simulated phishing messages.

↓

**Risk Reduction**

Employees are less likely to interact with real phishing attacks.

Each level provides stronger evidence of effectiveness.

### Activity Metrics

Activity metrics measure what the organization did.

Examples include:

* Number of employees trained.
* Training completion rate.
* Number of awareness campaigns.
* Number of phishing simulations.
* Number of newsletters distributed.
* Number of workshops conducted.

These metrics are useful for program management but do not prove that security behavior improved.

### Knowledge Metrics

Knowledge metrics measure whether employees understood the training.

Examples include:

* Assessment scores.
* Quiz results.
* Knowledge-check performance.
* Training assessment pass rates.
* Survey results.

For example:

> 94% of employees achieved the required score on the phishing awareness assessment.

This provides more information than completion alone, but it still does not prove that employees will behave securely in real situations.

### Behavioral Metrics

Behavioral metrics measure what employees actually do.

Examples include:

* Phishing simulation click rate.
* Phishing reporting rate.
* Time to report suspicious messages.
* Secure data-handling behavior.
* Incident reporting behavior.
* Repeated security mistakes.

Behavioral metrics are generally more valuable for determining whether awareness is influencing day-to-day security practices.

### Risk Metrics

Risk metrics connect employee behavior to organizational risk.

Examples include:

* Reduction in human-related security incidents.
* Reduction in successful phishing attacks.
* Reduction in accidental data exposure.
* Reduction in policy violations.
* Reduction in security incidents caused by human error.

These metrics help management understand whether awareness activities are contributing to broader cybersecurity objectives.

## Awareness Metrics

An awareness metric is a measurement used to understand some aspect of the security awareness program.

Examples include:

| Metric                  | What It Measures       |
| ----------------------- | ---------------------- |
| Training completion     | Participation          |
| Assessment score        | Knowledge              |
| Phishing click rate     | Risky behavior         |
| Phishing reporting rate | Positive behavior      |
| Time to report          | Responsiveness         |
| Repeat failure rate     | Persistent weakness    |
| Security incident rate  | Potential risk outcome |

A mature program should use a combination of metrics rather than relying on one number.

## Completion Rate

Training completion is one of the most commonly reported awareness metrics.

It is calculated as:

**Completion Rate = Completed Training ÷ Assigned Training × 100**

For example:

> 950 employees complete mandatory training out of 1,000 assigned employees.

The completion rate is:

> **95%**

This can demonstrate that the organization is successfully delivering required training.

However, a 95% completion rate does not necessarily mean that employees are security-aware.

An employee could complete a course without understanding or applying the information.

## Assessment Scores

Assessment scores provide additional information.

For example:

> Average phishing awareness assessment score: 88%

This may indicate that employees understand the material presented in the course.

However, assessment results can also be misleading.

Employees may memorize answers without developing the ability to apply the concepts in realistic situations.

This is why knowledge measurements should be combined with behavioral measurements.

## Phishing Simulation Metrics

Phishing simulations provide useful behavioral data.

Common metrics include:

* Click rate.
* Credential submission rate.
* Attachment interaction rate.
* Reporting rate.
* Time to report.
* Repeat interaction rate.

For example:

**Click rate:** 10%

**Reporting rate:** 38%

**Credential submission:** 2%

These metrics provide a more realistic picture of employee behavior than training completion alone.

## Incident Reporting

Employee reporting behavior is an important security awareness indicator.

Suppose employees receive suspicious emails in the normal course of business.

The organization can measure:

* Number of reports.
* Percentage of employees reporting.
* Time required to report.
* Number of legitimate threats identified through employee reports.

An increase in reporting may indicate that employees are becoming more comfortable identifying and reporting suspicious activity.

However, organizations should also consider whether employees are submitting excessive false reports.

### False Positives

Not every reported message will be malicious.

Employees may report legitimate emails because they are uncertain.

This is not necessarily a negative outcome.

Early in a security awareness program, increased reporting may actually be desirable because employees are becoming more willing to ask for help.

The security team can then improve employee guidance on distinguishing suspicious activity from legitimate communication.

## Behavioral Change Measurement

Behavioral change is one of the most important but difficult aspects of awareness measurement.

The organization should ask:

> **Are employees doing something differently because of the awareness program?**

Examples include:

* More employees reporting phishing.
* Fewer employees clicking simulated phishing links.
* More employees using approved data-sharing methods.
* Fewer employees leaving sensitive documents unsecured.
* More employees using MFA correctly.
* Faster reporting of security incidents.

Behavioral change should ideally be measured over time rather than through a single event.

## Measuring Trends

A single measurement provides limited insight.

For example:

> Phishing click rate = 12%

Without historical information, it is difficult to determine whether this is good or bad.

Suppose previous results were:

| Campaign | Click Rate |
| -------- | ---------: |
| 1        |        25% |
| 2        |        19% |
| 3        |        15% |
| 4        |        12% |

The trend indicates improvement.

Similarly, if reporting rates increase:

| Campaign | Reporting Rate |
| -------- | -------------: |
| 1        |            14% |
| 2        |            22% |
| 3        |            31% |
| 4        |            39% |

the organization can see evidence of stronger reporting behavior.

## Baselines

Before setting improvement targets, organizations should establish a baseline.

A baseline represents the organization's starting point.

For example:

> Initial phishing click rate: 23%

The organization may then establish a target:

> Target click rate: below 12%

Future measurements can be compared against the baseline.

Baselines should be established for the most important awareness behaviors rather than every possible activity.

## Benchmarks and Targets

Organizations may establish internal targets for awareness metrics.

Examples:

* 95% mandatory training completion.
* 90% assessment pass rate.
* Increasing phishing reporting rate.
* Decreasing simulated phishing interaction rate.
* Reducing repeat failures.
* Improving incident reporting time.

Targets should be realistic and risk-based.

A target should not exist simply because another organization achieved a particular number.

### Why 100% Is Not Always the Goal

Organizations sometimes assume that every security metric should reach 100%.

For example:

> "Our target should be zero employees clicking phishing simulations."

This may not be realistic.

Real phishing attacks are continuously evolving, and some simulated scenarios may intentionally be sophisticated.

A more meaningful objective may be:

* Continuous improvement.
* Reduced high-risk behavior.
* Increased reporting.
* Faster detection.
* Better resilience among high-risk roles.

## Measuring Training Effectiveness

Training effectiveness can be evaluated by comparing employee performance before and after training.

For example:

**Before training**

Phishing click rate: 20%

**After training**

Phishing click rate: 13%

**Reporting rate**

Before: 17%

After: 35%

This provides stronger evidence that training may have influenced behavior.

However, the organization should avoid claiming that training alone caused the improvement without considering other factors.

## Correlation Between Awareness and Incidents

Organizations can also examine whether awareness improvements correspond with changes in actual security incidents.

For example:

> Employee phishing reports increased while successful phishing incidents decreased.

This may indicate that improved awareness is contributing to stronger organizational resilience.

However, cybersecurity incidents are influenced by many factors, including:

* Technical controls.
* Threat volume.
* Attack sophistication.
* Security monitoring.
* Identity controls.
* Email filtering.
* Incident response.

Therefore, awareness metrics should not be interpreted in isolation.

## Leading and Lagging Indicators

Security awareness programs can use both **leading** and **lagging indicators**.

### Leading Indicators

Leading indicators provide information about activities or behaviors that may influence future outcomes.

Examples include:

* Training completion.
* Assessment performance.
* Phishing reporting.
* Participation in awareness activities.
* Security policy acknowledgments.

### Lagging Indicators

Lagging indicators measure outcomes that have already occurred.

Examples include:

* Number of successful phishing incidents.
* Security incidents caused by human error.
* Data exposure incidents.
* Policy violations.

A mature program should use both.

For example:

**Leading indicator:**

Phishing reporting increased.

**Lagging indicator:**

Successful phishing incidents decreased.

Together, these provide a stronger picture of program effectiveness.

## Risk-Based Measurement

Not every awareness topic has the same importance.

Organizations should prioritize measurement according to risk.

For example, a financial institution may place greater emphasis on:

* Phishing.
* Business email compromise.
* Data protection.
* Social engineering.

A software company may place greater emphasis on:

* Secure development.
* Secrets management.
* Data protection.
* Supply chain security.

A healthcare organization may prioritize:

* Privacy.
* Patient information protection.
* Phishing.
* Unauthorized disclosure.

Metrics should therefore reflect the organization's actual threat and risk environment.

## GRC Perspective

From a GRC perspective, security awareness metrics provide evidence that security controls are being monitored and evaluated.

A useful relationship is:

**Requirement → Awareness Objective → Metric → Result → Risk Assessment → Improvement**

For example:

**Requirement:** Employees must receive security awareness training.

**Objective:** Ensure employees understand phishing risks.

**Metric:** Phishing reporting rate.

**Result:** Reporting increased from 20% to 37%.

**Risk Assessment:** Employee phishing resilience improved.

**Improvement:** Continue targeted training for high-risk groups.

This makes awareness measurement part of the broader governance and risk management process.

## Common Measurement Mistakes

Organizations should avoid several common mistakes.

### Measuring Only Completion

Completion demonstrates participation, not effectiveness.

### Using Too Many Metrics

A dashboard containing dozens of metrics can make it difficult to identify what actually matters.

### Ignoring Behavioral Data

Knowledge tests alone do not demonstrate secure behavior.

### Focusing Only on Negative Behavior

Reporting and other positive security behaviors should also be measured.

### Comparing Inconsistent Campaigns

Metrics can be misleading if the difficulty of simulations or employee populations changes significantly.

### Ignoring Context

A sudden increase in phishing clicks may have several possible explanations.

### Failing to Act

Measurement has little value if the organization does not use the results to improve the program.

## Building an Awareness Measurement Framework

A simple framework can be structured around four levels:

### Level 1 – Participation

**Did employees participate?**

Examples:

* Completion rate.
* Attendance.
* Course participation.

### Level 2 – Knowledge

**Did employees understand the material?**

Examples:

* Assessment scores.
* Knowledge checks.
* Quiz performance.

### Level 3 – Behavior

**Did employees change what they do?**

Examples:

* Phishing reporting.
* Reduced clicking.
* Faster incident reporting.

### Level 4 – Risk

**Did the organization's human-related risk decrease?**

Examples:

* Reduced successful phishing incidents.
* Reduced human-error incidents.
* Reduced data exposure.

This model helps organizations move beyond basic training statistics.

## Key Takeaways

Measuring security awareness effectiveness allows organizations to determine whether their awareness program is actually improving security outcomes.

The key principles are:

1. **Measure more than training completion.**
2. **Evaluate knowledge as well as participation.**
3. **Measure real or simulated security behavior.**
4. **Track trends over time.**
5. **Establish meaningful baselines and targets.**
6. **Use both leading and lagging indicators.**
7. **Prioritize metrics according to organizational risk.**
8. **Connect awareness metrics to broader security outcomes.**
9. **Avoid excessive or meaningless metrics.**
10. **Use measurement results to continuously improve the awareness program.**

The ultimate purpose of security awareness measurement is not to prove that employees completed training. It is to determine whether the organization is **building stronger security behaviors and reducing human-related cybersecurity risk**.

# 19.3 Cybersecurity Governance Case Studies

## Part 1 – Establishing a Cybersecurity Governance Program

## 1. Case Study Overview

This case study demonstrates how a large organization establishes and matures a **cybersecurity governance program**.

The focus is not primarily on technical security implementation. Instead, it examines how an organization establishes:

* cybersecurity accountability;
* governance structures;
* policies;
* risk ownership;
* security decision-making;
* regulatory alignment;
* performance measurement;
* executive oversight.

The case illustrates an important GRC principle:

> **Cybersecurity governance establishes who makes security decisions, who is accountable for risk, how decisions are made, and how security performance is measured.**

---

# 2. Organization Profile

### Company

**GlobalNet Telecom Group**

### Industry

Telecommunications and Digital Services

### Employees

Approximately 12,000

### Annual Revenue

Approximately €3.8 billion

### Geographic Presence

* Spain
* Portugal
* France
* Germany
* Qatar
* Saudi Arabia

### Critical Services

* Mobile telecommunications
* Fixed broadband
* Enterprise connectivity
* Cloud services
* Digital customer platforms
* IoT services
* Managed services

---

# 3. Initial Situation

GlobalNet has invested heavily in cybersecurity technology.

The organization operates:

* Security Operations Center (SOC)
* SIEM
* Endpoint Detection and Response
* Firewalls
* Identity and Access Management
* Vulnerability Management
* Security Awareness
* Incident Response

However, the organization has a governance problem.

Security decisions are being made independently by different teams.

---

# 4. Governance Problems

The internal assessment identifies several weaknesses.

### Problem 1 – Unclear Accountability

Business executives are unsure who ultimately owns cybersecurity risk.

### Problem 2 – Fragmented Policies

Different departments maintain different security requirements.

### Problem 3 – Inconsistent Risk Decisions

Similar risks are treated differently across business units.

### Problem 4 – Weak Executive Reporting

The board receives technical security statistics but limited business risk information.

### Problem 5 – Compliance-Driven Security

Security activities are often initiated only when required by regulation or audit.

---

# 5. Governance Program Objective

The CEO establishes a cybersecurity governance transformation program.

The objectives are to:

1. establish clear accountability;
2. define cybersecurity governance structures;
3. establish security policies;
4. align cybersecurity with enterprise risk management;
5. integrate compliance requirements;
6. establish security performance metrics;
7. improve executive oversight;
8. establish consistent decision-making.

---

# 7. Board Responsibilities

The board is responsible for oversight rather than day-to-day cybersecurity operations.

The board should understand:

* major cyber risks;
* risk appetite;
* significant incidents;
* regulatory exposure;
* resilience;
* cybersecurity investment;
* management response.

The board should be able to answer:

> **"Is the organization managing cybersecurity risk at an acceptable level?"**

---

# 8. Executive Management Responsibilities

Executive management translates board expectations into business decisions.

Responsibilities include:

* approving cybersecurity strategy;
* allocating resources;
* accepting significant risks;
* approving major investments;
* resolving cross-business conflicts;
* ensuring regulatory compliance.

Cybersecurity becomes an enterprise responsibility rather than solely an IT responsibility.

---

# 9. CISO Responsibilities

The CISO leads the cybersecurity function.

Responsibilities include:

* cybersecurity strategy;
* security architecture;
* security operations;
* cyber risk management;
* incident response;
* security monitoring;
* security awareness;
* security performance;
* executive reporting.

The CISO should also provide independent challenge to business risk decisions where appropriate.

---

# 10. GRC Responsibilities

The cybersecurity GRC function provides governance and oversight.

Typical responsibilities include:

* policies;
* standards;
* risk assessments;
* compliance;
* control frameworks;
* audits;
* third-party risk;
* metrics;
* risk reporting;
* exception management.

GRC provides the bridge between cybersecurity operations and enterprise governance.

---

# 11. Business Owner Responsibilities

Business leaders remain responsible for the risks associated with their services.

For example:

The Digital Services Director owns risks associated with a customer application.

Cybersecurity may advise and monitor, but the business owner remains accountable for the business decision.

This prevents cybersecurity from becoming the sole owner of business risk.

---

# 12. Three Lines Alignment

The organization aligns cybersecurity governance with the Three Lines Model.

### First Line

Business and technology teams:

* own risks;
* operate controls;
* implement security requirements.

### Second Line

Cybersecurity GRC, risk, and compliance:

* establish frameworks;
* monitor;
* advise;
* challenge;
* report.

### Third Line

Internal Audit:

* independently evaluates governance;
* assesses risk management;
* evaluates controls.

---

# 19. Cybersecurity Policy

The top-level policy establishes mandatory organizational principles.

Examples:

* cybersecurity risk must be managed;
* critical information must be protected;
* access must be authorized;
* security incidents must be reported;
* regulatory obligations must be met;
* security controls must be monitored.

The policy should be approved by the appropriate governance authority.

---

# 20. Security Standards

Standards translate policy into measurable requirements.

Examples:

### Password Standard

Defines authentication requirements.

### Encryption Standard

Defines approved encryption requirements.

### Cloud Security Standard

Defines minimum cloud security controls.

### Logging Standard

Defines minimum logging requirements.

### Vulnerability Management Standard

Defines remediation expectations.

# 21. Governance Through Exceptions

Not every business system can immediately comply with every security requirement.

GlobalNet therefore establishes a formal security exception process.

Example:

A legacy telecommunications platform cannot support the current authentication standard.

Instead of ignoring the issue, the organization documents:

* requirement;
* reason for exception;
* affected system;
* risk;
* compensating controls;
* owner;
* expiration date;
* approval authority.

# 23. Cybersecurity Risk Appetite

The board establishes cybersecurity risk appetite.

Example:

GlobalNet has:

**Very low tolerance** for:

* prolonged critical service disruption;
* unauthorized disclosure of sensitive customer information;
* compromise of critical infrastructure;
* regulatory violations.

It may have greater tolerance for:

* minor security weaknesses;
* low-impact operational vulnerabilities;
* temporary deviations with approved compensating controls.

---

# 24. Risk-Based Governance

The governance process prioritizes decisions according to risk.

For example:

### Risk A

Low-impact internal system vulnerability.

### Risk B

Potential compromise of a national telecommunications platform.

Even if both systems have vulnerabilities, Risk B receives substantially greater governance attention.

---

# 25. Cybersecurity Governance Metrics

The organization establishes executive-level metrics.

Examples:

* critical vulnerabilities overdue;
* critical systems covered by EDR;
* privileged accounts reviewed;
* security incidents;
* mean time to detect;
* mean time to respond;
* unresolved high-risk findings;
* security exceptions;
* third-party security exposure.

Metrics are selected according to decision value rather than technical availability.

---

# 26. KPI and KRI Distinction

### KPI

Measures performance.

Example:

> Percentage of critical vulnerabilities remediated within SLA.

### KRI

Measures risk exposure.

Example:

> Number of critical vulnerabilities exceeding the remediation threshold.

The two metrics should not be confused.

# 43. Lessons Learned

## Lesson 1 – Governance Is Not the Same as Operations

The SOC operates security controls.

Governance determines whether the organization is managing security risk appropriately.

## Lesson 2 – Accountability Must Be Explicit

Security responsibilities should be documented and assigned.

## Lesson 3 – Business Ownership Matters

Cybersecurity should advise and challenge, but business owners remain accountable for business risk.

## Lesson 4 – Metrics Must Support Decisions

A dashboard containing hundreds of technical statistics does not necessarily provide effective governance.

## Lesson 5 – Exceptions Are Risk Decisions

A security exception should never become an undocumented permanent workaround.

## Lesson 6 – Governance Must Connect to Enterprise Risk

Cybersecurity risk should be visible within the organization's overall risk management framework.

# 46. Case Study Conclusion

The GlobalNet case demonstrates that effective cybersecurity governance is fundamentally about **accountability, decision-making, oversight, and risk management**.

A mature cybersecurity governance program connects:

**Business Strategy → Cybersecurity Strategy → Risk → Controls → Compliance → Assurance → Metrics → Executive Decisions**

The key principle is:

> **Cybersecurity governance ensures that security decisions are aligned with business objectives, risk appetite, regulatory obligations, and organizational accountability.**

For a GRC professional, the ability to design and operate this governance model is particularly important because it connects technical cybersecurity activities with **enterprise risk management and executive decision-making**.

# 19.3 Cybersecurity Governance Case Studies

## Part 2 – Cybersecurity Governance Maturity Assessment Case Study

## 1. Case Study Overview

This case study demonstrates how an organization can assess the maturity of its cybersecurity governance program, identify weaknesses, define a target state, and develop a practical improvement roadmap.

The organization has already established basic cybersecurity governance structures, but management wants to determine:

* How mature is the current governance program?
* Where are the major weaknesses?
* What should be improved first?
* What maturity level should the organization target?
* How can progress be measured?

The case demonstrates that **maturity assessment should lead to actionable improvement**, rather than simply producing a score.

---

# 2. Organization Profile

### Company

**EuroDigital Services Group**

### Industry

Telecommunications and Digital Services

# 3. Current Situation

EuroDigital has operated a cybersecurity program for several years.

The organization has:

* a CISO;
* cybersecurity policies;
* a SOC;
* vulnerability management;
* incident response;
* security awareness;
* ISO 27001 certification;
* cybersecurity risk assessments.

However, management believes that cybersecurity governance remains inconsistent.

The CEO therefore asks:

> **"Are we actually operating a mature cybersecurity governance program, or do we simply have many individual security activities?"**

---

# 4. Assessment Objective

The organization launches a cybersecurity governance maturity assessment.

The objectives are to:

1. establish the current maturity baseline;
2. identify governance weaknesses;
3. compare current capabilities with target maturity;
4. prioritize improvement initiatives;
5. establish measurable improvement objectives;
6. provide management with a governance roadmap.

---

# 5. Assessment Scope

The assessment covers eight domains:

1. Governance and accountability
2. Cybersecurity strategy
3. Risk management
4. Policies and standards
5. Security controls
6. Compliance and assurance
7. Performance measurement
8. Third-party cybersecurity governance

---

# 6. Maturity Model

EuroDigital adopts a five-level maturity model.

### Level 1 – Initial

Activities are largely reactive and ad hoc.

### Level 2 – Developing

Basic processes exist but are inconsistently applied.

### Level 3 – Defined

Processes are formally documented and consistently implemented.

### Level 4 – Managed

Processes are measured, monitored, and integrated with enterprise governance.

### Level 5 – Optimized

Processes are continuously improved using automation, analytics, intelligence, and measurable business outcomes.

---

# 7. Important Assessment Principle

A maturity score does **not** mean:

> "Level 5 is always better."

Instead:

> **The appropriate maturity level depends on business risk, regulatory obligations, criticality, and organizational objectives.**

A highly regulated critical infrastructure organization may require Level 4 or 5 for some capabilities, while Level 3 may be sufficient for lower-risk capabilities.

---

The assessment uses several evidence sources.

* procurement.

### Evidence Testing

The assessment examines:

* risk assessments;
* control records;
* exception records;
* security metrics;
* remediation records.

### Observation

The assessment team observes governance meetings and operational processes.

# 9. Assessment Scoring

**1 = Initial**

**2 = Developing**

**3 = Defined**

**4 = Managed**

**5 = Optimized**

Scores are supported by evidence rather than management opinion alone.

The EuroDigital case demonstrates how a cybersecurity governance maturity assessment can transform a collection of security activities into a structured improvement program.

# 19.3 Cybersecurity Governance Case Studies

## 1. Case Study Overview

This case study examines how an organization transforms a fragmented cybersecurity function into an integrated **Cybersecurity Governance Operating Model**.

The organization already has cybersecurity teams, policies, technologies, and compliance activities. However, these capabilities operate independently, creating duplicated work, unclear accountability, inconsistent risk decisions, and weak executive visibility.

The case focuses on how GRC professionals can design a target operating model that connects:

**Governance → Strategy → Risk → Compliance → Controls → Assurance → Operations → Reporting**

# 4. Key Governance Problems

### Problem 1 – Multiple Risk Registers

Different business units maintain separate risk registers.

Management cannot easily determine the organization's overall cyber risk exposure.

### Problem 2 – Duplicate Compliance Activities

The same control is assessed separately for:

* ISO 27001;
* GDPR;
* NIS2;
* internal policies;
* customer requirements.

### Problem 3 – Unclear Accountability

Security teams sometimes perform activities that should be owned by business or technology teams.

### Problem 4 – Inconsistent Reporting

Each region produces different cybersecurity metrics.

### Problem 5 – Fragmented Technology

The organization operates multiple GRC and security platforms that do not exchange information effectively.

* centralized visibility;
* efficient control management;
* effective assurance;
* risk-based decision-making.

Business and technology owners remain accountable for their risks.

Common cybersecurity requirements are established centrally.

### Principle 3 – Local Execution

Business units retain responsibility for operational execution.

### Principle 4 – Risk-Based Decision Making

Resources are prioritized according to risk.

### Principle 5 – Common Controls

Common controls should satisfy multiple requirements wherever practical.

### Principle 6 – Independent Assurance

Internal Audit remains independent from cybersecurity management.

# 44. Lessons Learned

## Lesson 1 – Operating Models Must Reflect the Organization

A global enterprise may require a federated model rather than complete centralization.

## Lesson 2 – Governance Must Have Clear Accountability

Every major decision should have an accountable owner.

## Lesson 3 – Common Controls Reduce Complexity

Multiple frameworks should be mapped to common controls where possible.

## Lesson 4 – GRC Is an Integration Function

GRC connects business, cybersecurity, risk, compliance, audit, and technology.

## Lesson 5 – Automation Should Support Governance

Automation should reduce administrative work, not replace human risk judgment.

## Lesson 6 – Governance Must Be Proportional

Too little governance creates uncontrolled risk.

Too much governance creates bureaucracy.

# 47. Case Study Conclusion

The NorthStar case demonstrates that cybersecurity transformation is not simply a technology modernization exercise.

A successful transformation creates an operating model in which:

**Business owns risk → GRC governs and challenges → Security implements and operates → Compliance verifies obligations → Internal Audit provides independent assurance → Management makes informed decisions.**

For a GRC professional, understanding the cybersecurity operating model is critical because it provides the organizational structure through which **risk, compliance, controls, governance, and assurance** are actually executed.

The ultimate objective is not to create more governance.

It is to create **effective governance that improves security decisions while minimizing unnecessary complexity**.

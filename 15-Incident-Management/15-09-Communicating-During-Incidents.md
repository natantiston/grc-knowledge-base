## Introduction

Effective communication is one of the most critical success factors during a cybersecurity incident. Even when technical response activities are executed correctly, poor communication can result in confusion, delayed decision-making, inconsistent messaging, regulatory non-compliance, and increased business disruption. **Internal communication** ensures that the appropriate individuals and teams receive accurate, timely, and actionable information throughout the incident response lifecycle. It enables coordinated decision-making, supports operational continuity, and helps maintain organizational confidence during periods of uncertainty.

Cybersecurity incidents often involve multiple departments working simultaneously under significant time pressure. The Security Operations Center (SOC), Computer Security Incident Response Team (CSIRT), IT Operations, executive management, legal counsel, compliance officers, privacy teams, human resources, and business units must all understand their roles and responsibilities. Without structured internal communication, response efforts may become fragmented, leading to duplicated work, conflicting decisions, delayed containment, or overlooked risks.

Internal communication should follow predefined procedures established within the organization's Incident Response Plan (IRP). Communications should be based on verified facts rather than assumptions and should be shared according to the principle of **need-to-know**. Sensitive information regarding ongoing investigations, affected systems, customer data, or attacker activities should be protected to prevent unnecessary disclosure while ensuring that decision-makers have sufficient information to support the response.

International standards such as **ISO/IEC 27035**, **ISO/IEC 27001**, **ISO 22301 Business Continuity Management Systems (BCMS)**, **NIST SP 800-61 Revision 2**, and the **NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF)** recognize communication as a fundamental component of effective incident management. Within Governance, Risk, and Compliance (GRC), structured internal communication supports executive oversight, enterprise risk management, regulatory compliance, and organizational resilience.

# What is Internal Communication?

**Internal communication** is the structured exchange of incident-related information among employees, management, technical teams, and organizational stakeholders during a cybersecurity incident.

Its objectives are to:

- Share accurate information.
- Coordinate response activities.
- Support timely decision-making.
- Reduce confusion.
- Protect sensitive information.
- Maintain business continuity.

Effective communication enables the organization to respond as a coordinated team.

# Practical Example

A multinational healthcare organization detects suspicious activity affecting its electronic medical records system. The Security Operations Center (SOC) immediately notifies the Computer Security Incident Response Team (CSIRT), which classifies the event as a high-severity cybersecurity incident. Secure communication channels are activated, and notifications are sent to IT Operations, the Chief Information Security Officer (CISO), Legal, Compliance, the Privacy Office, and executive management. Regular situation reports are issued every hour, summarizing affected systems, containment progress, operational impact, and decisions requiring executive approval. Business owners receive guidance on maintaining essential healthcare services while technical teams coordinate containment and recovery activities. All communications are documented within the incident management platform, creating a complete audit trail for post-incident review and regulatory reporting.

This example illustrates how structured internal communication enables technical teams, business stakeholders, and executive leadership to work together effectively, reducing confusion and supporting a coordinated response throughout the cybersecurity incident.

- # Executive and Board Communication

Cybersecurity incidents often require decisions that extend beyond technical response activities. Major incidents can disrupt business operations, affect financial performance, expose sensitive information, trigger regulatory obligations, and damage an organization's reputation. Consequently, executive leadership and the Board of Directors require timely, accurate, and business-focused information to fulfill their governance responsibilities. **Executive and Board communication** ensures that senior leadership understands the organization's current situation, associated risks, response progress, and strategic decisions required throughout the incident lifecycle.

Unlike technical teams, executives and board members generally do not require detailed forensic or operational information. Instead, they need concise reports that explain the business impact, operational status, financial implications, legal considerations, regulatory obligations, customer impact, and strategic response options. Effective communication enables leadership to allocate resources, approve critical decisions, manage enterprise risk, communicate with stakeholders, and provide organizational direction during high-impact incidents.

Communication with executives should occur throughout the incident, not only during major milestones. Initial notifications should provide an overview of the situation, while subsequent updates should report changes in business impact, containment progress, recovery status, emerging risks, and decisions requiring executive approval. Information should be presented using clear, non-technical language supported by business metrics and risk assessments that facilitate informed decision-making.

International standards such as **ISO/IEC 27035**, **ISO/IEC 27001**, **ISO 22301 Business Continuity Management Systems (BCMS)**, **NIST SP 800-61 Revision 2**, and the **NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF)** emphasize executive oversight during cybersecurity incidents. Within Governance, Risk, and Compliance (GRC), executive and board communication strengthens governance, supports enterprise risk management, enables regulatory compliance, and reinforces organizational resilience.

# Practical Example

A multinational telecommunications company experiences a large-scale ransomware attack affecting several customer-facing services. After the Security Operations Center (SOC) confirms the incident, the Computer Security Incident Response Team (CSIRT) prepares an executive situation report summarizing the affected systems, estimated operational impact, customer service disruptions, regulatory obligations, and initial containment progress. The Chief Information Security Officer (CISO) presents the report during an emergency executive meeting attended by the Chief Executive Officer (CEO), Chief Financial Officer (CFO), Chief Operating Officer (COO), General Counsel, and business executives. Based on the briefing, executive leadership authorizes additional funding for external incident response specialists, activates elements of the Business Continuity Plan (BCP), and approves customer communication strategies. Later, the Board of Directors receives a strategic briefing focused on business impact, enterprise risk exposure, financial implications, recovery progress, and long-term cybersecurity improvements rather than technical forensic details.

This example demonstrates how executive and board communication enables senior leadership to exercise effective governance, make informed strategic decisions, and support organizational resilience throughout a cybersecurity incident.

- # Customer, Vendor, and Media Communication

Cybersecurity incidents often affect stakeholders outside the organization, including customers, business partners, vendors, regulators, shareholders, and the general public. Effective **external communication** is essential for maintaining trust, protecting the organization's reputation, meeting contractual and regulatory obligations, and ensuring that accurate information is shared with affected parties. Poor communication during a security incident can create confusion, damage customer confidence, increase legal liability, and amplify reputational harm beyond the technical impact of the incident itself.

Unlike internal communication, which focuses on coordinating response activities, external communication is intended to provide clear, accurate, and consistent information to individuals and organizations that may be affected by the incident. These communications should balance transparency with security by providing sufficient information to help stakeholders understand the situation without disclosing sensitive technical details that could hinder investigations or assist attackers.

Organizations should establish predefined communication procedures before incidents occur. Public statements, customer notifications, media responses, and vendor communications should be coordinated through approved communication channels and reviewed by Legal, Compliance, Privacy, Executive Management, and Public Relations teams before publication. A single, consistent organizational message helps prevent conflicting statements and protects organizational credibility throughout the incident response process.

International standards such as **ISO/IEC 27035**, **ISO/IEC 27001**, **ISO 22301 Business Continuity Management Systems (BCMS)**, **NIST SP 800-61 Revision 2**, and the **NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF)** emphasize the importance of coordinated external communication during cybersecurity incidents. Within Governance, Risk, and Compliance (GRC), effective stakeholder communication supports regulatory compliance, enterprise risk management, customer confidence, and organizational resilience.

# Practical Example

A multinational cloud service provider discovers that attackers accessed a limited number of customer accounts through compromised credentials. After confirming the scope of the incident, the Computer Security Incident Response Team (CSIRT), Legal Department, Privacy Office, Public Relations, and Executive Management coordinate an external communication strategy. Affected customers receive personalized notifications explaining the nature of the incident, the information involved, recommended password changes, and the activation of Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA). Strategic technology partners and cloud vendors are informed of the incident so they can review shared systems for related Indicators of Compromise (IOCs). At the same time, the organization's designated spokesperson issues a public statement confirming the incident, describing the response actions being taken, and reassuring customers that security remains a top priority. Media inquiries are handled through a centralized communications team to ensure that all public messaging remains accurate, consistent, and aligned with legal and regulatory requirements.

This example demonstrates how coordinated communication with customers, vendors, and the media helps maintain stakeholder confidence, supports business continuity, and protects organizational reputation during a cybersecurity incident.

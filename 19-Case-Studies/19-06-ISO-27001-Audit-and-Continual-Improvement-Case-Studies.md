# 19.6 ISO 27001 Audit and Continual Improvement Case Studies

## Part 1 – Preparing for an ISO 27001 Internal Audit

## 1. Case Study Overview

**IberiaCloud Digital Services** has operated its ISO/IEC 27001 Information Security Management System (ISMS) for approximately one year.

Management now requires an internal audit before the organization's next certification surveillance audit.

The objective is not simply to determine whether documents exist.

The internal audit must determine whether the ISMS:

* conforms to applicable ISO 27001 requirements;
* conforms to the organization's own policies and procedures;
* is effectively implemented;
* operates as intended;
* produces sufficient evidence;
* identifies opportunities for improvement.

The GRC team therefore develops a structured internal-audit program.

---

# 2. Organization Profile

### Organization

**IberiaCloud Digital Services**

### Industry

Cloud and telecommunications services

### Employees

Approximately 1,800

### ISMS Scope

Cloud and managed digital services supporting operations in Spain and Portugal.

### Certification

ISO 27001 certified approximately one year earlier.

### Current Environment

Since certification, the organization has:

* migrated several workloads to Azure;
* introduced an AI-enabled customer-service platform;
* changed two critical suppliers;
* expanded remote working;
* implemented a new IAM platform;
* experienced an increase in phishing attempts.

These changes make the internal audit particularly important.

---

# 3. Why the Internal Audit Matters

Management initially asks:

> "Can we pass the surveillance audit?"

The GRC Manager reframes the objective:

> **"The internal audit should determine whether the ISMS is actually effective before the certification body evaluates it."**

This changes the audit from a compliance exercise into an assurance mechanism.

---

# 4. Internal Audit Objectives

The audit objectives are to determine whether:

1. the ISMS conforms to ISO 27001 requirements;
2. the ISMS conforms to internal policies;
3. risks are appropriately managed;
4. controls are implemented;
5. controls operate effectively;
6. evidence is reliable;
7. previous findings have been addressed;
8. management requirements are being met;
9. changes have been incorporated into the ISMS;
10. opportunities for improvement exist.

---

# 5. Internal Audit Scope

The audit covers:

### Governance

* ISMS governance;
* leadership;
* responsibilities;
* policies.

### Risk

* risk methodology;
* risk register;
* treatment;
* risk acceptance.

### Controls

* access management;
* supplier security;
* incident management;
* vulnerability management;
* business continuity.

### Assurance

* monitoring;
* internal audit;
* management review;
* corrective actions.

---

# 6. Audit Criteria

The audit team establishes formal criteria.

These include:

* ISO/IEC 27001 requirements;
* organizational security policies;
* documented procedures;
* risk-management methodology;
* Statement of Applicability;
* contractual requirements;
* applicable regulatory requirements.

The auditor will compare **actual practice against defined criteria**.

---

# 7. Audit Independence

The GRC Manager wants the IAM Manager to audit privileged-access management because the IAM Manager knows the process best.

Internal Audit rejects the proposal.

The reason is independence.

The IAM Manager owns the process and therefore should not audit their own work.

Instead, an independent auditor is assigned.

---

# 8. Audit Team

The audit team consists of:

| Role               | Responsibility                    |
| ------------------ | --------------------------------- |
| Lead Auditor       | Overall audit coordination        |
| GRC Auditor        | ISMS and risk management          |
| Technical Auditor  | Security controls                 |
| Privacy Specialist | Data protection controls          |
| Business Auditor   | Business continuity               |
| Process Owners     | Provide evidence and explanations |

The audit team has sufficient knowledge without auditing its own activities.

---

# 9. Audit Program

The organization establishes a risk-based annual audit program.

High-risk areas receive more attention.

Example:

| Area                | Risk   | Audit Frequency |
| ------------------- | ------ | --------------- |
| Privileged Access   | High   | Annual          |
| Supplier Security   | High   | Annual          |
| Incident Management | High   | Annual          |
| Business Continuity | High   | Annual          |
| Security Awareness  | Medium | Annual          |
| Document Management | Low    | Periodic        |

The program is adjusted when significant changes occur.

---

# 10. Risk-Based Audit Planning

The audit team does not allocate equal time to every control.

For example:

A low-risk document-control process may require:

**2 hours**

while privileged-access management may require:

**2 days**

because the potential business impact is substantially higher.

This is a fundamental GRC principle:

> **Audit effort should reflect risk.**

---

# 11. Step 1 – Review Previous Audit Results

The first activity is reviewing previous findings.

Last year's audit identified:

* supplier reassessment delays;
* incomplete access-review evidence;
* outdated incident procedures.

The team checks whether corrective actions were:

* implemented;
* documented;
* effective.

---

# 12. Step 2 – Review Organizational Changes

The audit team reviews major changes since the previous audit.

Changes include:

* new cloud architecture;
* AI platform;
* IAM migration;
* new suppliers;
* organizational restructuring.

The team asks:

> **Were these changes incorporated into the ISMS?**

# 14. Step 3 – Review ISMS Documentation

The auditor reviews:

* ISMS scope;
* policies;
* procedures;
* risk methodology;
* risk register;
* risk treatment plan;
* Statement of Applicability;
* objectives;
* management review records.

The purpose is not simply to check whether documents exist.

The auditor evaluates whether the documentation reflects **actual operations**.

---

# 15. Example Documentation Gap

The access-management procedure states:

> "Privileged access shall be reviewed quarterly."

The auditor asks:

> "Show me the most recent quarterly review."

The process owner provides evidence.

However, the review was actually performed after five months.

The auditor investigates the discrepancy between:

**Defined Process**

and

**Actual Process**.

---

# 16. Step 4 – Conduct Interviews

Interviews are an important audit technique.

Employees are asked questions such as:

* What are your responsibilities?
* How do you report an incident?
* How is privileged access approved?
* What happens when an employee leaves?
* How are security risks identified?
* Where do you find the relevant procedure?

The auditor compares employee responses with documented processes.

---

# 17. Interview Example

The procedure states:

> Employees must report security incidents through the security portal.

The employee explains:

> "We normally send an email to our manager."

The auditor identifies a possible process-awareness gap.

The issue is investigated rather than immediately classified as a finding.

---

# 18. Step 5 – Sampling

The audit team cannot test every transaction.

Instead, it uses sampling.

For example:

### Access Management

Sample:

**30 privileged accounts**

### Supplier Security

Sample:

**15 critical suppliers**

### Incident Management

Sample:

**20 incidents**

### Training

Sample:

**50 employees**

Sampling should be risk-based and sufficiently representative.

---

# 19. Step 6 – Evidence Testing

The auditor evaluates evidence using several dimensions:

### Authenticity

Is the evidence genuine?

### Completeness

Does it contain enough information?

### Relevance

Does it actually demonstrate the control?

### Timeliness

Is it from the appropriate period?

### Traceability

Can it be connected to the requirement and control?

---

# 20. Weak vs Strong Evidence

### Weak Evidence

A spreadsheet stating:

> "Quarterly review completed."

### Strong Evidence

A system-generated record showing:

* account population;
* review date;
* reviewer;
* decisions;
* exceptions;
* remediation;
* approval.

The second provides substantially stronger assurance.

---

# 21. Step 7 – Control Testing

The auditor selects a control:

**Privileged Access Review**

The defined control requires:

1. quarterly review;
2. review by account owner;
3. removal of unnecessary access;
4. documented approval.

The auditor tests whether each requirement was satisfied.

---

# 22. Control Testing Example

Sample:

**30 privileged accounts**

Results:

* 30 identified;
* 28 reviewed on time;
* 2 reviewed late;
* 3 unnecessary permissions identified;
* all 3 subsequently removed.

The control is operating, but there is a timeliness weakness.

The auditor investigates the significance.

---

# 23. Step 8 – Walkthrough Testing

The auditor performs a walkthrough.

Example:

# 24. Step 9 – Control Design vs Operating Effectiveness

The audit distinguishes two questions.

### Design

> Is the control appropriately designed?

### Operation

> Does the control actually operate as designed?

A control can therefore be:

### Well Designed + Effective

Ideal.

### Well Designed + Ineffective

Implementation problem.

### Poorly Designed + Consistently Performed

Still problematic.

### Poorly Designed + Ineffective

High concern.

---

The organization has a quarterly access-review control.

The process is clearly defined.

However, reviewers receive no automated reminders.

The control is:

**Adequately designed but operationally inefficient.**

The auditor may recommend automation.

The auditor selects several controls from the SoA.

* applicability;

# 29. Step 12 – Supplier Security Testing

* assessments;
* remediation;
* monitoring.

Results:

* 13 fully compliant;

The auditor assesses whether the organization is appropriately managing the associated risks.

# 30. Step 13 – Incident Management Testing

The auditor selects 20 incidents.

The review checks:

* classification;
* response;
* escalation;
* investigation;
* evidence;
* closure;
* lessons learned.

One incident lacks a documented lessons-learned record.

The auditor evaluates whether this represents:

* an isolated documentation issue;
* a process weakness;
* or a systemic failure.

---

# 31. Step 14 – Business Continuity Testing

The auditor reviews recovery testing.

The organization claims:

> "Critical systems are tested annually."

Evidence shows that 8 of 10 critical systems participated in the most recent exercise.

Two systems were excluded.

The auditor asks:

> "Why?"

The organization provides documented justification and a scheduled test.

This may be acceptable if risk is appropriately managed.

---

# 32. Step 15 – Security Awareness Testing

The auditor samples employee training records.

The organization reports:

**99% completion**

However, interviews reveal several employees cannot explain the incident-reporting procedure.

The auditor therefore distinguishes:

**Training completion**

from:

**Actual awareness effectiveness.**

This is an important GRC lesson.

---

# 33. Step 16 – Audit Findings Classification

The audit team classifies observations.

### Conformity

Requirement is satisfied.

### Observation

Potential improvement opportunity.

### Minor Nonconformity

A limited failure to meet a requirement.

### Major Nonconformity

A significant failure affecting the ability of the ISMS to achieve intended outcomes.

Organizations may use different internal classification terminology, but the underlying principle remains:

**Severity should reflect risk and the nature of the conformity gap.**

# 41. Internal Audit vs Certification Audit

The GRC team documents the distinction.

| Internal Audit                    | Certification Audit                     |
| --------------------------------- | --------------------------------------- |
| Conducted by/for the organization | Conducted by certification body         |
| Improvement-oriented              | Independent certification assurance     |
| Can be more exploratory           | Formal external assessment              |
| Identifies weaknesses early       | Determines conformity for certification |
| Supports management               | Provides certification assurance        |

The internal audit should not attempt to impersonate the certification body.

Its purpose is to provide **independent internal assurance**.

---

# 42. Lessons Learned

## Lesson 1 – Audit the Process, Not Just Documents

Documentation may say one thing while operations do another.

## Lesson 2 – Follow the Evidence

Auditors should trace claims back to objective evidence.

## Lesson 3 – Interview People

Employee understanding can reveal weaknesses invisible in documentation.

## Lesson 4 – Test Samples

Sampling provides practical assurance without testing every transaction.

---

# 43. Additional Lessons

## Lesson 5 – Test Effectiveness

A control existing on paper does not prove that it works.

## Lesson 6 – Follow Changes

New technology, suppliers and business processes can create new risks.

## Lesson 7 – Preserve Independence

People should not audit their own work wherever independence would be compromised.

## Lesson 8 – Use Findings for Improvement

The purpose of internal audit is not simply to produce a list of problems.

# 46. Case Study Conclusion

The IberiaCloud case demonstrates that an effective ISO 27001 internal audit is much more than a documentation checklist.

A mature internal audit evaluates the complete chain:

**Requirement → Process → Risk → Control → Operation → Evidence → Effectiveness → Finding → Corrective Action → Improvement**

The strongest internal audits do not ask only:

> **"Do you have the required document?"**

They ask:

> **"Show me how this process operates, show me the evidence, and demonstrate how you know the control is effective."**

For a GRC professional, this distinction is fundamental.

Internal audit provides the organization with an opportunity to discover weaknesses **before an external auditor, regulator, customer, or security incident exposes them**.

The ultimate purpose is therefore not simply to prepare for certification surveillance.

It is to provide management with **credible, risk-based assurance that the ISMS continues to operate effectively and support the organization's security objectives.**

# 19.6 ISO 27001 Audit and Continual Improvement Case Studies

## Part 2 – Managing ISO 27001 Audit Findings

## 1. Case Study Overview

**IberiaCloud Digital Services** has completed its annual ISO 27001 internal audit.

The audit identified:

* 4 minor nonconformities;
* 7 observations;
* 3 improvement opportunities.

Management now faces a critical GRC question:

> **How should audit findings be managed from identification through verified closure?**

The objective is not simply to close findings as quickly as possible.

A mature organization must ensure that findings are:

* understood;
* risk-assessed;
* assigned;
* investigated;
* corrected;
* subjected to root-cause analysis;
* addressed through corrective action;
* supported by objective evidence;
* tested for effectiveness;
* formally closed.

---

# 2. Organization Profile

### Organization

**IberiaCloud Digital Services**

### Industry

Cloud and telecommunications services

### Employees

Approximately 1,800

### ISMS Scope

Cloud and managed digital services in Spain and Portugal.

### Certification

ISO 27001 certified.

### Current Situation

The organization has just completed its internal audit before the upcoming surveillance audit.

The CISO wants all significant findings addressed before the certification body's visit.

---

# 3. Audit Findings

The internal audit produced the following results:

| Finding                           | Classification | Risk   |
| --------------------------------- | -------------- | ------ |
| Privileged-access reviews overdue | Minor NC       | High   |
| Supplier reassessment incomplete  | Minor NC       | High   |
| Incident lessons learned missing  | Minor NC       | Medium |
| Security evidence inconsistent    | Minor NC       | Medium |
| Policy review delays              | Observation    | Low    |
| Awareness effectiveness           | Observation    | Medium |
| Backup evidence                   | Observation    | Medium |

The organization must now establish a controlled remediation process.

---

# 5. Step 1 – Validate the Finding

The GRC Manager reviews each finding before it enters the corrective-action system.

For each finding, the team confirms:

* requirement;
* evidence;
* affected process;
* affected control;
* risk;
* classification;
* responsible owner.

This prevents poorly defined findings from becoming difficult-to-manage remediation projects.

---

# 6. Example Finding

### Finding

Privileged-access reviews were not consistently completed quarterly.

### Evidence

Two of 30 sampled accounts had reviews completed after the required deadline.

### Requirement

Internal privileged-access review procedure.

### Risk

Unnecessary privileged access may remain active for longer than intended.

### Classification

Minor nonconformity.

### Owner

IAM Manager.

---

# 7. Step 2 – Record the Finding in the GRC Platform

The finding is entered into the organization's GRC platform.

Required fields include:

* finding ID;
* audit ID;
* source;
* requirement;
* finding description;
* classification;
* risk rating;
* owner;
* due date;
* corrective action;
* status;
* evidence;
* closure date.

Example:

**Finding ID:** ISO-AUD-2026-004

**Status:** Open

**Risk:** High

**Owner:** IAM Manager

**Due Date:** 30 September

---

# 8. Step 3 – Perform Risk Assessment

Not all findings deserve identical treatment.

The GRC team assesses:

### Impact

What could happen if the weakness remains?

### Likelihood

How likely is exploitation or recurrence?

### Control Criticality

How important is the affected control?

### Exposure

How many systems, users or processes are affected?

---

# 9. Finding Risk Matrix

| Impact | Likelihood | Risk     |
| ------ | ---------- | -------- |
| High   | High       | Critical |
| High   | Medium     | High     |
| Medium | Medium     | Medium   |
| Low    | Low        | Low      |

A documentation issue may therefore be low risk.

A weakness affecting privileged access to critical infrastructure may be high risk.

---

# 10. Step 4 – Immediate Correction

The IAM Manager immediately reviews all privileged accounts.

The review identifies:

* 212 privileged accounts;
* 7 unnecessary permissions;
* 1 inactive account.

The unnecessary permissions are removed.

The inactive account is disabled.

This is the **correction**.

However, the finding remains open.

---

# 11. Why the Finding Remains Open

The organization has fixed the immediate issue.

But the auditor asks:

> **"Why did the control fail in the first place?"**

If the organization simply closes the finding because the accounts were reviewed, the same problem could occur again.

Therefore:

**Correction ≠ Corrective Action**

---

# 12. Step 5 – Root Cause Analysis

The organization uses the **5 Whys** technique.

### Why 1

Why were reviews overdue?

Because reviewers did not complete them on time.

### Why 2

Why did reviewers miss the deadlines?

There was no automated escalation.

### Why 3

Why was there no escalation?

The workflow relied on manual tracking.

### Why 4

Why was manual tracking used?

The GRC platform was not integrated with IAM.

### Why 5

Why was integration not implemented?

The access-review process was designed before the current IAM architecture.

---

# 13. Root Cause

The root cause is determined to be:

> **The access-review process was not updated following the IAM architecture change and therefore remained dependent on manual tracking.**

This is significantly more useful than stating:

> "The reviewers forgot."

---

# 14. Step 6 – Determine Corrective Action

The organization creates a corrective-action plan.

### Action 1

Integrate IAM with the GRC platform.

### Action 2

Automate review assignments.

### Action 3

Introduce deadline notifications.

### Action 4

Implement escalation.

### Action 5

Create management dashboards.

### Action 6

Update the access-review procedure.

### Action 7

Train control owners.

---

# 15. Corrective Action Plan

| Action             | Owner               | Due Date | Status      |
| ------------------ | ------------------- | -------- | ----------- |
| IAM integration    | IAM Manager         | 30 Sep   | In Progress |
| Automated workflow | GRC Manager         | 15 Sep   | In Progress |
| Escalation         | Security Operations | 20 Sep   | Open        |
| Procedure update   | GRC                 | 10 Sep   | Complete    |
| Training           | Security Awareness  | 25 Sep   | Open        |
| Dashboard          | GRC Analytics       | 30 Sep   | Open        |

Every action has a clear owner and deadline.

---

# 16. Step 7 – Define Acceptance Criteria

The organization establishes measurable closure criteria.

The finding will not be considered effectively addressed until:

* 100% of privileged accounts are included in the review population;
* reviews are assigned automatically;
* overdue reviews generate escalation;
* evidence is automatically retained;
* management can monitor completion;
* effectiveness testing demonstrates sustained operation.

This prevents vague corrective actions.

---

# 17. Weak Corrective Action

A weak action would be:

> "Remind administrators to complete reviews."

Problems:

* no measurable result;
* no systemic improvement;
* no automation;
* no escalation;
* no effectiveness criteria.

---

# 18. Strong Corrective Action

A stronger action is:

> "Implement automated privileged-access review workflows integrated with the IAM platform, including reviewer assignment, deadline notifications, escalation, evidence retention and management reporting."

This action directly addresses the identified root cause.

---

# 19. Step 8 – Monitor Progress

The GRC Manager monitors the corrective-action portfolio weekly.

Example:

Management can immediately see where remediation attention is required.

---

# 21. Step 10 – Manage Risk While Remediation Is Pending

Sometimes corrective action cannot be completed immediately.

For example, the IAM integration requires 90 days.

The organization therefore evaluates the interim risk.

Possible temporary measures include:

* additional manual reviews;
* increased monitoring;
* restricted privileged access;
* additional approval;
* compensating controls.

This prevents the organization from assuming that an open finding automatically means acceptable risk.

---

# 22. Compensating Control Example

Until automation is implemented:

**IAM administrators perform monthly manual reviews.**

The GRC team documents:

* control owner;
* frequency;
* evidence;
* review scope;
* approval.

This temporarily reduces the exposure.

---

# 23. Step 11 – Collect Evidence

Once corrective actions are implemented, evidence is collected.

Examples include:

* updated procedure;
* GRC workflow configuration;
* IAM integration records;
* screenshots;
* system logs;
* training records;
* review reports;
* management approvals;
* effectiveness test results.

Evidence should demonstrate **implementation**, not merely intention.

---

Yes.

Yes.

Yes.

However, the finding is still not automatically closed.

> **Does the corrective action actually work?**

# 25. Step 13 – Effectiveness Testing

Internal Audit performs a follow-up test.

Sample:

**50 privileged accounts**

# 45. Final Case Study Conclusion

The IberiaCloud case demonstrates that **audit finding management is a complete GRC lifecycle**, not an administrative exercise.

The mature sequence is:

**Identify → Validate → Risk Assess → Correct → Analyze Root Cause → Corrective Action → Implement → Collect Evidence → Test Effectiveness → Close → Learn → Improve**

The most important distinction is between:

**"We completed the action."**

and:

**"We demonstrated that the action eliminated or sufficiently addressed the underlying problem."**

A strong GRC organization therefore measures not only how many findings it closes, but also:

* how quickly it closes them;
* how often findings recur;
* how effective corrective actions are;
* how much risk remains;
* whether systemic weaknesses are being eliminated.

The ultimate objective of audit findings is not to achieve **zero open findings**.

It is to achieve a **stronger, more resilient and continuously improving ISMS**.

# 19.6 ISO 27001 Audit and Continual Improvement Case Studies

The organization is approaching its first **certification surveillance audit**.

Although the organization successfully achieved certification during the initial certification cycle, management understands that surveillance is not simply a repeat of the original certification audit.

The certification body will expect evidence that the ISMS has:

* remained operational;
* been maintained;
* continued to address information-security risks;
* incorporated significant changes;
* undergone internal audit;
* undergone management review;
* addressed nonconformities;
* demonstrated continual improvement.

# 51. Final Case Study Conclusion

The IberiaCloud case demonstrates that successful ISO 27001 surveillance preparation should be based on **continuous ISMS operation**, not an emergency preparation exercise.

The strongest organizations maintain a continuous chain:

**Business Change → Risk Assessment → Control Implementation → Evidence → Internal Audit → Management Review → Corrective Action → Continual Improvement → Surveillance**

The surveillance audit should therefore be viewed as an **assurance checkpoint within the ISMS lifecycle**, rather than as a separate annual event.

For a GRC professional, the key lesson is:

> **If the ISMS is genuinely operating, surveillance preparation should primarily involve demonstrating what the organization already does—not creating a temporary appearance of compliance.**

A mature GRC function makes the evidence available, ensures responsibilities are clear, tracks findings to closure, and continuously connects business changes to risk, controls and assurance.

# 19.17 Cybersecurity Transformation Case Studies

## Part 1 – Transforming a Fragmented Cybersecurity Governance Program

## 1. Case Study Overview

GlobalConnect is a large multinational organization operating telecommunications, cloud, digital, and customer-facing technology services across several countries.

Over the years, the organization has invested heavily in cybersecurity. It has:

* Security Operations Center (SOC)
* Vulnerability management
* Identity and access management
* Network security
* Cloud security
* Security architecture
* Incident response
* Penetration testing
* Security awareness
* Third-party security
* Compliance
* Internal audit

However, cybersecurity developed organically.

Different departments implemented their own processes, tools, policies, and reporting mechanisms.

The organization therefore has a paradox:

> **GlobalConnect has many cybersecurity capabilities, but does not have a consistently integrated cybersecurity governance model.**

The Board asks the CISO:

> "Are we actually managing cybersecurity risk as an enterprise, or are we simply operating a collection of security functions?"

The CISO launches a cybersecurity transformation program.

---

# 2. The Initial Problem

The cybersecurity organization appears mature when viewed function by function.

However, an enterprise assessment reveals significant fragmentation.

```text
                  CISO
                   │
      ┌────────────┼────────────┐

      │            │            │
```

The functions operate reasonably well individually, but their governance processes are not sufficiently integrated.

# 20. Standardized Risk Scoring

GlobalConnect uses a five-by-five model.

### Likelihood

1–5

### Impact

1–5

Risk score:

[
Risk = Likelihood \times Impact
]

Example:

Likelihood:

> 4

Impact:

> 5

Therefore:

[
4\times5=20
]

Risk classification:

> Critical

The organization defines detailed criteria around this calculation rather than relying on the numerical score alone.

# 59. Lessons from the Transformation

### 1. More cybersecurity capability does not automatically mean better cybersecurity governance.

An organization can have excellent technical teams while still having fragmented governance.

### 2. Transformation begins with understanding the current state.

The organization must identify fragmentation before designing the future state.

### 3. Cybersecurity must align with business services.

Protecting individual technologies is important, but understanding their business impact is more important for enterprise decision-making.

### 4. One risk methodology improves consistency.

Different cybersecurity teams should not use incompatible definitions of risk.

### 5. A common control framework reduces duplication.

One well-designed control can support multiple standards, regulations, and assurance requirements.

### 6. Technical findings need business context.

A vulnerability, alert, or configuration weakness becomes strategically meaningful when connected to business impact.

### 7. Business owners must participate in cyber risk decisions.

Cybersecurity should provide expertise and challenge, while appropriate business management owns business risk.

### 8. Integration is essential.

GRC, SIEM, IAM, CMDB, ITSM, cloud, vulnerability management, and business systems should exchange relevant information.

### 9. Metrics should support decisions.

The executive dashboard should answer:

> "What requires management attention?"

rather than simply reporting thousands of technical events.

### 10. Technology rationalization can be part of cybersecurity transformation.

Reducing unnecessary tool duplication can improve both cost efficiency and operational effectiveness.

### 11. Cybersecurity transformation is a business transformation.

It changes:

* Governance
* Accountability
* Processes
* Technology
* People
* Risk management
* Decision-making

### 12. Transformation should be continuous.

The target is not to "finish cybersecurity."

The target is to establish an operating model capable of continuously adapting to:

* New threats
* New technologies
* New regulations
* New business models
* New dependencies
* Emerging risks

# 19.17 Cybersecurity Transformation Case Studies

## Part 2 – Building a Risk-Based Cybersecurity Operating Model

## 1. Case Study Overview

GlobalConnect has completed the first phase of its cybersecurity transformation.

The organization has:

* Established cybersecurity governance
* Created a common cyber risk framework
* Rationalized security controls
* Integrated major GRC and security systems
* Defined cybersecurity responsibilities
* Established executive reporting
* Identified critical business services

However, a new problem emerges.

The organization has improved its cybersecurity capabilities, but the operating model remains heavily focused on **functions and technologies** rather than **business risk and outcomes**.

For example:

* SOC manages security events
* Vulnerability Management manages vulnerabilities
* IAM manages identities
* Cloud Security manages cloud controls
* GRC manages risks and compliance
* Security Architecture manages architecture reviews

Each team performs its responsibilities, but the organization needs a stronger model for answering:

> **Who makes cybersecurity decisions, who owns the risk, who performs the work, who provides oversight, and how are all these activities coordinated?**

GlobalConnect therefore designs a:

> **Risk-Based Cybersecurity Operating Model.**

---

# 2. The Operating Model Problem

The existing structure looks like:

```text
                         CISO
                          │
       ┌──────────┬───────┼────────┬──────────┐
       ↓          ↓       ↓        ↓          ↓
      SOC        IAM     GRC      Cloud     Architecture
       │          │       │        │          │
    Security   Identity  Risk     Cloud     Design
    Events     Controls  Reports  Security  Reviews
```

This structure is functionally organized.

The problem is that cybersecurity risks often cross these boundaries.

For example:

> A compromised privileged account may involve IAM, SOC, endpoint security, cloud security, vulnerability management, incident response, GRC, and business continuity.

No single technical function can manage the entire risk.

---

# 3. Objective of the New Operating Model

The new operating model is designed to ensure:

1. Clear accountability
2. Risk-based prioritization
3. Business alignment
4. Effective decision rights
5. Integrated cybersecurity capabilities
6. Consistent control ownership
7. Efficient resource allocation
8. Strong second-line oversight
9. Independent assurance
10. Continuous improvement

The operating model must connect:

> **People + Processes + Technology + Governance + Data + Risk**

# 63. Key Lessons Learned

### 1. A cybersecurity operating model defines how cybersecurity actually works.

It connects governance, people, processes, technology, risk, and accountability.

### 2. Organizational structure alone is not an operating model.

The operating model must also define:

* Decision rights
* Processes
* Data
* Technology
* Performance
* Accountability

### 3. Risk ownership must be explicit.

Cybersecurity can identify and advise on risk, but appropriate business and technology management must own the risk.

### 4. Centralization and federation should be balanced.

Enterprise standards should be consistent while business units retain appropriate operational accountability.

### 5. Cybersecurity should be capability-based.

The organization should manage cybersecurity capabilities rather than merely managing departments and tools.

### 6. Decision rights are critical.

Many cybersecurity failures are caused not by a lack of technical capability but by uncertainty over who can make decisions.

### 7. Security should be embedded into business processes.

Cybersecurity should be integrated into:

* Projects
* Procurement
* Cloud adoption
* Technology changes
* Supplier management
* Business continuity

### 8. Metrics must lead to decisions.

A metric has value when it helps management understand:

> What is happening, why it matters, and what should be done.

### 9. Risk-based resource allocation improves cybersecurity economics.

Limited cybersecurity resources should be directed toward the risks that matter most.

### 10. Governance should not become bureaucracy.

A mature operating model provides structure while allowing the business to move at an appropriate speed.

### 11. Integration creates leverage.

When GRC, security operations, IT, business, and enterprise systems are connected, cybersecurity decisions become faster and more contextual.

### 12. The operating model must evolve.

A cybersecurity operating model designed for today's threats may not be appropriate for tomorrow's environment.

The model should continuously adapt to:

* Cloud
* AI
* New regulations
* New business models
* Supply-chain dependencies
* Emerging threats
* Changing organizational strategy

# 19.17 Cybersecurity Transformation Case Studies

## Part 3 – Integrating Security, Risk, Compliance, and Audit

## 1. Case Study Overview

GlobalConnect has established a risk-based cybersecurity operating model. However, a new challenge emerges.

Although cybersecurity, enterprise risk, compliance, and internal audit now have clearer responsibilities, they still operate through partially separate processes.

The organization discovers that the same issue may be evaluated four different ways.

For example, a critical cloud configuration weakness may appear as:

* A security finding
* A cybersecurity risk
* A compliance gap
* An audit finding

Each function may create its own record, assign a different rating, request separate evidence, and track remediation independently.

The Chief Risk Officer summarizes the problem:

> **"We have improved governance, but we have not yet created an integrated assurance and risk ecosystem."**

GlobalConnect therefore launches an initiative to integrate:

> **Security + Risk + Compliance + Audit**

into a coordinated governance model.

---

# 2. The Integration Problem

The existing environment looks like this:

```text
Security
   ↓
Security Findings

Risk
   ↓
Risk Register

Compliance
   ↓
Compliance Gaps

Internal Audit
   ↓
Audit Findings
```

The same underlying weakness can appear in all four systems.

This creates:

* Duplicate records
* Duplicate evidence requests
* Conflicting priorities
* Multiple remediation plans
* Inconsistent risk ratings
* Excessive administrative effort
* Confusing executive reporting

---

# 3. Example of the Problem

A business-critical application has inadequate privileged-access controls.

### Security identifies:

> 27 privileged accounts without appropriate MFA.

### Risk identifies:

> Excessive privileged-access exposure.

### Compliance identifies:

> Nonconformance with internal security requirements.

### Internal Audit identifies:

> Ineffective access governance control.

The organization now has four records describing essentially the same underlying problem.

---

# 4. Business Impact

Fragmentation creates several consequences.

### Increased workload

Teams repeatedly investigate the same issue.

### Inconsistent risk information

The same weakness may receive different ratings.

### Duplicate remediation

Different teams may request separate corrective actions.

### Poor executive visibility

Management cannot easily determine the organization's true exposure.

### Audit fatigue

Business teams repeatedly provide the same evidence.

### Weak root-cause analysis

Organizations often treat symptoms separately instead of addressing the underlying cause.

---

# 5. Transformation Objective

GlobalConnect establishes the following objective:

# 6. Integration Principles

GlobalConnect establishes ten principles.

### Principle 1 – One source of truth

Where practical, common risk and control information should not be duplicated across systems.

### Principle 2 – Common taxonomy

Security, risk, compliance, and audit use consistent definitions.

### Principle 3 – Risk-based prioritization

Issues are prioritized according to business risk.

### Principle 4 – Control-centric management

Multiple requirements should map to common controls.

### Principle 5 – Evidence reuse

Valid evidence should be reused where appropriate.

### Principle 6 – Clear ownership

Every risk, control, finding, and remediation action has an accountable owner.

### Principle 7 – Independent assurance

Integration must not compromise Internal Audit independence.

### Principle 8 – Traceability

Organizations should be able to trace:

> Requirement → Control → Evidence → Finding → Remediation.

### Principle 9 – Automation

Repeatable processes should be automated.

### Principle 10 – Executive simplicity

Executives should receive consolidated information rather than four disconnected reports.

# 64. Lessons Learned

### 1. Integration begins with common definitions.

Organizations cannot integrate effectively if "risk," "control," "finding," and "compliance gap" mean different things to different teams.

### 2. One control can support many requirements.

Control rationalization reduces unnecessary duplication.

### 3. Findings should connect to risks.

A finding becomes strategically useful when management understands what risk it represents.

### 4. Evidence should be reusable where appropriate.

Centralized evidence can reduce audit and compliance fatigue.

### 5. Integration must preserve independence.

Internal Audit should coordinate with management without becoming part of management's operational control activities.

### 6. Assurance mapping reveals both duplication and gaps.

The objective is not merely to reduce audits but to ensure important risks receive appropriate assurance.

### 7. Root causes matter more than isolated findings.

Repeated findings often indicate a systemic governance or control weakness.

### 8. Technology can enable integration.

A properly designed GRC platform can connect requirements, risks, controls, evidence, findings, and remediation.

### 9. Executive reporting should consolidate without oversimplifying.

Management needs one coherent view while retaining the ability to drill into specialist information.

### 10. Integration should be risk-based.

Not every security issue requires compliance escalation, and not every compliance issue requires an audit.

### 11. Integration reduces organizational friction.

When teams share reliable information, the business spends less time answering duplicate requests.

### 12. The ultimate objective is better decision-making.

The purpose of integrated GRC is not to create a larger database.

It is to help management answer:

> **What risks matter most, which controls address them, where are the weaknesses, what actions are required, and whether the organization is improving?**

# 54. Lessons Learned

### 1. A roadmap is more than a project list.

A mature roadmap connects:

> Risk → Capability → Initiative → Investment → Outcome.

### 2. Foundations must come first.

Without asset visibility, identity governance, risk ownership, and control foundations, advanced capabilities may produce limited value.

### 3. Transformation should be sequenced.

Dependencies determine which initiatives should happen first.

### 4. Cybersecurity transformation is a portfolio.

Individual projects must be managed collectively.

### 5. Risk reduction is the ultimate measure.

Project completion alone does not demonstrate improved cybersecurity.

### 6. Business strategy determines cybersecurity priorities.

The cybersecurity roadmap must evolve with the organization.

### 7. Investment must be defensible.

Executives need to understand why an investment is necessary and what risk reduction it produces.

### 8. Transformation requires organizational change.

People, processes, governance, and culture must change alongside technology.

### 9. The roadmap must remain dynamic.

Major events can change priorities.

### 10. Metrics must connect to outcomes.

Maturity scores and project milestones are useful, but risk reduction and business resilience are more important.

### 11. Automation should follow process maturity.

Automating a poorly designed process can simply make inefficiency faster.

### 12. Cybersecurity transformation is continuous.

There is no permanent "finished" state.

Threats, technologies, regulations, and business models continually change.

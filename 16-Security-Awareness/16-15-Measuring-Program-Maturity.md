## Security Awareness Maturity Models

A security awareness program can exist at very different levels of maturity.

Some organizations have only basic mandatory training:

> "Complete the annual cybersecurity course."

Others operate sophisticated programs that use:

* Risk-based training.
* Phishing simulations.
* Behavioral analytics.
* Role-based awareness.
* Metrics and KPIs.
* Continuous improvement.
* Human risk management.
* Executive reporting.

A **security awareness maturity model** provides a structured way to determine where an organization currently stands and what it should do to improve.

The fundamental concept is:

> **Maturity measures how effectively and consistently an organization manages security awareness—not simply how much training it delivers.**

---

# What Is a Maturity Model?

A maturity model is a structured framework used to evaluate how developed a capability is.

For security awareness, it can assess areas such as:

* Governance.
* Training.
* Communication.
* Phishing simulations.
* Measurement.
* Behavioral change.
* Technology.
* Role-based awareness.
* Third-party awareness.
* Continuous improvement.

A maturity model normally defines several levels.

For example:

**Level 1 → Initial**

↓

**Level 2 → Developing**

↓

**Level 3 → Defined**

↓

**Level 4 → Managed**

↓

**Level 5 → Optimized**

The exact terminology can vary between organizations and frameworks.

---

# Why Measure Maturity?

Without maturity assessment, an organization may know:

> "We conduct security awareness training."

But it may not know:

* Whether the program is effective.
* Whether employees are changing behavior.
* Whether high-risk groups receive appropriate training.
* Whether management supports the program.
* Whether metrics are meaningful.
* Whether the program is sustainable.
* Whether the organization is improving.

Maturity assessment provides a broader perspective.

---

# Training Completion Is Not Maturity

Consider two organizations.

### Organization A

* 100% training completion.
* No phishing simulations.
* No behavioral metrics.
* No role-based training.
* No executive reporting.

### Organization B

* 95% training completion.
* Risk-based simulations.
* Behavioral analytics.
* Role-based awareness.
* Executive dashboards.
* Continuous improvement.

Organization A has higher completion but may have a less mature program.

This demonstrates:

> **Completion is a metric. Maturity is a capability.**

---

# Typical Security Awareness Maturity Levels

A five-level model can be used as a practical example.

## Level 1 – Initial

Security awareness is mostly reactive and informal.

Characteristics may include:

* Limited formal training.
* No dedicated ownership.
* Minimal metrics.
* Training occurs mainly after incidents.
* Policies exist but are not reinforced consistently.

Example:

> Employees receive occasional security emails after a security incident.

The program depends heavily on individual effort.

---

# Level 2 – Developing

The organization begins establishing a formal program.

Characteristics may include:

* Annual mandatory training.
* Basic awareness policies.
* Defined training requirements.
* Basic tracking.
* Some phishing simulations.
* Initial management involvement.

The organization has moved beyond an informal approach.

However, the program may still be largely compliance-driven.

---

# Level 3 – Defined

The organization has a structured and documented awareness program.

Characteristics may include:

* Formal security awareness strategy.
* Defined roles and responsibilities.
* Regular training.
* Phishing simulations.
* Role-based training.
* Defined metrics.
* Documented governance.
* Third-party awareness requirements.

At this stage, security awareness becomes an established organizational capability.

---

# Level 4 – Managed

The organization actively measures and manages awareness effectiveness.

Characteristics may include:

* Behavioral analytics.
* Human-risk indicators.
* Risk-based training.
* Executive dashboards.
* Advanced phishing simulations.
* Targeted remediation.
* Continuous measurement.
* Integration with GRC and security operations.

The organization can answer:

> **"Is our awareness program actually reducing human-related risk?"**

---

# Level 5 – Optimized

The program continuously evolves based on data, threat intelligence, and organizational risk.

Characteristics may include:

* AI-assisted personalization.
* Predictive human-risk analytics.
* Continuous learning.
* Advanced behavioral analysis.
* Threat-informed awareness.
* Automated recommendations.
* Mature security culture.
* Continuous improvement.
* Strong integration with enterprise risk management.

At this stage:

> **Security awareness becomes an integral component of enterprise cyber-risk management.**

# Maturity Is Multi-Dimensional

An organization does not necessarily have one maturity level across everything.

For example:

| Capability            | Maturity |
| --------------------- | -------: |
| Governance            |  Level 4 |
| Training              |  Level 3 |
| Phishing Simulation   |  Level 4 |
| Behavioral Analytics  |  Level 2 |
| Third-Party Awareness |  Level 2 |
| Executive Reporting   |  Level 3 |
| AI Enablement         |  Level 1 |

The organization might therefore have an overall maturity of approximately Level 3, while some capabilities are more advanced and others require improvement.

This is much more useful than simply assigning one overall number.

# Maturity Assessment Criteria

Each maturity level should have clearly defined characteristics.

For example:

### Phishing Simulation

**Level 1**

No simulations.

**Level 2**

Occasional simulations.

**Level 3**

Regular organization-wide campaigns.

Risk-based and role-based simulations.

Adaptive simulations based on threat intelligence and behavioral analytics.

This allows assessors to determine the organization's current position objectively.

# Current State vs. Target State

A maturity model should not simply ask:

> "What level are we?"

It should also ask:

> **"What level should we be?"**

A small organization may not need a Level 5 AI-enabled awareness program.

For example:

**Current state:** Level 2

**Target state:** Level 3

That may be appropriate.

Another organization handling highly sensitive information may need:

**Current state:** Level 3

**Target state:** Level 4 or 5

The target should be determined by **risk and business requirements**, not technology trends.

---

# Maturity and Risk

Maturity should be connected to organizational risk.

Consider two companies.

### Company A

Small organization with limited sensitive data.

Target maturity:

**Level 3**

### Company B

Large financial institution with highly sensitive customer data.

Target maturity:

**Level 4–5**

It would not make sense to apply exactly the same maturity expectations to both.

---

# Risk-Based Maturity

A useful approach is:

**Business Risk**

*

**Threat Landscape**

*

**Regulatory Requirements**

*

**Organizational Complexity**

↓

**Required Awareness Maturity**

This prevents organizations from pursuing maturity for its own sake.

---

# Maturity and Compliance

Compliance requirements can influence maturity.

For example, organizations may need to demonstrate:

* Security awareness.
* Employee training.
* Role-specific competence.
* Security responsibilities.
* Evidence of ongoing improvement.

However:

> **Compliance should be a baseline, not the ultimate objective.**

An organization can be technically compliant while still having poor security behavior.

---

# Maturity and ISO/IEC 27001

ISO/IEC 27001 provides requirements for establishing, implementing, maintaining, and continually improving an information security management system.

Security awareness and competence are important components of an ISMS.

A maturity assessment can therefore help an organization determine how effectively its awareness capability supports the ISMS.

For example:

**Requirement**

↓

Security awareness and competence

↓

**Current capability**

Level 2

↓

**Gap**

Limited measurement

↓

**Improvement**

Introduce behavioral metrics and targeted training

↓

**Target**

Level 3 or 4

This demonstrates a structured improvement approach.

---

# Maturity and NIST

NIST-based programs can also use maturity-style thinking to evaluate how well awareness capabilities are integrated into cybersecurity risk management.

For example:

**Identify**

Human-related risks.

**Protect**

Provide awareness and training.

**Detect**

Measure suspicious behavior and reporting.

**Respond**

Train employees on incident reporting.

**Recover**

Use lessons learned to improve awareness.

The awareness program therefore supports the broader cybersecurity lifecycle.

---

# Maturity and Human Risk Management

Modern security programs increasingly view employees as part of the organization's risk landscape.

This shifts the focus from:

> **Security Awareness Training**

to:

> **Human Risk Management**

The maturity model should therefore consider whether the organization can:

* Identify human risks.
* Measure human behavior.
* Prioritize high-risk populations.
* Apply interventions.
* Measure improvement.

# Maturity Assessment Process

A practical assessment can follow these steps.

### Step 1 – Define the Scope

Determine what is being assessed.

For example:

> Enterprise security awareness program.

### Step 2 – Define the Maturity Model

Establish the maturity levels and criteria.

### Step 3 – Gather Evidence

Review:

* Policies.
* Procedures.
* Training.
* Metrics.
* Reports.
* Interviews.

### Step 4 – Score Each Domain

Assign maturity levels.

### Step 5 – Validate the Results

Discuss findings with stakeholders.

### Step 6 – Identify Gaps

Compare current and target states.

### Step 7 – Prioritize Improvements

Focus on the highest-risk gaps.

### Step 8 – Create a Roadmap

Define actions, owners, and timelines.

### Step 9 – Implement

Execute improvement initiatives.

### Step 10 – Reassess

Measure maturity again.

# Maturity Assessment Scoring

An organization can use a simple 1–5 scoring approach.

### 1 – Initial

Ad hoc.

### 2 – Developing

Partially established.

### 3 – Defined

Documented and standardized.

### 4 – Managed

Measured and actively managed.

### 5 – Optimized

Continuously improved and adaptive.

The score itself is less important than the evidence and improvement actions behind it.

---

# Avoiding False Precision

A common mistake is treating:

> **3.72**

as though it represents an exact measurement.

Maturity models involve judgment.

A score of 3.7 does not necessarily mean an organization is objectively 23% more mature than an organization scoring 3.0.

Therefore:

> **Use maturity scores as decision-support tools, not mathematical truths.**

# Prioritizing Maturity Gaps

Not every gap needs immediate remediation.

A GRC professional can prioritize based on:

**Risk Impact**

*

**Likelihood**

*

**Regulatory Importance**

*

**Effort**

For example:

### Gap A

No executive awareness training.

High risk.

→ High priority.

### Gap B

Awareness dashboard lacks advanced visualization.

Low risk.

→ Lower priority.

This ensures maturity improvement remains risk-driven.

# Maturity Roadmap

Suppose an organization is currently Level 2 and wants to reach Level 4.

A roadmap might look like:

### Year 1

* Establish governance.
* Formalize training.
* Introduce phishing simulations.
* Define KPIs.

### Year 2

* Implement behavioral analytics.
* Introduce role-based training.
* Develop executive reporting.
* Integrate awareness with GRC.

### Year 3

* Implement human-risk management.
* Introduce advanced analytics.
* Develop predictive capabilities.
* Establish continuous improvement.

The roadmap converts the maturity assessment into actionable improvement.

---

# Common Mistakes

Organizations should avoid:

### Pursuing Maturity Without Risk Context

Higher maturity is not automatically better.

### Focusing Only on Training

Awareness includes culture, behavior, measurement, and governance.

### Scoring Without Evidence

A maturity score should be supported by objective evidence.

### Treating Compliance as Maturity

Compliance represents requirements; maturity measures capability.

### Ignoring Human Behavior

Completion does not equal secure behavior.

### Trying to Reach Level 5 Immediately

Organizations should improve progressively.

### Ignoring Sustainability

A mature program must continue operating even when personnel change.

### Failing to Reassess

Maturity should be measured periodically.

# Practical Example

Consider a multinational company with 15,000 employees.

The organization performs a maturity assessment.

### Findings

**Governance:** Level 3

A formal program exists.

**Training:** Level 3

Annual training is established.

**Phishing:** Level 4

Regular risk-based simulations are conducted.

**Metrics:** Level 2

The organization mainly measures completion.

**Culture:** Level 3

Employees generally report suspicious activity.

**Third Party:** Level 1

Contractors receive limited awareness requirements.

The assessment identifies:

> **Third-party awareness as the largest maturity gap.**

The organization therefore prioritizes:

1. Vendor awareness requirements.
2. Contractor training.
3. Third-party security clauses.
4. Third-party completion tracking.
5. Third-party phishing awareness.

This is a much more effective approach than simply increasing employee training frequency.

# Key Takeaways

Security awareness maturity models help organizations determine how effectively their awareness capability is governed, implemented, measured, and improved.

The key principles are:

1. **Maturity is broader than training completion.**
2. **A maturity model provides a structured way to evaluate program capability.**
3. **Organizations can use multiple maturity levels, such as Initial through Optimized.**
4. **Different security awareness domains can have different maturity levels.**
5. **Maturity assessments should be evidence-based.**
6. **The target maturity level should be determined by organizational risk and business requirements.**
7. **Compliance requirements can influence maturity but should not be the sole objective.**
8. **Behavioral measurement becomes increasingly important at higher maturity levels.**
9. **Maturity gaps should be prioritized based on risk rather than convenience.**
10. **A maturity assessment should result in an actionable improvement roadmap.**
11. **Maturity scores should not create false precision.**
12. **Security awareness maturity should be reassessed periodically.**
13. **A mature program integrates governance, training, technology, metrics, culture, and continuous improvement.**
14. **Third-party and human-risk considerations should be included in enterprise maturity assessments.**
15. **The ultimate goal is not achieving the highest maturity level—it is achieving the level necessary to manage the organization's human-related security risks effectively.**

The strongest maturity programs evolve from:

> **Compliance-driven training**

to:

> **Structured awareness**

to:

> **Measured behavioral change**

to:

> **Risk-based human security management**

Ultimately, a maturity model gives the GRC professional a structured answer to an important question:

> **"How capable are we today, where do we need to be, and what must we do to close the gap?"**

## Program Assessment

A security awareness maturity model tells an organization **what maturity looks like**. A **program assessment** determines where the organization's actual security awareness program currently stands.

The assessment provides a structured examination of:

* Governance.
* Policies.
* Training.
* Phishing simulations.
* Metrics.
* Behavioral change.
* Technology.
* Security culture.
* Third-party awareness.
* Continuous improvement.

The fundamental principle is:

> **A program assessment should determine whether the security awareness program is actually designed, implemented, measured, and improved effectively.**

---

# What Is a Security Awareness Program Assessment?

A security awareness program assessment is a structured evaluation of an organization's existing awareness program against defined requirements, objectives, risks, and maturity expectations.

It answers questions such as:

* Does the organization have a formal awareness program?
* Who owns it?
* Are employees receiving appropriate training?
* Is training relevant to their roles?
* Are high-risk users identified?
* Are phishing simulations being conducted?
* Is behavioral change being measured?
* Are awareness metrics reported to management?
* Are third parties included?
* Is the program continuously improved?

The assessment should evaluate both:

**What the organization says it does**

and

**What the organization can demonstrate that it actually does.**

---

# Why Conduct a Program Assessment?

Security awareness programs can gradually become outdated.

For example:

An organization may have introduced annual training five years ago.

Since then:

* Cloud services were adopted.
* Remote work became common.
* AI tools were introduced.
* Phishing techniques changed.
* Regulatory requirements changed.
* The workforce expanded.
* Third-party relationships increased.

Yet the awareness program may still use the same training material.

A program assessment identifies these gaps.

---

# Assessment vs. Maturity Assessment

These concepts are related but not identical.

### Maturity Assessment

Asks:

> **"How mature is the program?"**

### Program Assessment

Asks:

> **"How well is the program currently designed and operating?"**

A maturity assessment may assign:

> Level 3 – Defined.

A detailed program assessment explains **why** it is Level 3 and what needs to change to reach Level 4.

# Interviews

Interviews are particularly important because documents may not reflect actual practice.

For example, the policy might state:

> "All employees receive annual security awareness training."

The assessment team can ask:

> "How are new employees trained?"

> "What happens when an employee does not complete training?"

> "Who monitors completion?"

> "How are contractors handled?"

These questions reveal whether the documented process operates effectively.

---

# Employee Surveys

Employee surveys can provide additional information.

Questions might include:

* Do you know how to report phishing?
* Do you understand the organization's data classification rules?
* Do you know where to find security policies?
* Do you feel comfortable reporting mistakes?
* Do managers reinforce security requirements?

Surveys can reveal weaknesses that formal documentation does not capture.

Where appropriate, the assessment can include technical validation.

For example:

* Verify LMS reporting.
* Verify training assignment rules.

This can demonstrate whether the program works in practice.

# Finding Gaps

An assessment should identify gaps between:

**Expected State**

and

**Actual State**

For example:

### Requirement

High-risk roles should receive specialized training.

### Current State

All employees receive identical annual training.

### Gap

No role-based awareness.

### Risk

Employees with specialized responsibilities may not understand threats specific to their roles.

### Recommendation

Implement role-based security awareness.

---

# Gap Classification

Findings can be categorized by severity.

### Critical

Significant weakness that could create major risk.

### High

Important weakness requiring timely remediation.

### Medium

Meaningful improvement opportunity.

### Low

Minor weakness or optimization opportunity.

This helps prioritize remediation.

---

# Root Cause Analysis

Finding a gap is not enough.

The assessment should determine **why** the gap exists.

Example:

### Finding

Only 65% of contractors complete security awareness training.

Possible causes:

* No contractual requirement.
* No centralized contractor database.
* No LMS integration.
* No ownership.
* Managers are not notified.

The actual root cause may therefore be governance or process—not employee unwillingness.

---

# Risk-Based Assessment

Not every weakness has equal importance.

For example:

### Finding A

Annual training reminders are not automated.

Potential impact: Low.

### Finding B

Privileged administrators receive no security awareness training.

Potential impact: High.

The second finding should receive greater attention.

This is why:

> **Assessment findings should be prioritized according to risk.**

---

# Control Effectiveness

A program assessment can evaluate whether controls are effective.

For example:

**Control**

Annual phishing awareness training.

**Control Objective**

Employees should recognize and report phishing.

**Evidence**

Training completion = 98%.

But:

**Phishing simulation failure = 14%.**

This suggests that the control may be implemented but not sufficiently effective.

This is an important GRC distinction:

> **Control implementation does not necessarily equal control effectiveness.**

---

# Design Effectiveness vs. Operating Effectiveness

A mature assessment can examine two dimensions.

## Design Effectiveness

Is the control appropriately designed?

Example:

> Training addresses phishing threats.

## Operating Effectiveness

Does the control actually work in practice?

Example:

> Phishing susceptibility decreases after training.

A program may be well designed but poorly implemented.

---

# Example

### Control

Quarterly phishing simulations.

### Design

The organization has defined:

* Frequency.
* Scope.
* Target groups.
* Metrics.
* Remediation.

Design effectiveness:

**Effective**

### Operation

However, campaigns have not been conducted for the past nine months.

Operating effectiveness:

**Ineffective**

This illustrates why both dimensions should be assessed.

# Employee Experience

A technically strong program can still fail if employees find it:

* Too long.
* Repetitive.
* Irrelevant.
* Difficult to access.
* Poorly translated.
* Disconnected from their work.

Therefore, assessment should consider employee experience.

For example:

> Does training help employees make better security decisions in their actual work?

# Measuring Program Outcomes

The assessment should distinguish between:

### Activity

How much training was delivered?

### Output

How many employees completed it?

### Behavior

Did employees change their behavior?

### Outcome

Did human-related security risk decrease?

This progression is important.

---

# Example

### Activity

10 training modules delivered.

### Output

98% completion.

### Behavior

Phishing reporting increased from 50% to 75%.

### Outcome

Phishing-related incidents decreased by 30%.

The final two measurements provide stronger evidence of program effectiveness.

# Example Finding

### Finding: Limited Role-Based Security Awareness

**Risk Rating:** High

**Observation:**

The organization provides the same annual awareness training to all employees regardless of role.

Training records and interviews with business units.

# Validation

After remediation, the organization should validate whether the issue has actually been resolved.

For example:

**Finding**

Phishing reporting is weak.

↓

**Action**

Conduct targeted training.

↓

**Retest**

Run another phishing simulation.

↓

Reporting rate increases from 52% to 81%.

The organization now has evidence that the remediation worked.

# Common Assessment Mistakes

Organizations should avoid:

### Assessing Only Training Completion

Completion does not prove effectiveness.

### Relying Only on Documentation

Documents may not reflect actual practice.

### Ignoring Employees

Users experience the program directly.

### Ignoring Third Parties

Contractors and suppliers may also create human risk.

### Treating All Findings Equally

Risk should determine priority.

### Producing a Report Without Owners

Every significant improvement should have accountability.

### No Follow-Up

Findings should be validated after remediation.

### Excessive Scoring

A precise score without evidence can create false confidence.

# GRC Perspective

For a GRC professional, a security awareness program assessment is essentially a **control and capability assessment**.

The GRC professional can establish:

**Requirement**

↓

**Control Objective**

↓

**Current State**

↓

**Evidence**

↓

**Gap**

↓

**Risk**

↓

**Recommendation**

↓

**Management Action**

↓

**Validation**

This structure makes the security awareness program auditable and measurable.

For example:

> **Requirement:** Personnel must receive appropriate security awareness and competence training.

↓

> **Current State:** Annual training exists.

↓

> **Evidence:** 98% completion.

↓

> **Gap:** No role-based training for privileged administrators.

↓

> **Risk:** Administrators may be insufficiently prepared for threats targeting privileged access.

↓

> **Recommendation:** Implement privileged-user-specific security awareness.

↓

> **Validation:** Measure training completion and behavioral performance.

This is the type of structured thinking expected from a mature GRC function.

# Key Takeaways

A security awareness program assessment determines how effectively an organization's awareness program is designed, implemented, measured, and improved.

The key principles are:

1. **A program assessment evaluates the actual operation of the awareness program.**
2. **It should be based on defined criteria and objective evidence.**
3. **Assessment scope and objectives should be clearly established before starting.**
4. **Documentation, interviews, surveys, metrics, and technical evidence can all support the assessment.**
5. **Training completion should not be treated as proof of effectiveness.**
6. **Both control design and operating effectiveness should be evaluated.**
7. **Findings should be prioritized according to security and business risk.**
8. **Root-cause analysis helps prevent organizations from treating every weakness as a training problem.**
9. **Third-party awareness should be included where contractors or suppliers create organizational risk.**
10. **Employee experience is an important part of program effectiveness.**
11. **Assessment results should lead to specific recommendations and accountable owners.**
12. **Management action plans should include priorities and target dates.**
13. **Remediation should be validated to determine whether the identified risk was actually reduced.**
14. **Program assessments should be repeated periodically and after significant organizational changes.**
15. **The strongest assessments connect awareness requirements to controls, risks, evidence, remediation, and continuous improvement.**

The ultimate objective is not:

> **"We completed an assessment."**

It is:

> **"We understand the current effectiveness of our security awareness program, have identified the most significant human-related risks, and have a measurable plan to improve them."**

From a GRC perspective, the program assessment provides the bridge between **security awareness activities and demonstrable risk reduction**.
